    \section{Static Coulomb Blockade}
    \label{sec:ssn-set:static}
    %=========================================================

        In this section, I use the term static Coulomb blockade for charging effects described by discrete island charge states and orthodox sequential tunneling, in contrast to environmental dynamical Coulomb blockade.

        \singlefitfigure{ssn-set/ssn1-sin}{
            Static transport of SET1 before opening the break junction. The panels show the measured \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} responses together with the SIN BCS Dynes fit. The fit describes the quasiparticle onset and the conductance above the gap.
        }{fig:ssn1-set:sin}

        I first characterized SET1 with the mechanically controlled break junction in its unbroken state. The closed metallic constriction had a much lower resistance than the oxide tunnel barrier, so the measured voltage dropped predominantly across the Al--AlO\textsubscript{x}--Cu junction. The device therefore provided an SIN tunnel junction reference before the superconducting island was formed.

        In the superconducting state, the current was suppressed below the quasiparticle threshold and increased sharply near $eV=\Delta_0$, as expected for an SIN junction. I fitted the complete \textit{I--V} characteristic with the BCS density of states including Dynes broadening. The fit yielded the normal-state resistance and conductance
        \begin{align}
            R_\mathrm{TB} = \qty{32.381(3)}{\kilo\ohm}\,, &&
            G_\mathrm{TB} = 0.39857(4)\,G_0\,.
            \label{eq:ssn-set:static:sin-normal}
        \end{align}

        The same fit gave the superconducting gap, fitted electronic temperature, and Dynes broadening
        \begin{align}
            \Delta_0 = \qty{196.106(9)}{\micro\electronvolt}\,, &&
            T_\mathrm{fit} = \qty{68.1(5)}{\milli\kelvin}\,, &&
            \gamma = \qty{0.86(3)}{\micro\electronvolt}\,.
            \label{eq:ssn-set:static:sin-superconducting}
        \end{align}

        The fitted temperature was higher than the measured bath temperature, $T_\mathrm{bath}=\qty{52(1)}{\milli\kelvin}$. Such a difference is expected because the bath thermometer does not directly measure the electronic temperature of the junction, and residual electrical noise or imperfect thermalization can broaden the transport response. I therefore treat $T_\mathrm{fit}$ as an effective electronic temperature.

        The fit reproduced both the quasiparticle onset and the conductance peak in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:sin}. The extracted gap provides the superconducting energy scale used below, while $R_\mathrm{TB}$ characterizes the fixed oxide barrier before the device was converted into an SET.

        In addition, I measured the device after suppressing superconductivity. The normal-state \textit{I--V} characteristic showed no resolvable zero bias anomaly. I therefore found no evidence for appreciable dynamical Coulomb blockade in the unbroken device.

        %=========================================================
        \subsection*{Normal-State Single-Electron Transistor}
        %=========================================================

            Opening the break junction separated the constriction and the fixed oxide tunnel barrier by a small island. I suppressed superconductivity to first characterize this three-terminal device in the normal state. A representative \textit{I--V} curve is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:ssn1-nset-iv0}. It shows a reduced low bias conductance compared with the approximately linear response at larger bias.

            \singlefitfigure{ssn-set/ssn1-nset-iv0}{
                Normal-state transport of SET1 after opening the break junction at $V_\mathrm{gate}=\qty{0}{\milli\volt}$. The measured \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} responses are compared with the orthodox SET fit. The suppressed low bias conductance is the local signature of Coulomb blockade.
            }{fig:ssn1-set:ssn1-nset-iv0}

            Sweeping the gate voltage revealed that this suppression was periodic and bounded by diagonal conductance onsets, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:ssn1-nset:exp}. The two families of Coulomb diamond edges had different absolute slopes in the gate--bias plane, as expected for unequal junction capacitances. Above $V_\mathrm{gate}\approx\qty{1.7}{\milli\volt}$, the measured pattern changed abruptly, as seen more clearly in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-nset:dGdiff}. This discontinuity was consistent with an atomic rearrangement of the mechanically formed break junction during the sweep, although the conductance did not provide an independent signature of such a change. The normal-state data therefore established gate controlled Coulomb blockade and an asymmetric SET geometry before superconducting transport was considered, but the complete map did not represent a single unchanged configuration.

                \caption{
                    Measured normal-state gate--bias transport of SET1 after opening the break junction. The periodic low conductance regions form Coulomb diamonds whose two edge families have different absolute slopes. The abrupt change during the gate sweep is consistent with an atomic rearrangement of the break junction.

            I fitted the measured \textit{I--V} gate map below the discontinuity with the orthodox sequential tunneling model introduced in the theory chapter. All bias and gate points at $V_\mathrm{gate}<\qty{1.7}{\milli\volt}$ were fitted simultaneously with a common set of circuit parameters rather than by fitting individual gate traces separately. The island energy and normal-state tunneling rates follow Eqs.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:set-electrostatic-energy} and \eqref{eq:stochastic:set-normal-rate}. At each bias and gate voltage, I solved the stationary master equation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:set-master-equation} and evaluated the current from Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:set-current}. I optimized the circuit parameters with the nonlinear least squares implementation in SciPy using a soft L1 loss to reduce the influence of isolated outliers.

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \thesisplot{ssn-set/ssn1-nset}{dGdiff}
                \caption{
                    Comparison of the measured and fitted normal-state differential conductance. The left and center panels show the experimental map and the orthodox SET result, while the right panel shows the differential conductance of the current residual $\delta I=I_\mathrm{exp}-I_\mathrm{fit}$. Only the stable region was included in the fit. Data above the fit boundary are shown as a visual reference for the discontinuity and are not interpreted as fit residuals.
                }
                \label{fig:ssn1-nset:dGdiff}
            \end{figure}

            The intact SIN measurement already determined the resistance of the oxide tunnel barrier. I therefore held this resistance fixed and fitted the complete normal-state transport map simultaneously to extract the remaining junction resistances and capacitances, the gate coupling, the offset charge, and the effective electronic temperature. 
            
            The individual junction resistances and conductances, together with the resulting totals, are
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{alignedat}{3}
                    R_\mathrm{TB} &= \qty{32.38}{\kilo\ohm}\,, &\qquad
                    R_\mathrm{BJ} &= \qty{24.15(4)}{\kilo\ohm}\,, &\qquad
                    R_\mathrm{N}  &= \qty{56.53(4)}{\kilo\ohm}\,, \\
                    G_\mathrm{TB} &= 0.3985\,G_0\,, &\qquad
                    G_\mathrm{BJ} &= 0.5344(9)\,G_0\,, &\qquad
                    G_\mathrm{N}  &= 0.2283(2)\,G_0\,. 
                \end{alignedat}
                \label{eq:ssn-set:static:nset-resistance}
            \end{equation}

            The resistance of the opened break junction was comparable to that of the oxide tunnel barrier. Both junctions therefore contributed appreciably to the measured voltage drop. From this point onward, $R_\mathrm{N}=R_\mathrm{TB}+R_\mathrm{BJ}$ and $G_\mathrm{N}=1/R_\mathrm{N}$ denote the total normal-state resistance and conductance of the complete SET, rather than those of the intact SIN reference.

            The fit further yielded the junction and gate capacitances
            \begin{align}
                C_\mathrm{TB} = \qty{957(3)}{\atto\farad}\,, &&
                C_\mathrm{BJ} = \qty{214(3)}{\atto\farad}\,, &&
                C_\mathrm{gate} = \qty{37.3(2)}{\atto\farad}\,,
                \label{eq:ssn-set:static:nset-capacitances}
            \end{align}
            and therefore the total island capacitance and charging energy
            \begin{align}
                C_\Sigma = \qty{1.208(4)}{\femto\farad}\,, &&
                E_\mathrm{C} = \qty{66.3(2)}{\micro\electronvolt}\,.
                \label{eq:ssn-set:static:nset-charging-energy}
            \end{align}

            The fit also yielded an effective temperature. Expressed as the thermal energy $E_\mathrm{T}=k_\mathrm{B}T_\mathrm{fit}$, it was
            \begin{align}
                T_\mathrm{fit} = \qty{132(2)}{\milli\kelvin}\,, &&
                E_\mathrm{T} = \qty{11.4(2)}{\micro\electronvolt}\,.
                \label{eq:ssn-set:static:nset-temperature}
            \end{align}

            The charging energy was therefore about six times larger than the thermal energy, $E_\mathrm{C}/E_\mathrm{T}\approx5.8$. This separation is consistent with the clearly resolved Coulomb blockade. As in the SIN reference measurement above, the fitted temperature was higher than the measured bath temperature, $T_\mathrm{bath}=\qty{97(1)}{\milli\kelvin}$. I again interpret this fit parameter as an effective electronic temperature rather than the direct cryostat reading.

            The substantially different junction capacitances quantify the asymmetry between the two families of Coulomb diamond edges. The oxide tunnel barrier supplied most of the total island capacitance, while the gate capacitance set the conversion from applied gate voltage to induced charge,
            \begin{align}
                n_\mathrm{gate} = C_\mathrm{gate}V_\mathrm{gate}/e-n_{\mathrm{gate},0}\,, && 
                n_{\mathrm{gate},0} = 0.213(3)\,.
                \label{eq:ssn-set:static:nset-gate-charge}
            \end{align}

            Overall, the calculated maps in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:ssn1-nset:fit} reproduced the dominant gate period, asymmetric blockade boundaries, and normal-state current scale of the stable break junction configuration.

            Within the fitted region, the residual in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-nset:dGdiff} is structured rather than uniformly distributed. Its diagonal features follow approximately the same directions as the measured conductance onsets. The data above the fit boundary are shown only as a visual reference and are not interpreted as fit residuals. I report the structures in the fitted region without assigning a microscopic origin here.

            I used the orthodox model as a minimal electrostatic description. Its validity in the tunnel limit was marginal because $R_\mathrm{BJ}=\qty{24.15(4)}{\kilo\ohm}$ was slightly below the resistance quantum $R_\mathrm{K}=h/e^2$. The normal-state resistance alone did not determine the individual channel transmissions of the break junction, and the present data did not provide its mesoscopic pincode. Higher-order and transmission dependent processes could therefore have contributed to the structured residuals. I consequently used the fit to quantify the dominant charging scale, gate coupling, and junction asymmetry, but I did not interpret the remaining structures microscopically.

        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}

                \subfigure[\textit{dI--dV}]{\thesisplot{ssn-set/ssn1-sisin}{didv}}
                \subfigure[Sweep difference]{\thesisplot{ssn-set/ssn1-sisin}{dvdi}}
                \caption{
                    Superconducting transport of the broken device for increasing and decreasing voltage sweeps. Panels (a) and (b) show the \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} responses, while panel (c) shows the current difference $I_\uparrow-I_\downarrow$ between the two sweep directions. The low voltage response is sweep direction dependent and contains abrupt transitions, but no separate retrapping transition can be distinguished from the broader sweep dependent response.
                }
                \label{fig:ssn1-set:ssn-iv}
            \end{figure}
            
            The normal-state measurement established the electrostatic calibration of the island. I used this reference to examine how superconductivity changed the low voltage transport and how the resulting features depended on the gate induced charge.

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \thesisplot{ssn-set/ssn1-sset-Ic}{dGdiff}
                \caption{
                    Gate dependent sweep difference and switching voltages in the superconducting state. The left panel shows the positive-bias discontinuity $V_\mathrm{sw}^{+}$ extracted from the up sweep and the negative-bias discontinuity $V_\mathrm{sw}^{-}$ extracted from the down sweep. The center panel shows the current difference $\delta I=I_\uparrow-I_\downarrow$, while the right panel shows the corresponding differential conductance difference. The axes use the normalized coordinates $n_\mathrm{gate}$ and $eV_\mathrm{bias}/\Delta_0$. The extracted switching voltages follow the abrupt boundaries of the sweep dependent region.
                }
                \label{fig:ssn1-set:dGdiff}
            \end{figure}

            After the normal-state characterization, I removed the magnetic field and measured the same device in the superconducting state. Figure~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:ssn-iv} compares increasing and decreasing voltage sweeps. The two traces differ around the low voltage branch and show abrupt transitions whose positions depend on sweep direction. I refer to this difference as hysteresis without assigning it to a particular dynamical model.

            I next repeated the measurement as a function of gate voltage. Figures~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:ssn-iv-gate} and \ref{fig:ssn1-set:ssn-didv-gate} show that both the low voltage response and the abrupt transitions varied periodically with the gate. I used the electrostatic calibration from the normal SET fit for the gate coordinate. I scaled the superconducting bias by the gap, the current by the corresponding normal-state current scale, and the differential conductance by the total normal-state conductance. This followed the normalization used for the normal SET, with the charging energy replaced by the superconducting gap as the relevant bias scale.

            The high-bias superconducting conductance did not coincide exactly with $G_\mathrm{N}$. This mismatch could result from a contact rearrangement between the measurements or from superconducting transport contributions not captured by the normal-state conductance scale. I therefore retained $G_\mathrm{N}$ only as a common normalization and did not interpret the mismatch quantitatively.

            To quantify these boundaries, I operationally defined $V_\mathrm{sw}^{+}$ as the positive-bias discontinuity in the up sweep and $V_\mathrm{sw}^{-}$ as the negative-bias discontinuity in the down sweep. I did not assign the other sweep direction dependent features to retrapping because they did not provide additional boundaries that could be identified with comparable confidence. Figure~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:dGdiff} shows the extracted switching voltages together with the measured differential conductance landscape.

            I evaluated the current at $V_\mathrm{sw}^{+}$ in the up sweep and at $V_\mathrm{sw}^{-}$ in the down sweep to obtain $I_\mathrm{sw}^{+}$ and $I_\mathrm{sw}^{-}$, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ssn1-set:Ic}. Their magnitudes were approximately symmetric between the two bias polarities and varied periodically with gate induced charge. Lukas Kammermeier and I identified this gate dependence during the analysis. The data therefore established gate dependent switching in the superconducting SSN device. I associated the discontinuities with switching of the superconducting break junction, but the present measurement did not determine the microscopic origin of their gate dependence or of the observed hysteresis.
