%=========================================================
\section{BCS Theory and Tunnel Spectroscopy}
\label{sec:micro}
%=========================================================

    We develop the minimal weak-coupling Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) description needed for superconducting transport. We first define the normal-state spectrum on which pairing acts. BCS mean field theory then introduces the pair potential and gives the Bogoliubov quasiparticles whose spectrum determines the superconducting density of states (DOS). We use this spectrum and its occupation to formulate the static tunnel current in the low-transmission limit. Finally, we extend this description to photon assisted tunneling (PAT) under harmonic driving. Phase dynamics and finite-transmission transport are treated in later sections. \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}

    We consider a spin-singlet \textit{s}-wave superconductor with one effective isotropic band. This provides the low-energy description used for the aluminum electrodes in this thesis but does not reproduce their detailed band structure. It excludes anisotropic, strongly correlated, and quantitative multiband superconductors.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{BCS Mean Field and Bogoliubov Quasiparticles}
    \label{subsec:micro:bcs-bdg}
    %=========================================================

        The normal-state problem supplies the reference DOS and the diagonal part of the BdG Hamiltonian. Pairing then couples its electron and hole sectors.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Normal-State Reference}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:normal-reference}
        %=========================================================

            At the energies relevant for superconducting transport, the electronic structure is represented by the effective single-particle Hamiltonian
            \begin{equation}
                \hat{H}_\mathrm{N}(\vec{r})
                = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^\ast}\nabla^2
                + U(\vec{r})-\mu\,.
                \label{eq:quasi:normal-hamiltonian}
            \end{equation}
            Here $m^\ast$ describes the band curvature, while $U(\vec r)$ includes the effective lattice background, confinement, barriers, and disorder. Energies are measured relative to the chemical potential $\mu$. This normal-state operator later forms the diagonal part of the Bogoliubov-de~Gennes Hamiltonian. \cite{gross_festkorperphysik_2014}

            In a homogeneous system, the effective band introduced above is approximated as parabolic,
            \begin{align}
                \varepsilon(\vec{k})
                \approx \frac{\hbar^2 \lvert\vec{k}\rvert^2}{2m^\ast}\,,
                &&
                E_\mathrm{F}
                = \frac{\hbar^2 k_\mathrm{F}^2}{2m^\ast}\,,
                &&
                v_\mathrm{F}
                = \frac{\hbar k_\mathrm{F}}{m^\ast}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:normal-dispersion}
            \end{align}
            The corresponding three-dimensional DOS per unit volume, including both spin directions, is
            \begin{equation}
                N(\varepsilon)
                = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}
                \left(\frac{2m^\ast}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}
                \sqrt{\varepsilon}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:normal-dos}
            \end{equation}
            Here, $\varepsilon$ is measured from the band edge. Superconductivity involves only a narrow energy shell around $E_\mathrm{F}$. We therefore use
            \begin{equation}
                N(\varepsilon) \approx N_0 \equiv N(E_\mathrm{F})\,.
                \label{eq:micro:constant-normal-dos}
            \end{equation}
            In the superconducting description below, $\xi$ and $E$ are instead measured relative to the chemical potential, which satisfies $\mu\simeq E_\mathrm{F}$ for a conventional metal.

            Within the free-electron approximation with three conduction electrons per atom, bulk aluminum has $m^\ast\approx m$, $E_\mathrm{F}\approx\qty{11.7}{\electronvolt}$, $v_\mathrm{F}\approx2.0\cdot10^6\,\unit{\meter\per\second}$, and a Fermi wavelength of approximately $\qty{0.36}{\nano\meter}$. We use these as representative material scales, not as parameters extracted from the devices studied in this thesis. \cite{ashcroft_solid_1976}

            The normal-state electron and hole excitations are independent. BCS pairing couples these sectors and reorganizes them into Bogoliubov quasiparticles.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{BCS Mean Field}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:bcs-mean-field}
        %=========================================================

            In BCS theory, an effective attraction pairs electrons in time reversed states near the Fermi surface. The mean field approximation replaces this interaction by the complex local pair potential
            \begin{align}
                \widetilde{\Delta}(\vec r)
                =-V\left\langle
                \hat\psi_\downarrow(\vec r)
                \hat\psi_\uparrow(\vec r)
                \right\rangle
                =\Delta(\vec r)e^{\ima\phi(\vec r)}\,,
                &&
                \Delta(\vec r):=|\widetilde{\Delta}(\vec r)|\,.
                \label{eq:micro:complex-delta}
            \end{align}
            In the homogeneous bulk system considered below, the nonnegative magnitude $\Delta$ equals the excitation gap, while $\phi$ describes condensate coherence. The attractive coupling $V<0$ acts within the weak-coupling pairing shell. The pair potential must be determined self-consistently from the quasiparticle spectrum derived below. \cite{bardeen_theory_1957}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Bogoliubov-de~Gennes Eigenproblem}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:bdg-eigenproblem}
        %=========================================================

            For spin-singlet pairing without spin dependent fields, the mean field problem separates into two equivalent $2\times2$ electron--hole blocks. We define the reduced Nambu spinor and Bogoliubov-de~Gennes (BdG) Hamiltonian as
            \begin{align}
                \Psi_k(\vec r)
                =
                \begin{pmatrix}
                    u_k(\vec r)\\
                    v_k(\vec r)
                \end{pmatrix}\,,
                &&
                \hat H_\mathrm{BdG}(\vec r)
                =
                \begin{pmatrix}
                    \hat H_\mathrm{N}(\vec r) & \widetilde{\Delta}(\vec r)\\
                    \widetilde{\Delta}^{\ast}(\vec r) & -\hat H_\mathrm{N}^\ast(\vec r)
                \end{pmatrix}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:bdg-definitions}
            \end{align}
            The amplitudes $u_k$ and $v_k$ describe the electron and hole components of one quasiparticle mode. The diagonal blocks propagate both components according to the normal-state problem, while the off-diagonal pair potential converts an electron into its time reversed hole partner and vice versa. A superconducting excitation is therefore generally a coherent mixture of both components.

            The allowed quasiparticle modes follow from the eigenvalue problem
            \begin{equation}
                \hat H_\mathrm{BdG}(\vec r)\Psi_k(\vec r)
                =E_k\Psi_k(\vec r)\,.
                \label{eq:micro:bdg}
            \end{equation}
            Particle--hole symmetry relates every solution at $+E_k$ to a partner at $-E_k$ with exchanged electron and hole components. In the normal-state limit $\widetilde{\Delta}\to0$, the off-diagonal coupling vanishes and both sectors decouple. \cite{bogoljubov_new_1958,nambu_quasi-particles_1960,de_gennes_superconductivity_1966}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Homogeneous Limit}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:homogeneous-limit}
        %=========================================================

            For uniform $\widetilde{\Delta}=\Delta e^{\ima\phi}$, the normal-state energy $\xi_{\vec q}=\hbar^2q^2/(2m^\ast)-\mu$ gives
            \begin{align}
                \begin{pmatrix}
                    \xi_{\vec q} & \widetilde{\Delta}\\
                    \widetilde{\Delta}^{\ast} & -\xi_{\vec q}
                \end{pmatrix}
                \begin{pmatrix}u_{\vec q}\\v_{\vec q}\end{pmatrix}
                =E_{\vec q}
                \begin{pmatrix}u_{\vec q}\\v_{\vec q}\end{pmatrix},
                &&
                E_{\vec q}=\sqrt{\xi_{\vec q}^2+\Delta^2}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:bdg-homogeneous}
            \end{align}
            The quasiparticle spectrum is separated from the Fermi level by the gap $\Delta$. The homogeneous spectrum has two immediate consequences. It closes the BCS self-consistency equation for $\Delta(T)$, and it maps the normal-state DOS onto the superconducting DOS.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Superconducting Gap}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:superconducting-gap}
        %=========================================================

            Writing the homogeneous dispersion from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:bdg-homogeneous} as $E(\xi)$, evaluation of the pair expectation value in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:complex-delta} gives the weak-coupling self-consistency equation
            \begin{equation}
                1
                =\frac{|V|N_0}{2}
                \int_0^{\hbar\omega_\mathrm{D}}
                \frac{1}{E(\xi)}
                \tanh\!\left(\frac{E(\xi)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)
                \,\mathrm d\xi\,.
                \label{eq:micro:gap-self-consistency}
            \end{equation}
            Here, $N_0/2$ is the normal-state DOS per spin and $\omega_\mathrm{D}$ is the Debye frequency, so $\hbar\omega_\mathrm{D}$ bounds the phonon mediated pairing shell. At each temperature, Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:gap-self-consistency} implicitly determines $\Delta(T)$. Its zero-temperature solution defines $\Delta_0\equiv\Delta(0)$, while $T_\mathrm{c}$ is the temperature at which the nonzero solution vanishes. Comparing these limits gives the weak-coupling relation
            \begin{equation}
                \Delta_0
                \approx 1.764\, k_\mathrm{B} T_\mathrm{c}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:gap-zero-temperature}
            \end{equation}
            Thus, $\Delta_0$ or $T_\mathrm{c}$ sets the material scale, while the self-consistency equation fixes the normalized temperature dependence $\Delta(T)/\Delta_0$. \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}
            Representative bulk aluminum values are
            \begin{align}
                \Delta_0 \approx \qty{180}{\micro\electronvolt}\,, &&
                T_\mathrm{c} \approx \qty{1.2}{\kelvin}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:aluminum-scales}
            \end{align}
            Thin-film disorder, thickness, and fabrication can shift both values. Unless stated otherwise, the following figures use these representative scales. \cite{giaever_study_1961,tinkham_introduction_2004}

            \begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                % \vspace{-1em}
                \thesisplot{theory/micro}{gap}
                % \vspace{-.5em}
                \caption{Temperature dependence of the superconducting gap in the weak-coupling limit (Eq.~\ref{eq:micro:gap-temperature}).}
                \label{fig:micro:gap}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            Rather than solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:gap-self-consistency} numerically at every temperature, we use the convenient interpolation
            \begin{equation}
                \frac{\Delta(T)}{\Delta_0}
                    \approx \tanh\!\left(
                        1.74\,\sqrt{\frac{T_\mathrm{c}}{T} - 1}
                    \right).
                \label{eq:micro:gap-temperature}
            \end{equation}
            The gap vanishes continuously at $T_\mathrm{c}$. At low temperature, it sets the approximate quasiparticle onset at $|eV|=\Delta$ for an NIS junction and $|eV|=2\Delta$ for an identical SIS junction. \cite{muhlschlegel_thermodynamischen_1959,tinkham_introduction_2004}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Density of States}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:density-of-states}
        %=========================================================

            Pairing changes the relation between the normal-state energy $\xi$ and the quasiparticle energy $E$ while preserving the total spectral weight. The superconducting DOS therefore follows from the Jacobian of the homogeneous BdG dispersion,
            \begin{equation}
                \frac{N_\mathrm{S}(E)}{N_0}
                = \left|\frac{\mathrm d\xi}{\mathrm dE}\right|
                = \frac{E}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}}
                \qquad (E\geq\Delta)\,.
                \label{eq:micro:dos-bcs}
            \end{equation}
            No real $\xi$ exists for $0\leq E<\Delta$, so the DOS vanishes inside the gap. Particle--hole symmetry extends the result to negative energies through $N_\mathrm{S}(-E)=N_\mathrm{S}(E)$. The Jacobian diverges at the gap edges. \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}

            Nonideal spectra are commonly described by Dynes broadening,
            \begin{equation}
                \frac{N_\mathrm{S}(E)}{N_0}
                    = \left|\Re\!\left(
                        \frac{E+\ima\gamma}{
                        \sqrt{(E+\ima\gamma)^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}
                    \right)\right|\,.
                \label{eq:micro:dos-dynes}
            \end{equation}
            The parameter $\gamma$ describes finite subgap spectral weight and smeared coherence peaks but does not identify a unique microscopic mechanism. \cite{dynes_direct_1978,plecenik_finite-quasiparticle-lifetime_1994}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Occupation and Thermal Broadening}
        \label{subsubsec:micro:occupation-broadening}
        %=========================================================

            The DOS specifies the available states, while the Fermi function determines their equilibrium occupation,
            \begin{equation}
                f(E) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(E/k_\mathrm{B}T\right)}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:fermi-function}
            \end{equation}
            The derivative of this function defines the thermal broadening kernel
            \begin{equation}
                K_\mathrm{T}(E) := - \frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E}\,.
                \label{eq:micro:fermi-kernel}
            \end{equation}
            In the zero-temperature limit, $f(E)\to\theta(-E)$ and $K_\mathrm{T}(E)\to\delta(E)$. Dynes broadening modifies the DOS itself, whereas temperature broadens the energy window sampled in transport. Figure~\ref{fig:micro:dos-fermi} separates these effects.

            The DOS and occupation function provide the spectral and statistical inputs for the tunnel current model that follows.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Tunnel Current}
    \label{subsec:micro:tunnel-current}
    %=========================================================

        A quasiparticle can tunnel when an occupied state in one electrode is aligned with an empty state in the other, with the applied bias controlling their relative energies. We consider electrodes separated by a thin insulating barrier in the low-transmission limit and use a momentum averaged tunneling matrix element. The quasiparticle current is then an incoherent single-particle transfer process. \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_electron_1960,giaever_study_1961}

        In the expressions below, $N_i(E)$ is the DOS normalized to the constant normal-state value of electrode $i$. Thus, a normal electrode has $N_i(E)=1$. This normalization, the junction geometry, and tunneling matrix element are absorbed into the normal-state conductance $G_\mathrm{N}$.

        We define the bias by $eV=\mu_1-\mu_2$. An energy $E$ relative to the chemical potential of electrode 1 therefore corresponds to $E+eV$ relative to that of electrode 2.

        Under these conditions, the microscopic current flowing from electrode 1 to electrode 2 is proportional to the number of occupied states at energy $E$ in electrode 1 and the number of empty states at energy $E+eV$ in electrode 2,
        \begin{equation}
            I_{1\to2}(V)  = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(N_1(E) f_1(E) \right)\cdot \left(N_2(E+eV)\left(1-f_2(E+eV)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
            \label{eq:micro:tunnel-1to2}
        \end{equation}
        where $V$ is the externally applied bias voltage. The reverse process $2\to1$ is described by an analogous expression. Subtracting the two contributions yields the net tunnel current
        \begin{equation}
            I(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^\infty N_1(E)\, N_2(E+eV)\, \left(f_1(E)-f_2(E+eV)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
            \label{eq:micro:tunnel}
        \end{equation}
        where $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the normalized DOSs defined above \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_study_1961}.

        For numerical evaluation, it is useful to express the current through a convolution\footnote{
            Throughout this thesis, we define convolution as $\left(f\otimes g\right)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(E)g(E+x)\,\mathrm{d}E$.
            }
        \begin{equation}
            I(V)
            = G_\mathrm{N}/e\,
            \left[
                \left(N_1f_1\right)\otimes\left(N_2(1-f_2)\right)
                -
                \left(N_1(1-f_1)\right)\otimes\left(N_2f_2\right)
            \right](eV)\,.
            \label{eq:micro:tunnel-conv}
        \end{equation}

        Depending on the electronic state of the electrodes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel} describes the three configurations in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:tunnel-current}. Here, N denotes a normal electrode, I the insulating barrier, and S a superconducting electrode \cite{giaever_electron_1960,giaever_study_1961}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{NIN Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            For a fully normal tunnel junction, both electrodes provide a flat density of states near the Fermi level. The current reduces to
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIN}(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
            \end{equation}
            If both electrodes share the same temperature, the difference of Fermi functions integrates to the applied energy shift $eV$. The result is Ohm's law,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIN}(V) =  G_\mathrm{N}V\,,
            \end{equation}
            showing that an NIN junction is the Ohmic reference for the tunneling limit.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{NIS Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            In an NIS junction, the flat normal-state DOS leaves the current sensitive to the superconducting quasiparticle spectrum,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIS}(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} N_\mathrm{S}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nis}
            \end{equation}

            % \textit{dI--dV} NIS case
            In the NIS case, the \textit{dI--dV} characteristic probes the superconducting DOS through its convolution with the thermal broadening kernel from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:fermi-kernel}. Temperature broadens the measured \textit{dI--dV} response without introducing spectral broadening into the equilibrium DOS\footnote{
                The full width at half maximum of $K_\mathrm{T}(E)$ is approximately $3.5\,k_\mathrm{B}T$. At \qty{30}{\milli\kelvin} this corresponds to about \qty{10}{\micro\electronvolt}, whereas at \qty{300}{\milli\kelvin} it is about \qty{90}{\micro\electronvolt}. The relevant temperature is the electronic temperature, which can exceed the cryostat base temperature due to imperfect filtering, finite electron--phonon coupling, or microwave leakage.
            }.

            Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel-nis} with respect to $V$ acts only on $f(E+eV)$ because both $N_{\mathrm{S}}(E)$ and $f(E)$ are independent of bias. The chain rule produces the thermal broadening kernel at $E+eV$. Using the particle--hole symmetry of $N_\mathrm{S}(E)$ and the even symmetry of $K_\mathrm{T}(E)$ gives
            \begin{align}
                \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}V}I_\mathrm{NIS}(V) = G_\mathrm{N} \left( N_\mathrm{S} \otimes K_\mathrm{T}\right)(eV) \overset{T\to 0}{=} G_\mathrm{N}\,N_\mathrm{S}(eV)\,.
            \end{align}

            These considerations underlie scanning tunneling microscopy, where a normal metal tip above a superconducting surface realizes an NIS junction in the tunneling limit. At sufficiently low electronic temperatures, \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} characteristics provide a local probe of the superconducting quasiparticle DOS.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{SIS Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            In a junction where both electrodes are superconducting, the tunnel current depends on the interaction of two gapped densities of states at $E$ and $E+eV$,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{SIS}(V)
                    = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}
                    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
                        N_\mathrm{S}(E)\,
                        N_\mathrm{S}(E+eV)\,
                        \left(f(E) - f(E+eV)\right)
                    \mathrm{d}E\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-sis}
            \end{equation}

            Unlike the NIS case, the SIS differential conductance is not reduced here to a convolution of one DOS with the thermal kernel. We evaluate it numerically from the full current. For identical electrodes, the dominant quasiparticle threshold occurs near $|eV|=2\Delta$, producing a pronounced feature in both \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV}.

            In NIS junctions, temperature broadens the occupation factor and therefore rounds the coherence peak and smooths the gap edge, while Dynes broadening directly smears the superconducting DOS.

            In SIS junctions, temperature produces thermally activated subgap current and weakens the threshold feature as $T$ approaches $T_\mathrm{c}$. Dynes broadening introduces subgap spectral weight in both electrodes and smears the threshold.

        Fits to measured \textit{I--V} or \textit{dI--dV} curves can provide estimates of $\Delta_0$, $T$, and $\gamma$. However, correlations among these parameters, voltage noise, and spatial gap variations can produce similar line shapes. Reliable extraction therefore requires independent calibration or comparison across multiple spectra.

    \subsection{Photon Assisted Tunneling}
    \label{subsec:micro:pat}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.35\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-pat.pdf_tex}
            \caption{
                Energy schematic of photon assisted tunneling in an SIS junction. Exchange of $nh\nu$ shifts the threshold. Black and gray arrows show photon exchange and quasiparticle transfer.
                }
            \label{fig:micro:pat-schematic}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The static characteristics derived above define the reference current $I_0(V)$. Under microwave irradiation, a quasiparticle may absorb or emit $|n|$ photons while tunneling, changing its energy from $E_0$ to
        \begin{equation}
            E_n = E_0 + n h\nu \qquad (n\in\mathbb{Z})\,,
        \end{equation}
        where $n>0$ corresponds to absorption and $n<0$ to emission. These energy exchanges shift the static tunnel thresholds by integer multiples of $h\nu/e$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:pat-schematic}. To determine the sideband weights, we represent the harmonic voltage by a time dependent phase factor in the tunneling amplitude. Its Fourier expansion then leads to the Tien-Gordon current \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Harmonic Drive and Sideband Formation}
        %=========================================================

        We model the effective voltage across the junction as
        \begin{equation}
            V(t) = V_0 + A\cos(2\pi\nu t)\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat-drive-voltage}
        \end{equation}
        where $V_0$ is the static bias, $A$ is the effective ac voltage amplitude at the junction, and $\nu$ is the drive frequency. We treat the coherent tone as a classical drive, neglect junction backaction, and work in the tunneling limit. The electrodes are assumed to remain locally thermal.

        Gauge freedom allows the voltage dependence to be represented either through time dependent electrode energies or through a phase in the charge transfer amplitude. We use the phase factor representation. For a transferred charge $q$, the accumulated phase and corresponding coupling factor are
        \begin{align}
            \phi_q(t)
            = \frac{q}{\hbar}\int_0^t V(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'\,, &&
            U_q(t)
            \equiv \exp\!\left[-\ima\phi_q(t)\right]\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat-phase}
        \end{align}
        Here, $q=e$ applies to quasiparticle tunneling.

        For the harmonic voltage in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-drive-voltage}, the phase factor separates into static and oscillatory contributions,
        \begin{align}
            U_q(t)
            &= \exp\!\left[-\ima\phi_{q,0}(t)\right]
            \exp\!\left[-\ima\alpha_{\nu, q}\sin(2\pi\nu t)\right]\,,\\
            \phi_{q,0}(t)
            &= \frac{qV_0t}{\hbar}\,,
            \qquad
            \alpha_{\nu, q} = \frac{qA}{h\nu}\,.
        \end{align}
        The oscillatory phase is expanded with the Jacobi-Anger identity,
        \begin{equation}
            \exp\!\left[\pm\ima\alpha_{\nu, q}\sin(2\pi\nu t)\right]
            = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
            J_n(\alpha_{\nu, q})\,
            \exp\!\left(\pm\ima n\,2\pi\nu t\right)\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat-jacobi-anger}
        \end{equation}
        This expansion produces the energy shifted processes introduced above, with amplitudes set by the Bessel coefficients $J_n(\alpha_{\nu, q})$ \cite{nist_dlmf_2025}. Figure~\ref{fig:micro:pat-drive} summarizes the drive and resulting sideband amplitudes.

        The sideband picture assumes that the drive redistributes existing quasiparticle tunneling processes without substantially changing the electrode properties. At sufficiently high photon energies, radiation can break Cooper pairs and generate additional quasiparticles, so the equilibrium DOS and local thermal assumptions may fail. At low temperature, the single-photon pair breaking threshold is
        \begin{equation}
            h\nu_\mathrm{pb} = 2\Delta_0\,.
        \end{equation}
        Using the representative gap in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:aluminum-scales} gives $\nu_\mathrm{pb}\approx\qty{87}{\giga\hertz}$ \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}. The installed microwave paths transmitted frequencies up to approximately $\qty{20}{\giga\hertz}$, as established from microwave induced changes in measured \textit{dI--dV} spectra \cite{raif_electronic_2024}. This experimental upper frequency is below the single-photon pair breaking threshold. Direct single-photon pair breaking is therefore excluded within this estimate, although heating, multiphoton absorption, and nonequilibrium quasiparticle generation may still occur.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Tien-Gordon Quasiparticle Tunneling}
        %=========================================================

        We now specialize to quasiparticle tunneling with $q=e$. The Fourier components of $U_e(t)$ describe energy transfers $nh\nu$. For a stationary incoherent tunnel current, averaging over one drive period removes cross terms between different components and leaves the probabilities $J_n^2$. The resulting Tien-Gordon current is
        \begin{equation}
            I_\mathrm{PAT}(V_0)
                = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
                    J_n^2\!\left(\frac{eA}{h\nu}\right)
                    I_0\!\left(V_0 - \frac{n h\nu}{e}\right)\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat-iv}
        \end{equation}
        where $I_0(V)$ is the static current derived above. Each term contributes a replica of the static characteristic shifted by $n h\nu/e$ and weighted by the sideband probability $J_n^2(\alpha_{\nu,e})$ \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963}.

Differences between driven NIS and SIS junctions therefore follow from their static characteristics, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:pat}. The treatment excludes junction backaction, drive induced heating, and coherent subgap transport.
