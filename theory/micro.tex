\section{Microscopic Description}
\label{sec:micro}
%=========================================================

    The microscopic description begins with Bardeen--Cooper--Schrieffer (BCS) theory. An effective phonon-mediated attraction allows electrons near the Fermi surface to form Cooper pairs with opposite momentum and spin. The resulting condensate is characterized by a complex pairing order parameter whose magnitude determines the gap in the quasiparticle spectrum. This section focuses on the spectral properties and tunnel-limit quasiparticle current. Phase-dependent Cooper-pair transport is introduced in the macroscopic description, while finite-transmission electron--hole processes are treated in the mesoscopic description. \cite{cooper_bound_1956,bardeen_microscopic_1957}

    A key assumption of the BCS framework is that this effective interaction is weak compared to the electronic energy scales of the metal. This situation is referred to as the weak-coupling limit. In this regime, the attractive interaction acts only within a narrow energy shell around the Fermi surface, typically a few meV compared to Fermi energies of several eV. As a result, only a small fraction of electrons near the Fermi level participate in pairing, while the rest of the electronic structure remains essentially unaffected. Because the coupling is weak, the resulting energy gap and the critical temperature are small but can be predicted with high accuracy from the microscopic parameters. Aluminum is a representative weak-coupling BCS superconductor and provides the material parameters used throughout this chapter.

    The superconducting order parameter is the complex pairing amplitude
    \footnote{
        Here and in the following, ``$s$-wave'' refers to the isotropic angular dependence of the superconducting order parameter on the Fermi surface. It should not be confused with atomic $s$ orbitals. Aluminum has predominantly $sp$-hybridized conduction states, while its Cooper-pair wave function is $s$-wave in the sense of isotropic pairing symmetry.
    }
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta(\vec{r}) = \left|\Delta\right| e^{\ima\phi(\vec{r})}\,.
        \label{eq:micro:complex-delta}
    \end{equation}
    Its magnitude represents the pairing strength, while its phase encodes macroscopic phase coherence. In the Bogoliubov--de~Gennes formalism, $\Delta(\vec{r})$ appears as the pairing potential that couples electron and hole amplitudes. Although this connection belongs to the microscopic mean-field description, we postpone its discussion to the mesoscopic treatment in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:bdg}. \cite{bogoljubov_new_1958,de_gennes_boundary_1964,de_gennes_superconductivity_1966}
    
    For the tunnel-spectroscopy model developed here, only the magnitude $|\Delta|$ enters the quasiparticle spectrum. We therefore treat $\Delta$ as a spatially uniform scalar. Phase-dependent transport and spatial variations of the order parameter require additional descriptions introduced later in this chapter.

    The following subsections introduce the temperature dependence of the superconducting gap, the superconducting DOS and its phenomenological broadening, and the tunnel-current expressions that connect these quantities to spectroscopy. The final subsection introduces the common electromagnetic-drive convention and extends the static tunnel model to photon-assisted transport.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Superconducting Gap}
    \label{subsec:micro:sc-gap}
    %=========================================================

        In the weak-coupling limit, the theoretical treatment of superconductivity simplifies considerably. The normal-state density of states can be taken as constant in the narrow energy shell relevant for pairing, and the attractive interaction may be approximated as uniform across this shell. As a consequence, many observable quantities assume universal forms, most notably the ratio between the zero-temperature gap and the critical temperature,
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta_0 \approx 1.764\, k_\mathrm{B} T_\mathrm{c}\,,
            \label{eq:micro:Delta0}
        \end{equation}
            which holds for all weak-coupling BCS superconductors.

        For aluminum, the characteristic superconducting scales are, to good approximation,
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta_0 \approx 180\,\mu\mathrm{eV}\,,
            \qquad
            T_\mathrm{c} \approx 1.2\,\mathrm{K}\,.
            \label{eq:micro:aluminum-scales}
        \end{equation}
        These values set the energy, voltage, and temperature scales of the aluminum junctions considered throughout this thesis. Unless stated otherwise, all following figures use these values. \cite{tinkham_introduction_2004,murray_material_2021}

        The superconducting gap, however, is not constant with temperature. At $T=0$, electrons within the pairing shell around the Fermi energy form a condensate characterized by a well-defined order parameter. With increasing temperature, thermal excitations break an increasing fraction of Cooper pairs, reducing the number of electrons bound in the superconducting state. As fewer pairs contribute to the condensate, the magnitude of the order parameter and the minimum quasiparticle excitation energy $\Delta(T)$ decrease. Breaking a Cooper pair creates two quasiparticles and therefore requires an energy of at least $2\Delta(T)$.

        This reduction proceeds smoothly until the critical temperature $T_\mathrm{c}$ is reached. At $T=T_\mathrm{c}$, thermal fluctuations fully disrupt the pairing correlations, the order parameter vanishes continuously, and superconductivity is lost. The temperature dependence of the gap reflects the balance between the condensation energy gained by pairing and the entropy associated with thermal excitations. The universal weak-coupling curve is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:gap}.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{theory/micro}{gap.pgf}
            \caption{Temperature dependence of the superconducting gap $\Delta(T)$ in the weak-coupling limit.}
            \label{fig:micro:gap}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        In the BCS framework, the functional form of $\Delta(T)$ is obtained from the self-consistent gap equation. Its numerical solution yields a universal curve valid for all weak-coupling superconductors. A commonly used approximation to this solution is
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{\Delta(T)}{\Delta_0}
                \approx \tanh\!\left(
                    1.74\,\sqrt{\frac{T_\mathrm{c}}{T} - 1}
                \right),
            \label{eq:micro:DeltaT}
        \end{equation}
        which reproduces the numerical result with high accuracy throughout the full temperature range. \cite{muhlschlegel_thermodynamischen_1959}

        At low temperature, the gap directly sets the approximate quasiparticle onset in tunnel spectroscopy: $|eV|=\Delta(T)$ for an NIS junction and $|eV|=2\Delta(T)$ for an SIS junction with identical electrodes.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Density of States}
    \label{subsec:micro:dos}
    %=========================================================

        Throughout this section, energies are measured relative to the chemical potential, which for conventional metals coincides with the Fermi energy to very good approximation. Thus $E=0$ corresponds to the Fermi level around which superconducting correlations form.

        As introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:quasi:micro}, the normal-state density of states (DOS) is approximately constant near the Fermi level. We denote its value per unit volume, including both spin directions, by $N_0 \equiv N(E_\mathrm{F})$.

        When superconductivity sets in, pairing correlations reorganize this otherwise flat spectrum. A gap of width $2\Delta$ opens around the Fermi level where single-particle excitations are absent in the ideal BCS limit. The spectral weight removed from the gap interior is redistributed to the gap edges, producing the characteristic coherence peaks. The resulting normalized DOS is
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{N_\mathrm{S}(E)}{N_0} = 
                \left\{
                \begin{array}{@{}r@{\quad}l@{}}
                    0 & (|E| < \Delta)\\
                    \dfrac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}} & (|E| \ge \Delta)
                \end{array}
                \right.\,.
            \label{eq:micro:dos-bcs}
        \end{equation}       
        which diverges at the gap edges due to the flattening of the Bogoliubov quasiparticle dispersion. Within mean-field BCS theory this DOS is strictly sharp. Temperature affects only the occupation of the states through the Fermi--Dirac distribution and does not broaden the DOS itself.

        Real superconductors, however, do not exhibit perfectly sharp coherence peaks. A widely used phenomenological description is Dynes broadening, introduced through the substitution $E \rightarrow E + \ima\gamma$,
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{N_\mathrm{S}(E)}{N_0}
                = \left|\Re\!\left(
                    \frac{E+\ima\gamma}{
                    \sqrt{(E+\ima\gamma)^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}
                \right)\right|\,.
            \label{eq:micro:dos-dynes}
        \end{equation}
        The Dynes parameter\footnote{Throughout this work we distinguish between the Dynes broadening parameter $\gamma$, which enters the quasiparticle DOS in the microscopic tunneling description, and the tunneling rates $\Gamma$ that appear in the stochastic description of incoherent charge transfer. These quantities refer to different physical mechanisms and should not be confused.} $\gamma$ parametrizes finite subgap spectral weight and smeared coherence peaks. Similar line shapes can arise from finite lifetimes, spatial inhomogeneity, pair breaking, or nonequilibrium effects. A fitted value of $\gamma$ therefore does not uniquely identify one mechanism. \cite{dynes_direct_1978}

        The DOS specifies the available single-particle states, whereas the Fermi--Dirac distribution determines their occupation at a given temperature. For conventional metals $\mu \approx E_\mathrm{F}$, so the energy scale remains consistent with the above convention. The equilibrium occupation is
        \begin{equation}
            f(E) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\frac{E}{k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)}\,,
            \label{eq:micro:fermi-dirac}
        \end{equation}
        which reduces to the step function $\theta(-E)$ as $T\to 0$.

        For future application, we can define the thermal broadening kernel
        \begin{equation}
            K_\mathrm{T}(E) := - \frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E}\,,
            \label{eq:micro:fermi-kernel}
        \end{equation}
        which reduces to a delta distribution $\delta(E)$ as $T\to 0$.

        Figure~\ref{fig:micro:dos-fermi}(a) visualizes how Dynes broadening smooths the coherence peaks of $N_\mathrm{S}(E)$, while Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:dos-fermi}(b) and (c) show how finite temperature smears the Fermi edge and widens the thermal broadening kernel.

        Dynes broadening modifies the phenomenological DOS itself, whereas temperature broadens the energy window sampled by a transport measurement. This distinction is essential when interpreting measured spectral line shapes.

    \subsection{Tunnel Current}
    \label{subsec:micro:tunnel-current}
    %=========================================================

        Tunneling spectroscopy provides a direct and conceptually transparent probe of the quasiparticle excitation spectrum. When two electrodes are separated by a sufficiently thin insulating barrier, electrons may tunnel quantum-mechanically between them even though the process is classically forbidden. We consider the low-transmission limit and use a momentum-averaged tunneling matrix element. In this model, the quasiparticle current is an incoherent single-particle transfer process governed by the available electronic states and their occupations. \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_electron_1960,giaever_study_1961}

        The tunnel barriers used in this thesis are formed by controlled oxidation between two aluminum depositions. The resulting Al--AlO$_x$--Al junctions operate in the low-transmission regime. Their barrier thickness and interface quality determine the normal-state conductance, channel transparencies, and subgap leakage \cite{niemeyer_observation_1976,dolan_offset_1977,sprenger_interplay_2019,sobral-rey_interplay_2022}.
        
        % %=========================================================
        % \subsubsection*{General Tunnel Junction}
        % %=========================================================

            In the expressions below, $N_i(E)$ is the DOS relative to the constant normal-state value of electrode $i$. Thus, a normal electrode has $N_i(E)=1$. The constant DOS scales, junction geometry, and tunneling matrix element are absorbed into the normal-state conductance $G_\mathrm{N}$.

            Under these conditions, the microscopic current flowing from electrode 1 to electrode 2 is proportional to the number of occupied states at energy $E$ in electrode 1 and the number of empty states at energy $E+eV$ in electrode 2,
            \begin{equation}
                I_{1\to2}(V)  = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(N_1(E) f_1(E) \right)\cdot \left(N_2(E+eV)\left(1-f_2(E+eV)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-1to2}
            \end{equation}
            where $V$ is the externally applied bias voltage. The reverse process $2\to1$ is described by an analogous expression. Subtracting the two contributions yields the measurable tunnel current
            \begin{equation}
                I(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^\infty N_1(E)\, N_2(E+eV)\, \left(f_1(E)-f_2(E+eV)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel}
            \end{equation}
            where $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the relative DOS factors defined above.

            For numerical evaluation, it is useful to express the current through a convolution\footnote{
                Throughout this thesis, we define convolution as $\left(f\otimes g\right)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(E)g(E+x)\,\mathrm{d}E$.
                }
            \begin{equation}
                I(V)
                = G_\mathrm{N}/e\,
                \left[
                    \left(N_1f_1\right)\otimes\left(N_2(1-f_2)\right)
                    -
                    \left(N_1(1-f_1)\right)\otimes\left(N_2f_2\right)
                \right](eV)\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-conv}
            \end{equation}

            This equation highlights the two microscopic ingredients of tunneling. The densities of states $N_1(E)$ and $N_2(E)$ specify where electrons may tunnel, while the Fermi--Dirac distributions specify which states are occupied. The factor $f_1(E)-f_2(E+eV)$ encodes the occupation imbalance created by the applied bias and ensures that current flows only when filled states in one electrode overlap with empty states in the other. The tunnel current therefore represents a convolution of the electronic structure of the two electrodes with their occupations, directly linking quasiparticle physics to the measurable \textit{I--V} characteristic.

            Throughout this section we adopt the asymmetric voltage convention, in which the applied bias shifts only the quasiparticle energies of electrode 2, $E \to E+eV$. This choice is equivalent to a gauge transformation and leaves the physics unchanged, but keeps the microscopic expressions particularly transparent.

            Depending on the electronic state of the two electrodes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel} describes the three tunnel-junction configurations shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:tunnel-current}. Here, $N$ denotes a normal electrode, I the insulating barrier, and S a superconducting electrode. An NIN junction provides an Ohmic reference and determines the normal-state conductance $G_\mathrm{N}$. In an NIS junction, the normal electrode acts as an approximately energy-independent probe of the superconducting DOS. An SIS junction instead measures the overlap of two gapped densities of states, which shifts the quasiparticle threshold to $|eV|=2\Delta$ for identical electrodes. These configurations therefore provide increasing sensitivity to the superconducting excitation spectrum.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{NIN Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            In the case of a fully normal-conducting tunnel junction, both electrodes provide a flat density of states near the Fermi level. The microscopic expression for the current therefore reduces to
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIN}(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nin}
            \end{equation}
            If both electrodes share the same temperature, the difference of Fermi functions integrates to the applied energy shift $eV$. The microscopic expression thus reproduces Ohm's law,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIN}(V) =  G_\mathrm{N}V\,,
                \label{eq:micro:ohms-law}
            \end{equation}
            showing that an NIN junction exhibits purely Ohmic behavior in the tunneling limit.

            % NIS case
            \subsubsection*{NIS Tunnel Junction}
            In a junction between a normal metal and a superconductor, the tunneling current becomes sensitive to the superconducting quasiparticle spectrum. Since the normal electrode provides a flat DOS, only the superconducting DOS enters the microscopic expression,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIS}(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} N_\mathrm{S}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nis}
            \end{equation}

            % dI/dV NIS case
            In the NIS case, the \textit{dI--dV} characteristic probes the superconducting DOS through its convolution with the thermal broadening kernel from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:fermi-kernel}. This kernel sets the fundamental energy resolution\footnote{
                A commonly used estimate is $\delta E \approx 3.5\,k_\mathrm{B}T$. At 30\,mK (typical for a dilution refrigerator), this corresponds to about 10\,\textmu eV, whereas at 300\,mK (He$_\text{3}$ cryostat) the resolution is limited to about 100\,\textmu eV. The relevant temperature is the electronic temperature, which can exceed the cryostat base temperature due to imperfect filtering, finite electron-phonon coupling, or microwave leakage.
            } in NIS tunnel spectroscopy. Temperature does not change the equilibrium superconducting DOS in this model. Instead, it broadens the measured \textit{dI--dV} response. As the temperature approaches zero, \textit{dI--dV} becomes directly proportional to the superconducting DOS.

            Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel-nis} with respect to $V$ acts only on $f(E+eV)$ because both $N_{\mathrm{S}}(E)$ and $f(E)$ are independent of the applied bias. The chain rule then produces the thermal broadening kernel at the shifted energy $E+eV$. Using the particle-hole symmetry of $N_\mathrm{S}(E)$ and the even symmetry of $K_\mathrm{T}(E)$, the result is the convolution evaluated at $eV$,
            \begin{equation}
                \frac{\mathrm{d}I_\mathrm{NIS}(V)}{\mathrm{d}V} = G_\mathrm{N} \left( N_\mathrm{S} \otimes K_\mathrm{T}\right)(eV)\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nis-didv}
            \end{equation}

            These considerations directly underlie scanning tunneling microscopy, where a normal-metal tip above a superconducting surface realizes an NIS junction in the tunneling limit. At sufficiently low electronic temperatures, the resulting \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} characteristics provide a direct and spatially localized probe of the superconductor's quasiparticle DOS. The thermal broadening of the spectrum can also constrain the electronic temperature.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{SIS Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            In a junction where both electrodes are superconducting, the tunnel current depends on the multiplicative overlap of the two gapped densities of states, evaluated at energies $E$ and $E+eV$. The microscopic expression now reads
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{SIS}(V)
                    = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}
                    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
                        N_\mathrm{S}(E)\,
                        N_\mathrm{S}(E+eV)\,
                        \left(f(E) - f(E+eV)\right)
                    \mathrm{d}E\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-sis}
            \end{equation}

            SIS transport probes the voltage-dependent overlap of both superconducting densities of states. Unlike the NIS case, its differential conductance is not reduced here to a convolution of one DOS with the thermal kernel. The \textit{dI--dV} characteristics shown below are therefore evaluated numerically from the full SIS current. For identical electrodes, quasiparticle tunneling begins near $|eV|=2\Delta$, producing a pronounced threshold in both \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV}.

            % ref figures
            Figure~\ref{fig:micro:tunnel-iv-didv} summarizes these effects and makes the contrasting roles of temperature and Dynes broadening in NIS and SIS junctions explicit.

            In NIS junctions, temperature primarily broadens the occupation factor and therefore rounds the coherence peak and smooths the gap edge, while Dynes broadening directly reduces the sharpness of the superconducting DOS itself.

            In SIS junctions, the impact of both mechanisms is amplified by the multiplicative appearance of the DOS from each electrode. Temperature generates noticeable subgap conductance and diminishes the peak height as it approaches the critical temperature. Dynes broadening suppresses the spectral features more strongly than in the NIS case because it acts on both electrodes simultaneously.

            Comparing measured \textit{I--V} or \textit{dI--dV} data with this model can constrain $\Delta_0$, $T$, and $\gamma$. These parameters may be correlated, and additional broadening from noise or spatial inhomogeneity can produce similar line shapes. Their interpretation therefore requires independent calibration or comparison across multiple spectra.

    \subsection{Photon-Assisted Tunneling}
    \label{subsec:micro:pat}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-pat.pdf_tex}
            \caption{Photon-assisted SIS tunneling. Exchange of $nh\nu$ shifts the threshold. Black and grey arrows show photon exchange and quasiparticle transfer.}
            \label{fig:micro:pat:schematic}
        \end{wrapfigure}

        The static characteristics derived above define the reference current $I_0(V)$. Under microwave irradiation, a quasiparticle may absorb or emit $|n|$ photons while tunneling. Its energy then changes from the value $E_0$ in the static process to
        \begin{equation}
            E_n = E_0 + n h\nu \qquad (n\in\mathbb{Z})\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat-En}
        \end{equation}
        where $n>0$ corresponds to absorption and $n<0$ to emission. These energy exchanges open additional tunneling channels and shift the static thresholds by integer multiples of $h\nu/e$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:pat:schematic}. We now describe this intuitive sideband picture through the phase generated by a harmonic voltage before deriving the corresponding Tien--Gordon current.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Harmonic Drive and Sideband Formation}
        %=========================================================

        We model the effective voltage across the junction as
        \begin{equation}
            V(t) = V_0 + A\cos(2\pi\nu t)\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat:drive-voltage}
        \end{equation}
        where $V_0$ is the static bias, $A$ is the effective ac voltage amplitude at the junction, and $\nu$ is the drive frequency. We treat the coherent tone as a prescribed classical drive, neglect junction backaction, and work in the tunneling limit. The electrodes are assumed to remain locally thermal.

        Gauge freedom allows the voltage dependence to be represented either through time-dependent electrode energies or through a phase in the charge-transfer amplitude. We use the phase-factor representation. For a process that transfers a positive charge magnitude $q$, the accumulated phase is
        \begin{equation}
            \phi_q(t)
            = \frac{q}{\hbar}\int_0^t V(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat:phase}
        \end{equation}
        and the corresponding coupling amplitude carries the factor
        \begin{equation}
            U_q(t)
            \equiv \exp\!\left[-\ima\phi_q(t)\right]\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:phase-factor}
        \end{equation}
        Here, $q=e$ applies to quasiparticle tunneling. The corresponding $q=2e$ phase dynamics of the superconducting condensate are treated separately in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:shapiro}.

        For the harmonic voltage in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat:drive-voltage}, the phase factor separates into static and oscillatory contributions,
        \begin{align}
            U_q(t)
            &= \exp\!\left[-\ima\phi_{q,0}(t)\right]
            \exp\!\left[-\ima\alpha_q\sin(2\pi\nu t)\right]\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat:phase-decomposition}\\
            \phi_{q,0}(t)
            &= \frac{qV_0t}{\hbar}\,,
            \qquad
            \alpha_q = \frac{qA}{h\nu}\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:drive-parameters}
        \end{align}
        The oscillatory phase is expanded with the Jacobi--Anger identity,
        \begin{equation}
            \exp\!\left[\pm\ima\alpha_q\sin(2\pi\nu t)\right]
            = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
            J_n(\alpha_q)\,
            \exp\!\left(\pm\ima n\,2\pi\nu t\right)\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:jacobi-anger}
        \end{equation}
        This expansion produces the energy-shifted channels introduced above, with amplitudes set by the Bessel coefficients $J_n(\alpha_q)$. Figure~\ref{fig:micro:pat:drive} summarizes the harmonic drive, charge-transfer phase, phase factor, and resulting sideband amplitudes.

        The sideband picture assumes that the drive redistributes existing quasiparticle tunneling processes without substantially changing the electrodes themselves. At sufficiently high photon energies, however, radiation can break Cooper pairs and generate additional quasiparticles, so the equilibrium DOS and local thermal assumptions may no longer apply. The single-photon pair-breaking threshold is
        \begin{equation}
            h\nu_\mathrm{pb} = 2\Delta_0\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:pair-breaking}
        \end{equation}
        For aluminum, this corresponds to $\nu_\mathrm{pb}\approx87\,\mathrm{GHz}$. The experimental setup transmitted frequencies up to approximately $20\,\mathrm{GHz}$, below this threshold. Direct single-photon pair breaking is therefore excluded, although heating, multiphoton absorption, and nonequilibrium quasiparticle generation may still occur. \cite{tinkham_introduction_2004,murray_material_2021}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Tien--Gordon Quasiparticle Tunneling}
        %=========================================================

        We now specialize to quasiparticle tunneling with $q=e$ and $\alpha_e=eA/(h\nu)$. The present calculation applies the phase factor $U_e(t)$ to the quasiparticle tunneling amplitude and retains the resulting incoherent contribution. The Jacobi--Anger expansion assigns the probability $J_n^2(\alpha_e)$ to the $n$-th sideband. Averaging the tunneling probability over one drive period gives the Tien--Gordon current
        \begin{equation}
            I_\mathrm{PAT}(V_0)
                = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
                    J_n^2\!\left(\frac{eA}{h\nu}\right)
                    I_0\!\left(V_0 - \frac{n h\nu}{e}\right)\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat-iv}
        \end{equation}
        where $I_0(V)$ is the static current derived above. Each term contributes a replica of the static characteristic shifted by $n h\nu/e$ and weighted by the probability $J_n^2(\alpha_e)$ of absorbing or emitting $|n|$ photons. \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963}

        Photon-assisted tunneling redistributes an existing quasiparticle current rather than introducing a new transport mechanism. Differences between driven NIS and SIS junctions therefore follow from their static characteristics, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:pat}. Within this model, the sideband spacing determines $\nu$, while the relative weights provide the effective drive amplitude $A$. The treatment excludes junction backaction, drive-induced heating, and coherent subgap transport.
