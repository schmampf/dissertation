%=========================================================
\section{Microscopic Description}
\label{sec:micro}
%=========================================================

    The microscopic description begins with Bardeen--Cooper--Schrieffer (BCS) theory. An effective phonon-mediated attraction allows electrons near the Fermi surface to form Cooper pairs with opposite momentum and spin. The resulting condensate has a complex pairing order parameter whose magnitude sets the quasiparticle gap. This section focuses on spectral properties and tunnel-limit quasiparticle current. Phase-dependent Cooper-pair transport is introduced in the macroscopic description, while finite-transmission electron--hole processes are treated in the mesoscopic description. \cite{cooper_bound_1956,bardeen_theory_1957}

    We work in the weak-coupling limit, where the attractive interaction acts only within a narrow energy shell around the Fermi surface. Only electrons near the Fermi level participate in pairing, while the rest of the electronic structure remains essentially unchanged. Aluminum is a representative weak-coupling \textit{s}-wave BCS superconductor and provides the material parameters used throughout this chapter.\footnote{
        Here and in the following, ``\textit{s}-wave'' refers to the isotropic angular dependence of the superconducting order parameter on the Fermi surface. It should not be confused with atomic \textit{s} orbitals. Aluminum has predominantly \textit{sp}-hybridized conduction states, while its Cooper-pair wave function is \textit{s}-wave in the sense of isotropic pairing symmetry.
    } \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}

    The superconducting order parameter is the complex pairing amplitude
    
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta(\vec{r}) = \left|\Delta\right| e^{\ima\phi(\vec{r})}\,.
        \label{eq:micro:complex-delta}
    \end{equation}
    Its magnitude represents the pairing strength, while its phase encodes macroscopic phase coherence. In the Bogoliubov--de~Gennes formalism, $\Delta(\vec{r})$ couples electron and hole amplitudes as a pairing potential. We postpone this connection to the mesoscopic treatment in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:bdg}. \cite{bogoljubov_new_1958,de_gennes_boundary_1964,de_gennes_superconductivity_1966}
    
    For the tunnel-spectroscopy model developed here, only the magnitude $|\Delta|$ enters the quasiparticle spectrum. We therefore treat $\Delta$ as a spatially uniform scalar. Phase-dependent transport and spatial variations of the order parameter require additional descriptions introduced later in this chapter.

    The following subsections introduce the temperature dependence of the superconducting gap, the superconducting DOS and its phenomenological broadening, and the tunnel-current expressions that connect these quantities to spectroscopy. The final subsection introduces the common electromagnetic-drive convention and extends the static tunnel model to photon-assisted transport.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Superconducting Gap}
    \label{subsec:micro:sc-gap}
    %=========================================================

        In the weak-coupling limit, the normal-state density of states can be treated as constant over the pairing shell, and the attractive interaction can be approximated as uniform. Many observable quantities then become universal, most notably the ratio between the zero-temperature gap and the critical temperature,
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta_0 \approx 1.764\, k_\mathrm{B} T_\mathrm{c}\,,
            \label{eq:micro:Delta0}
        \end{equation}
            which holds for all weak-coupling BCS superconductors \cite{bardeen_theory_1957}.

        Representative bulk aluminum values are
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta_0 \approx 180\,\mu\mathrm{eV}\,,
            \qquad
            T_\mathrm{c} \approx 1.2\,\mathrm{K}\,.
            \label{eq:micro:aluminum-scales}
        \end{equation}
        The gap is consistent with early aluminum tunneling measurements and with the weak-coupling ratio in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:Delta0} \cite{giaever_study_1961,tinkham_introduction_2004}. These are reference material values, not parameters extracted from a particular device. Thin-film disorder, thickness, and fabrication can shift both $\Delta_0$ and $T_\mathrm{c}$. Unless stated otherwise, the following figures use the representative values in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:aluminum-scales}.

        The superconducting gap is temperature dependent. At $T=0$, electrons within the pairing shell form a condensate with a well-defined order parameter. Increasing temperature breaks an increasing fraction of Cooper pairs, reducing both the order-parameter magnitude and the minimum quasiparticle excitation energy $\Delta(T)$. Breaking one Cooper pair creates two quasiparticles and therefore requires at least $2\Delta(T)$ \cite{bardeen_theory_1957}.

        This reduction proceeds smoothly until $T_\mathrm{c}$ is reached. At $T=T_\mathrm{c}$, the order parameter vanishes continuously and superconductivity is lost. Figure~\ref{fig:micro:gap} shows the universal weak-coupling curve.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[12]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{theory/micro}{gap.pgf}
            \caption{Temperature dependence of the superconducting gap $\Delta(T)$ in the weak-coupling limit.}
            \label{fig:micro:gap}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        In the BCS framework, the functional form of $\Delta(T)$ is obtained from the self-consistent gap equation. Its numerical solution yields a universal curve valid for all weak-coupling superconductors. A commonly used approximation to this solution is
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{\Delta(T)}{\Delta_0}
                \approx \tanh\!\left(
                    1.74\,\sqrt{\frac{T_\mathrm{c}}{T} - 1}
                \right),
            \label{eq:micro:DeltaT}
        \end{equation}
        which provides a convenient interpolation of the numerical weak-coupling result \cite{muhlschlegel_thermodynamischen_1959,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

        At low temperature, the gap directly sets the approximate quasiparticle onset in tunnel spectroscopy: $|eV|=\Delta(T)$ for an NIS junction and $|eV|=2\Delta(T)$ for an SIS junction with identical electrodes.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Density of States}
    \label{subsec:micro:dos}
    %=========================================================

        Throughout this section, energies are measured relative to the chemical potential. For conventional metals this coincides with the Fermi energy to very good approximation, so $E=0$ marks the Fermi level around which superconducting correlations form.

        As introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:quasi:micro}, the normal-state density of states (DOS) is approximately constant near the Fermi level. We denote its value per unit volume, including both spin directions, by $N_0 \equiv N(E_\mathrm{F})$.

        Pairing correlations reorganize this otherwise flat spectrum. In the ideal BCS limit, a gap of width $2\Delta$ opens around the Fermi level, and spectral weight is redistributed from the gap interior to the gap edges. The resulting normalized DOS is
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{N_\mathrm{S}(E)}{N_0} = 
                \left\{
                \begin{array}{@{}r@{\quad}l@{}}
                    0 & (|E| < \Delta)\\
                    \dfrac{|E|}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}} & (|E| \ge \Delta)
                \end{array}
                \right.\,.
            \label{eq:micro:dos-bcs}
        \end{equation}       
        This expression diverges at the gap edges due to the flattening of the Bogoliubov quasiparticle dispersion. Within mean-field BCS theory this DOS is strictly sharp. Temperature changes the occupations through the Fermi--Dirac distribution, not the DOS itself \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

        Real superconductors, however, do not exhibit perfectly sharp coherence peaks. A widely used phenomenological description is Dynes broadening, introduced through the substitution $E \rightarrow E + \ima\gamma$,
        \begin{equation}
            \frac{N_\mathrm{S}(E)}{N_0}
                = \left|\Re\!\left(
                    \frac{E+\ima\gamma}{
                    \sqrt{(E+\ima\gamma)^{2}-\Delta^{2}}}
                \right)\right|\,.
            \label{eq:micro:dos-dynes}
        \end{equation}
        The Dynes parameter\footnote{Throughout this work we distinguish between the Dynes broadening parameter $\gamma$, which enters the quasiparticle DOS in the microscopic tunneling description, and the tunneling rates $\Gamma$ that appear in the stochastic description of incoherent charge transfer. These quantities refer to different physical mechanisms.} $\gamma$ parametrizes finite subgap spectral weight and smeared coherence peaks \cite{dynes_direct_1978}. Similar line shapes can arise from finite lifetimes and other nonideal spectral effects, so a fitted value of $\gamma$ does not identify one microscopic mechanism by itself \cite{plecenik_finite-quasiparticle-lifetime_1994}.

        The DOS specifies the available single-particle states, whereas the Fermi--Dirac distribution determines their occupation. The equilibrium occupation is
        \begin{equation}
            f(E) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\left(\frac{E}{k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)}\,,
            \label{eq:micro:fermi-dirac}
        \end{equation}
        which reduces to the step function $\theta(-E)$ as $T\to 0$ \cite{gross_festkorperphysik_2014}.

        For future application, we can define the thermal broadening kernel
        \begin{equation}
            K_\mathrm{T}(E) := - \frac{\partial f(E)}{\partial E}\,,
            \label{eq:micro:fermi-kernel}
        \end{equation}
        which reduces to a delta distribution $\delta(E)$ as $T\to 0$.

        Figure~\ref{fig:micro:dos-fermi} separates these effects. Dynes broadening smooths the coherence peaks of $N_\mathrm{S}(E)$, while finite temperature smears the Fermi edge and widens the thermal broadening kernel.

        Dynes broadening modifies the phenomenological DOS itself, whereas temperature broadens the energy window sampled by a transport measurement. This distinction is the main observable consequence for fitting measured line shapes.

    
    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Tunnel Current}
    \label{subsec:micro:tunnel-current}
    %=========================================================

        Tunneling spectroscopy probes the quasiparticle excitation spectrum through the overlap of available states and occupations. We consider electrodes separated by a thin insulating barrier in the low-transmission limit and use a momentum-averaged tunneling matrix element. The quasiparticle current is then an incoherent single-particle transfer process. \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_electron_1960,giaever_study_1961}

        % The tunnel barriers used in this thesis are formed by controlled oxidation between two aluminum depositions. Double-angle evaporation with an intermediate oxidation step produces the Al--AlO$_x$--Al overlap junction \cite{dolan_offset_1977,sprenger_interplay_2019}. The tunnel-limit assumption is assessed experimentally from the normal-state resistance and the absence of resolvable high-transmission subgap features.
        
        % %=========================================================
        % \subsubsection*{General Tunnel Junction}
        % %=========================================================

            In the expressions below, $N_i(E)$ is the DOS relative to the constant normal-state value of electrode $i$. Thus, a normal electrode has $N_i(E)=1$. The constant DOS scales, junction geometry, and tunneling matrix element are absorbed into the normal-state conductance $G_\mathrm{N}$.

            Under these conditions, the microscopic current flowing from electrode 1 to electrode 2 is proportional to the number of occupied states at energy $E$ in electrode 1 and the number of empty states at energy $E+eV$ in electrode 2,
            \begin{equation}
                I_{1\to2}(V)  = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left(N_1(E) f_1(E) \right)\cdot \left(N_2(E+eV)\left(1-f_2(E+eV)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-1to2}
            \end{equation}
            where $V$ is the externally applied bias voltage. The reverse process $2\to1$ is described by an analogous expression. Subtracting the two contributions yields the measurable tunnel current
            \begin{equation}
                I(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^\infty N_1(E)\, N_2(E+eV)\, \left(f_1(E)-f_2(E+eV)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel}
            \end{equation}
            where $N_1$ and $N_2$ are the relative DOS factors defined above \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_study_1961}.

            For numerical evaluation, it is useful to express the current through a convolution\footnote{
                Throughout this thesis, we define convolution as $\left(f\otimes g\right)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(E)g(E+x)\,\mathrm{d}E$.
                }
            \begin{equation}
                I(V)
                = G_\mathrm{N}/e\,
                \left[
                    \left(N_1f_1\right)\otimes\left(N_2(1-f_2)\right)
                    -
                    \left(N_1(1-f_1)\right)\otimes\left(N_2f_2\right)
                \right](eV)\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-conv}
            \end{equation}

            The densities of states $N_1(E)$ and $N_2(E)$ specify where electrons may tunnel, while the Fermi--Dirac distributions specify which states are occupied. The factor $f_1(E)-f_2(E+eV)$ encodes the bias-induced occupation imbalance. The tunnel current therefore links quasiparticle spectra directly to the measurable \textit{I--V} characteristic.

            Throughout this section we adopt the asymmetric voltage convention, in which the applied bias shifts only the quasiparticle energies of electrode 2, $E \to E+eV$. This gauge choice leaves the physics unchanged and keeps the microscopic expressions compact.

            Depending on the electronic state of the electrodes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel} describes the three configurations in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:tunnel-current}. Here, $N$ denotes a normal electrode, I the insulating barrier, and S a superconducting electrode. An NIN junction provides an Ohmic reference for $G_\mathrm{N}$. An NIS junction probes the superconducting DOS with an approximately flat normal electrode. An SIS junction probes the overlap of two gapped densities of states, shifting the quasiparticle threshold to $|eV|=2\Delta$ for identical electrodes \cite{giaever_electron_1960,giaever_study_1961}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{NIN Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            For a fully normal-conducting tunnel junction, both electrodes provide a flat density of states near the Fermi level. The current reduces to
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIN}(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right)\mathrm{d}E\,,
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nin}
            \end{equation}
            If both electrodes share the same temperature, the difference of Fermi functions integrates to the applied energy shift $eV$. The result is Ohm's law,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIN}(V) =  G_\mathrm{N}V\,,
                \label{eq:micro:ohms-law}
            \end{equation}
            showing that an NIN junction is the Ohmic reference for the tunneling limit.

            % NIS case
            \subsubsection*{NIS Tunnel Junction}
            In a junction between a normal metal and a superconductor, the normal electrode provides a flat DOS, so the current is sensitive to the superconducting quasiparticle spectrum,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{NIS}(V) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} N_\mathrm{S}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nis}
            \end{equation}

            % dI/dV NIS case
            In the NIS case, the \textit{dI--dV} characteristic probes the superconducting DOS through its convolution with the thermal broadening kernel from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:fermi-kernel}. This kernel sets the energy resolution\footnote{
                The full width at half maximum of $K_\mathrm{T}(E)$ is approximately $3.5\,k_\mathrm{B}T$. At 30\,mK this corresponds to about 10\,\textmu eV, whereas at 300\,mK it is about 90\,\textmu eV. The relevant temperature is the electronic temperature, which can exceed the cryostat base temperature due to imperfect filtering, finite electron--phonon coupling, or microwave leakage.
            } in NIS tunnel spectroscopy. Temperature broadens the measured \textit{dI--dV} response without changing the equilibrium DOS. As $T\to0$, \textit{dI--dV} becomes directly proportional to the superconducting DOS.

            Differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel-nis} with respect to $V$ acts only on $f(E+eV)$ because both $N_{\mathrm{S}}(E)$ and $f(E)$ are independent of bias. The chain rule produces the thermal broadening kernel at $E+eV$. Using the particle-hole symmetry of $N_\mathrm{S}(E)$ and the even symmetry of $K_\mathrm{T}(E)$ gives
            \begin{equation}
                \frac{\mathrm{d}I_\mathrm{NIS}(V)}{\mathrm{d}V} = G_\mathrm{N} \left( N_\mathrm{S} \otimes K_\mathrm{T}\right)(eV)\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-nis-didv}
            \end{equation}

            These considerations underlie scanning tunneling microscopy, where a normal-metal tip above a superconducting surface realizes an NIS junction in the tunneling limit. At sufficiently low electronic temperatures, \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} characteristics provide a local probe of the superconducting quasiparticle DOS. The thermal broadening can also constrain the electronic temperature.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{SIS Tunnel Junction}
        %=========================================================

            In a junction where both electrodes are superconducting, the tunnel current depends on the overlap of two gapped densities of states at $E$ and $E+eV$,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{SIS}(V)
                    = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}
                    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
                        N_\mathrm{S}(E)\,
                        N_\mathrm{S}(E+eV)\,
                        \left(f(E) - f(E+eV)\right)
                    \mathrm{d}E\,.
                \label{eq:micro:tunnel-sis}
            \end{equation}

            SIS transport probes the voltage-dependent overlap of both superconducting densities of states. Unlike the NIS case, its differential conductance is not reduced here to a convolution of one DOS with the thermal kernel. The \textit{dI--dV} characteristics below are evaluated numerically from the full SIS current. For identical electrodes, quasiparticle tunneling begins near $|eV|=2\Delta$, producing a pronounced threshold in both \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV}.

            % ref figures
            Figure~\ref{fig:micro:tunnel-iv-didv} summarizes these effects and compares the roles of temperature and Dynes broadening in NIS and SIS junctions.

            In NIS junctions, temperature primarily broadens the occupation factor and therefore rounds the coherence peak and smooths the gap edge, while Dynes broadening directly reduces the sharpness of the superconducting DOS itself.

            In SIS junctions, both mechanisms are amplified because the DOS appears for each electrode. Temperature generates subgap conductance and reduces the peak height as it approaches the critical temperature. Dynes broadening suppresses spectral features more strongly than in the NIS case because it acts on both electrodes.

            Comparing measured \textit{I--V} or \textit{dI--dV} data with this model can constrain $\Delta_0$, $T$, and $\gamma$. These parameters may be correlated, and additional broadening from noise or spatial inhomogeneity can produce similar line shapes. Their interpretation therefore requires independent calibration or comparison across multiple spectra.

    \subsection{Photon-Assisted Tunneling}
    \label{subsec:micro:pat}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-pat.pdf_tex}
            \caption{
                Energy schematic of pho- ton-assisted tunneling in SIS junction. Exchange of $nh\nu$ shifts the threshold. Black and gray arrows show photon exchange and quasiparticle transfer.
                }
            \label{fig:micro:pat:schematic}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The static characteristics derived above define the reference current $I_0(V)$. Under microwave irradiation, a quasiparticle may absorb or emit $|n|$ photons while tunneling, changing its energy from $E_0$ to
        \begin{equation}
            E_n = E_0 + n h\nu \qquad (n\in\mathbb{Z})\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat-En}
        \end{equation}
        where $n>0$ corresponds to absorption and $n<0$ to emission. These energy exchanges open additional tunneling channels and shift the static thresholds by integer multiples of $h\nu/e$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:pat:schematic}. We describe this sideband picture through the phase generated by a harmonic voltage before deriving the Tien--Gordon current \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Harmonic Drive and Sideband Formation}
        %=========================================================

        We model the effective voltage across the junction as
        \begin{equation}
            V(t) = V_0 + A\cos(2\pi\nu t)\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat:drive-voltage}
        \end{equation}
        where $V_0$ is the static bias, $A$ is the effective ac voltage amplitude at the junction, and $\nu$ is the drive frequency. We treat the coherent tone as a prescribed classical drive, neglect junction backaction, and work in the tunneling limit. The electrodes are assumed to remain locally thermal.

        Gauge freedom allows the voltage dependence to be represented either through time-dependent electrode energies or through a phase in the charge-transfer amplitude. We use the phase-factor representation. For a transferred charge magnitude $q$, the accumulated phase is
        \begin{equation}
            \phi_q(t)
            = \frac{q}{\hbar}\int_0^t V(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat:phase}
        \end{equation}
        and the corresponding coupling amplitude carries the factor
        \begin{equation}
            U_q(t)
            \equiv \exp\!\left[-\ima\phi_q(t)\right]\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:phase-factor}
        \end{equation}
        Here, $q=e$ applies to quasiparticle tunneling. The corresponding $q=2e$ condensate dynamics are treated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:shapiro}.

        For the harmonic voltage in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat:drive-voltage}, the phase factor separates into static and oscillatory contributions,
        \begin{align}
            U_q(t)
            &= \exp\!\left[-\ima\phi_{q,0}(t)\right]
            \exp\!\left[-\ima\alpha_q\sin(2\pi\nu t)\right]\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat:phase-decomposition}\\
            \phi_{q,0}(t)
            &= \frac{qV_0t}{\hbar}\,,
            \qquad
            \alpha_q = \frac{qA}{h\nu}\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:drive-parameters}
        \end{align}
        The oscillatory phase is expanded with the Jacobi--Anger identity,
        \begin{equation}
            \exp\!\left[\pm\ima\alpha_q\sin(2\pi\nu t)\right]
            = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
            J_n(\alpha_q)\,
            \exp\!\left(\pm\ima n\,2\pi\nu t\right)\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:jacobi-anger}
        \end{equation}
        This expansion produces the energy-shifted channels introduced above, with amplitudes set by the Bessel coefficients $J_n(\alpha_q)$ \cite{noauthor_dlmf_nodate}. Figure~\ref{fig:micro:pat:drive} summarizes the drive and resulting sideband amplitudes.

        \end{figure}

        The sideband picture assumes that the drive redistributes existing quasiparticle tunneling processes without substantially changing the electrodes. At sufficiently high photon energies, radiation can break Cooper pairs and generate additional quasiparticles, so the equilibrium DOS and local thermal assumptions may fail. The single-photon pair-breaking threshold is
        \begin{equation}
            h\nu_\mathrm{pb} = 2\Delta_0\,.
            \label{eq:micro:pat:pair-breaking}
        \end{equation}
        Using the representative gap in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:aluminum-scales} gives $\nu_\mathrm{pb}\approx87\,\mathrm{GHz}$ \cite{bardeen_theory_1957,tinkham_introduction_2004}. The installed microwave paths transmitted frequencies up to approximately $20\,\mathrm{GHz}$, as established from microwave-induced changes in measured \textit{dI--dV} spectra \cite{raif_electronic_2024}. This experimental upper frequency is below the representative single-photon threshold. Direct single-photon pair breaking is therefore excluded within this estimate, although heating, multiphoton absorption, and nonequilibrium quasiparticle generation may still occur.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Tien--Gordon Quasiparticle Tunneling}
        %=========================================================

        We now specialize to quasiparticle tunneling with $q=e$ and $\alpha_e=eA/(h\nu)$. Applying $U_e(t)$ to the quasiparticle tunneling amplitude and averaging the tunneling probability over one drive period gives the Tien--Gordon current
        \begin{equation}
            I_\mathrm{PAT}(V_0)
                = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
                    J_n^2\!\left(\frac{eA}{h\nu}\right)
                    I_0\!\left(V_0 - \frac{n h\nu}{e}\right)\,,
            \label{eq:micro:pat-iv}
        \end{equation}
        where $I_0(V)$ is the static current derived above. Each term contributes a replica of the static characteristic shifted by $n h\nu/e$ and weighted by the sideband probability $J_n^2(\alpha_e)$ \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963}.

            \caption{
                Photon-assisted NIS (gray) and SIS (blue) spectra. Sidebands are separated by $h\nu/e$ and weighted by $J_n^2(eA/h\nu)$.

        Photon-assisted tunneling redistributes an existing quasiparticle current rather than introducing a new transport mechanism. Differences between driven NIS and SIS junctions therefore follow from their static characteristics, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:pat}. Within this model, the sideband spacing determines $\nu$, while the relative weights provide the effective drive amplitude $A$. The treatment excludes junction backaction, drive-induced heating, and coherent subgap transport.
