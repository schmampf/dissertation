\section{Josephson Dynamics}
\label{sec:macro}
%=========================================================

    The microscopic section introduced the complex pair potential $\widetilde{\Delta}(\vec r)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:complex-delta}, whose magnitude characterizes the local pairing strength and whose phase describes condensate coherence. When two superconductors are separated by a sufficiently thin weak link, superconducting pair correlations extend into the link from both sides and retain a finite overlap. The two condensates are therefore no longer independent.

    Since their magnitudes remain fixed by the bulk electrodes, their coherent coupling is governed by the phase difference
    \begin{equation}
        \phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2\,,
        \label{eq:macro:phase-difference}
    \end{equation}
    which becomes the central variable of the macroscopic description and determines the supercurrent through the weak link. Figure~\ref{fig:macro:schematic} depicts the resulting coherent Cooper pair transfer across an SIS junction.

    A Josephson junction denotes such a weak link between two superconductors. It can be formed by an insulating barrier, a metallic constriction, or a short normal region. In the low-transmission limit considered here, the overlap and resulting Josephson coupling are weak, but remain sufficient for coherent Cooper pair transfer. Finite-transmission weak links require the mesoscopic description of Sec.~\ref{sec:meso}. \cite{josephson_possible_1962,likharev_superconducting_1979}

    We first introduce the Josephson relations, the Ambegaokar-Baratoff critical current, and the coupling energy. We then connect the ideal voltage biased SIS spectrum to current bias using a generalized resistively shunted junction (RSJ) model that retains the nonlinear quasiparticle branch. The resistively and capacitively shunted junction (RCSJ) model changes the modeling goal by replacing this spectrum with a linear shunt and adding capacitance to describe phase dynamics. Finally, we discuss the distinct voltage and current biased manifestations of microwave phase locking in the Shapiro effect.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Josephson Effect}
    \label{subsec:macro:josephson}
    %=========================================================

        The phase difference $\phi$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:phase-difference} is the collective variable that describes the coherent coupling between the two superconductors. Its static and time-dependent behavior are expressed by the Josephson relations, which connect condensate coherence to the measurable current and voltage \cite{josephson_possible_1962}.

        \subsubsection*{AC Josephson Relation}

            The ac Josephson relation connects the voltage across the weak link directly to the temporal evolution of the phase difference,
            \begin{align}
                \frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{2e}{\hbar}\,V(t)\,.
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-voltage-phase}
            \end{align}
            It follows from the condensate charge $2e$ and is independent of the detailed structure and transmission of the junction. The ac Josephson relation therefore applies to all junction regimes considered in this chapter. A voltage drives the phase difference rather than fixing the current through the weak link \cite{josephson_possible_1962}.

            For a constant voltage $V(t)=V_0$, the phase evolves linearly as
            \begin{align}
                \phi(t) = \phi_0 + 2\pi \nu_0t\,, &&
                \nu_0 = \frac{2e}{h}\,V_0\,,
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-phase-evolution}
            \end{align}
            where $\phi_0$ is the phase difference at $t=0$ and $\nu_0$ is the Josephson frequency. Thus, a dc voltage produces a phase that advances periodically modulo $2\pi$. An oscillating current follows only after this phase evolution is combined with the current--phase relation introduced below.

        \subsubsection*{DC Josephson Relation}

            In the absence of a voltage, Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-voltage-phase} gives a stationary phase difference. The coherent coupling between the condensates can then support a time independent supercurrent. To leading order in the weak coupling, its dependence on phase is described by the sinusoidal current--phase relation (CPR)
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{J}(\phi) = I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi\,,
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-current-phase}
            \end{equation}
            where $I_\mathrm{c}$ is the maximum supercurrent supported by the junction. Figure~\ref{fig:macro:josephson}(a) shows this sinusoidal phase dependence. A finite phase difference can therefore drive a dissipationless current without a voltage drop. This is the dc Josephson effect \cite{josephson_possible_1962}.

            Under a constant finite voltage, inserting the phase evolution from Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-phase-evolution} into the CPR gives
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{J}(t)=I_\mathrm{c}\sin\!\left(\phi_0+2\pi\nu_0t\right)\,.
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-current-evolution}
            \end{equation}
            The supercurrent then oscillates at the Josephson frequency $\nu_0$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:josephson}(b). The dc and ac Josephson effects are therefore two bias conditions of the same phase dynamics. Under microwave irradiation, synchronization of this intrinsic oscillation with the external drive produces voltage plateaus. This effect is known as the inverse ac Josephson effect or as Shapiro steps and is discussed below.

        \subsubsection*{Ambegaokar-Baratoff Relation}

            The Josephson relations determine the phase dependence and temporal evolution of the supercurrent, but do not fix its magnitude. For an SIS junction formed by identical BCS superconductors, the Ambegaokar-Baratoff (AB) theory derives this magnitude from coherent pair tunneling through the barrier. The resulting critical current is
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{c}(T) = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{G_\mathrm{N}\Delta(T)}{e}\,
                \tanh\!\left(\frac{\Delta(T)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right),
                \label{eq:macro:critical-current}
            \end{equation}
            where $G_\mathrm{N}$ is the normal-state conductance of the junction. The temperature dependence combines the reduction of the superconducting gap from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:gap-temperature} with the thermal occupation factor, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:josephson}(c). At zero temperature, Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:critical-current} reduces to $I_\mathrm{c}=\pi G_\mathrm{N}\Delta_0/(2e)$ \cite{ambegaokar_tunneling_1963}.

            Together with the sinusoidal current--phase relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-current-phase}, the AB result determines the complete equilibrium supercurrent for the junction considered here.

        \newpage
        \subsubsection*{Josephson Coupling Energy}

            The strength of the coherent phase coupling is quantified by the Josephson energy
            \begin{equation}
                E_\mathrm{J}=\frac{\hbar I_\mathrm{c}}{2e}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-energy}
            \end{equation}
            which sets the energetic stiffness of the phase and the robustness of coherent Cooper pair transfer. Large $E_\mathrm{J}$ favors a well-defined phase difference, while competing fluctuations weaken this phase locking. The influence of thermal fluctuations is considered phenomenologically in the RCSJ model introduced below. The charge dominated regime, in which the charging energy competes with or exceeds $E_\mathrm{J}$, is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:stochastic} \cite{tinkham_introduction_2004,grabert_single_2013}.

        \subsubsection*{DC Josephson Spectroscopy}

            We first consider dc spectroscopy of an ideal voltage biased SIS Josephson junction. At finite voltage, the phase evolves at the Josephson frequency according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-phase-evolution}. The supercurrent consequently oscillates and averages to zero over one period, so the measured dc current follows the SIS quasiparticle characteristic derived in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel-sis}. At $V=0$, the phase remains at its initial value $\phi_0$ and the junction can support the finite current $I_\mathrm{J}=I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi_0$. In this idealized limit, the voltage fixes only the phase velocity, while the stationary value $\phi_0$ is set by the initial condition or the external circuit. Figures~\ref{fig:macro:Irsj}(a) and (b) show the resulting voltage biased spectrum and its dependence on the initial phase.

            Under current bias, the voltage is no longer fixed externally and instead becomes the dynamical response of the junction. We describe this response with a generalized RSJ model. Coherent Cooper pair transfer and dissipative quasiparticle tunneling occur in parallel and experience the same time dependent voltage, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:rsj}. In contrast to the conventional linear shunt $V/R$, the generalized model retains the microscopic nonlinear quasiparticle characteristic $I_\mathrm{qp}(V)$. 

            The applied current therefore divides according to
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{bias}
                = I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi(t)
                + I_\mathrm{qp}\!\left(V(t)\right)\,.
                \label{eq:macro:rsj-current-balance}
            \end{equation}
            We denote the inverse of the BCS quasiparticle characteristic by $V_\mathrm{qp}(I)$. This construction assumes that the quasiparticle branch follows the instantaneous voltage and that its \textit{I--V} characteristic can be inverted on the branch considered. Together with the ac Josephson relation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rsj-current-balance} determines the time evolution of the phase and voltage. For $|I_\mathrm{bias}|\sleq{} I_\mathrm{c}$, a stationary phase can carry the applied current and the voltage remains zero. Above this range, the phase runs through successive $2\pi$ periods. Its velocity changes during each rotation because the current available for quasiparticle tunneling depends on $\phi$.
            
            Consequently, the phase does not sample all values for equal durations. Although the unweighted phase average of $\sin\phi$ vanishes over one rotation, its time average need not vanish under current bias. This differs from ideal voltage bias, where the phase advances uniformly and the supercurrent averages to zero. The measured dc voltage is the time average over the nonuniform phase motion. For one complete phase rotation, it is
            \begin{equation}
                \overline{V}
                = 2\pi\,\left(
                    \displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}
                    \frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{
                        V_\mathrm{qp}\!\left(I_\mathrm{bias}-I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi\right)
                    }
                \right)^{-1}\,.
                \label{eq:macro:rsj-average-voltage}
            \end{equation}
            The current balance and Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rsj-average-voltage} thus convert the voltage biased quasiparticle spectrum into the current biased response shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:Irsj}(c). This generalized RSJ description retains the nonlinear superconducting spectrum instead of replacing the dissipative branch by a linear resistance. It remains deterministic and neglects capacitive phase inertia and thermal fluctuations, which are considered phenomenologically with the RCSJ model below.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{RCSJ Model}
    \label{subsec:macro:rcsj}
    %=========================================================

        The generalized RSJ model above retains the nonlinear quasiparticle characteristic to describe dc spectroscopy. We now change the modeling goal from reconstructing this spectrum to describing the classical phase dynamics. For this purpose, the RCSJ model approximates the dissipative branch by a linear shunt resistance $R$ and adds a capacitance $C$ in parallel with the Josephson element. The resistance accounts for damping, while the capacitance stores electrostatic energy and introduces phase inertia.

        We assume the Josephson energy far exceeds the charging energy, such that quantum fluctuations of the phase can be neglected. Thermal fluctuations may nevertheless perturb the classical dynamics and are discussed phenomenologically below without introducing an explicit fluctuating current source.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{32mm}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-.75em}
            \import{theory/schema/rcsj}{rcsj-model.pdf_tex}
            \caption{
                Circuit diagram of the RCSJ model. Josephson, resistive, and capacitive current paths are connected in parallel.
            }
            \label{fig:macro:rcsj}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The three parallel branches are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:rcsj}. Combining their current balance with the ac Josephson relation produces a second-order equation for $\phi$. It is mathematically equivalent to the motion of a damped particle in a tilted periodic potential. In this phase-particle analogy, the capacitance sets the effective mass, the linear shunt provides viscous damping, and the Josephson coupling generates the periodic potential. The analogy connects stationary and running phase states to switching, retrapping, phase diffusion, and microwave phase locking \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.

        We first derive the RCSJ phase equation and then use the phase-particle picture to organize these dynamical regimes.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Current Bias}
        %=========================================================

            The phase dynamics follow by summing the currents through the three parallel elements,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{bias} = I_\mathrm{c}\sin(\phi)
                    + \frac{V}{R}
                    + C\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}\,.
            \end{equation}
            This current balance defines the standard RCSJ model \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.


            Using the ac Josephson voltage--phase relation, the current balance becomes
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{bias}
                =
                \underbrace{I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi}_{\text{Josephson}}
                +
                \underbrace{\frac{\hbar}{2eR}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}}_{\text{damping}}
                +
                \underbrace{\frac{\hbar C}{2e}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\phi}{\mathrm{d}t^2}}_{\text{inertia}}\,.
                \label{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr}
            \end{equation}
            The three terms represent the Josephson, dissipative, and capacitive contributions, respectively. In the mechanical analogy developed below, the latter two provide viscous damping and inertia. Together with the bias current, the Josephson contribution gives rise to the tilted-washboard potential, discussed below \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr} is deterministic. The resistance introduces damping, but it does not add a fluctuating force. Thermal current fluctuations from the electromagnetic environment can perturb the phase dynamics, enabling thermally activated switching and phase diffusion. These effects are considered phenomenologically below without introducing a stochastic extension of the RCSJ equation.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Dimensionless RCSJ Model}
        %=========================================================

            A dimensionless form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr} separates inertia, damping, and the nonlinear Josephson term.

            The natural time scale of the junction is set by the Josephson plasma frequency,
            \begin{equation}
                \omega_\mathrm{p}
                    = \sqrt{\frac{2eI_\mathrm{c}}{\hbar C}}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:rcsj-plasma-frequency}
            \end{equation}
            which determines the small-oscillation angular frequency of the phase in a potential minimum. The corresponding ordinary frequency is $\nu_\mathrm{p}=\omega_\mathrm{p}/(2\pi)$. It is distinct from the AC Josephson frequency, which reflects voltage driven phase evolution, and instead characterizes the intrinsic phase resonance in the absence of bias \cite{mccumber_effect_1968,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

            Introducing the rescaled time $t'=\omega_\mathrm{p} t$ and the normalized current $i = I_\mathrm{bias}/I_\mathrm{c}$ gives the compact dimensionless form
            \begin{equation}
                i 
                = \sin\phi
                + \frac{1}{Q}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t'}
                + \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\phi}{\mathrm{d}t'^2}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr-norm}
            \end{equation}
            where the quality factor
            \begin{equation}
                Q = \sqrt{\frac{2eI_\mathrm{c}R^2C}{\hbar}}
            \end{equation}
            quantifies the damping of the phase dynamics \cite{mccumber_effect_1968}.

            The single parameter $Q$ distinguishes underdamped ($Q\sgg 1$) from overdamped ($Q\sll 1$) junctions and sets the stage for the dynamical behavior discussed next.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Tilted Washboard Potential}
        %=========================================================

            The normalized RCSJ equation~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr-norm} has the form of Newton's equation for a unit mass phase particle subject to viscous damping. The phase difference $\phi$ acts as the particle coordinate, while $1/Q$ sets the normalized damping. The bias current $i$ and the Josephson term $\sin\phi$ define the force acting on the phase particle. Identifying this force with the negative gradient of a dimensionless potential and integrating over $\phi$ gives
            \begin{equation}
                -\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\phi}u=i-\sin\phi\,,
                \qquad
                u(\phi)=-\cos\phi-i\phi\,.
                \label{eq:macro:washboard-potential}
            \end{equation}
            The Josephson coupling produces the periodic cosine landscape, while the normalized bias current $i$ produces its linear tilt. The corresponding dimensional phase potential is $U(\phi)=E_\mathrm{J}u(\phi)$. In the untilted case ($i=0$), adjacent minima are separated by a barrier $\Delta U=2E_\mathrm{J}$. Figure~\ref{fig:macro:rcsj-u-phi} shows how this tilted washboard potential evolves with increasing bias current \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

            For $i=0$, the periodic potential has equivalent stable minima. A finite subcritical bias, $0\slt{}i\slt{}1$, tilts the potential and turns them into metastable minima separated by finite barriers. While the phase remains localized in one well, its average velocity and therefore the junction voltage are zero. Small oscillations about a minimum are governed by the plasma-frequency scale introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-plasma-frequency}.

                \captionsetup{format=plain}%

                \caption{
                    Tilted washboard potential of the RCSJ model (Eq.~\ref{eq:macro:washboard-potential}).                 }
                \label{fig:macro:rcsj-u-phi}

            As the bias approaches $i=1$, the barrier in the direction of the tilt decreases and vanishes at the deterministic critical current. For $i\sgt{}1$, no local minima remain and the phase runs continuously, corresponding to a finite-voltage state. Thermal and environmental fluctuations can induce escape already below this threshold and thereby cause premature switching. When the bias is subsequently reduced below $i=1$, local minima reappear. The return from the running state to a localized minimum is called retrapping.

            Not every escape leads to a sustained running state. If the phase is captured in an adjacent minimum, it has undergone a phase slip of approximately $2\pi$. Through the Josephson voltage--phase relation, each slip produces a voltage pulse with time integral $h/(2e)$. Forward and backward slips balance at zero bias, whereas a directional imbalance produces a finite average phase velocity. Repeated slips interrupted by retrapping constitute phase diffusion.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Switching Dynamics and Phase Diffusion}
        %=========================================================

            The intrinsic critical current $I_\mathrm{c}$ is set by the Josephson coupling, while the switching current $I_\mathrm{sw}$ is the observed current at which the junction leaves the localized zero-voltage state and establishes a sustained running state. In the deterministic noise-free limit, the phase remains trapped until the barrier vanishes at $I_\mathrm{bias}=I_\mathrm{c}$. Thermal activation and environmental current noise can instead drive escape at a lower bias. The resulting $I_\mathrm{sw}$ varies between sweeps and depends on temperature, noise, and the bias ramp rate \cite{barone_physics_1982,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

            Escape does not necessarily produce a sustained running state. The phase may be captured in a neighboring minimum after one or several slips. Under finite bias, an imbalance between forward and backward slips generates a small average phase velocity and hence a finite voltage below $I_\mathrm{c}$. This phase diffusion gives the supercurrent branch a finite slope and broadens the transition into the running state. It is not contained in deterministic Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr} and is considered here phenomenologically \cite{duprat_phase_2005,barone_physics_1982,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Retrapping Dynamics}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{wrapfigure}[17]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1em} % fine-tune vertical position
                \thesisplot{theory/macro}{rcsj-iv}
                \caption{
                    Phenomenological \textit{I--V} characteristic of a Josephson junction with prescribed switching current $I_\mathrm{sw}$ and retrapping current $I_\mathrm{rt}$. The finite slope represents phase diffusion, while the separation between $I_\mathrm{sw}$ and $I_\mathrm{rt}$ represents hysteretic switching and retrapping.
                }
                \label{fig:macro:rcsj-iv}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            Once a sustained running state is established, the junction develops a finite average voltage. On a downward current sweep, metastable minima reappear below $I_\mathrm{c}$, but the moving phase is not necessarily captured immediately. The bias at which it is eventually recaptured in a minimum defines the retrapping current $I_\mathrm{rt}$.

            The relation between $I_\mathrm{sw}$ and $I_\mathrm{rt}$ is controlled primarily by the damping. In the overdamped limit, the phase loses energy rapidly, so it retraps as soon as stable minima reappear and switching and retrapping occur at similar currents. In the underdamped limit, the phase retains enough kinetic energy to remain in the running state well below $I_\mathrm{c}$, so it retraps only at a much lower bias, producing pronounced hysteresis in the \textit{I--V} characteristic. In the strongly underdamped limit, the retrapping current approaches zero relative to $I_\mathrm{c}$ \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

            Figure~\ref{fig:macro:rcsj-iv} shows a representative phenomenological \textit{I--V} trace with a finite phase diffusion slope, switching at $I_\mathrm{sw}$, and retrapping at $I_\mathrm{rt}$. These currents are prescribed observables in the plotted characteristic rather than outputs of the deterministic equation. Thermal fluctuations, frequency dependent environmental damping, and heating can shift and broaden both transitions. For the atomic aluminum contact considered here, we use the overdamped limit.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Macroscopic Quantum Dynamics}
        %=========================================================

            The phase particle analogy provides the starting point for a quantum mechanical description of the junction. Quantizing the conservative junction degree of freedom while treating the dissipative environment separately promotes the phase difference and junction charge to conjugate observables. The capacitive energy supplies the kinetic term of the resulting Hamiltonian, while the tilted Josephson potential of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:washboard-potential} supplies its potential term. This Hamiltonian supports discrete energy levels associated with quantized phase oscillations within each metastable minimum. Their observation demonstrated energy quantization of the collective phase difference across a current biased Josephson junction \cite{martinis_energy-level_1985}.

            At sufficiently low temperature, the phase can escape a metastable minimum by tunneling through the potential barrier rather than by thermal activation. In an underdamped junction, this escape can initiate the running state. The process is known as macroscopic quantum tunneling because the tunneling coordinate is the collective phase difference across the junction. Its experimental observation, together with the quantized phase particle levels, established that an electrical circuit can exhibit quantum behavior in a collective macroscopic degree of freedom \cite{devoret_measurements_1985}. These quantum effects lie outside the classical RCSJ treatment used throughout this section.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Shapiro Effect}
    \label{subsec:macro:shapiro}
    %=========================================================

        Under microwave irradiation, the Josephson oscillation can synchronize with the applied drive. The observable manifestation of this locking depends fundamentally on the bias condition. We first consider an ideal voltage biased junction, for which the junction voltage is prescribed and the coherent Josephson contribution appears only at discrete locking voltages. Current biased phase dynamics constitute a separate physical problem and are treated below \cite{shapiro_josephson_1963,barone_physics_1982}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Driven Phase and Ideal Locking}
        %=========================================================

            To obtain the driven phase evolution, we integrate the ac Josephson relation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-voltage-phase} using the harmonic voltage of Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-drive-voltage}, which gives
            \begin{align}
                \phi(t)=\phi_0+2\pi\nu_0t+\alpha_{\nu,2e}\sin(2\pi\nu t)\,, &&
                \nu_0=\frac{2eV_0}{h}\,, &&
                \alpha_{\nu,2e}=\frac{2eA}{h\nu}\,.
                \label{eq:macro:shapiro-phase}
            \end{align}
            The phase therefore contains uniform Josephson evolution and a periodic modulation set by the Cooper pair charge $2e$.

            Inserting Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:shapiro-phase} into the sinusoidal CPR (Eq.~\ref{eq:macro:josephson-current-phase}) and using the Jacobi-Anger expansion (Eq.~\ref{eq:micro:pat-jacobi-anger}) produces 
            \begin{align}
                I_\mathrm{J}(t)
                    =
                    I_\mathrm{c}
                    \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty
                    J_n(\alpha_{\nu,2e})
                    \sin\!\left(
                    \phi_0+2\pi(\nu_\mathrm{J}+n\nu)t
                    \right)\,.
            \end{align}

            A harmonic becomes stationary whenever $\nu_0$ is an integer multiple of $\nu$. The corresponding locking voltages and full current intervals are
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    V_n&=nh\nu/2e\,, \\
                    \Delta I_n &=2I_\mathrm{c}\left|J_n\!\left(\alpha_{\nu,2e}\right)\right|\,.
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:macro:shapiro-iv}
            \end{equation}

            The full interval follows by allowing the stationary phase offset to vary between the two extrema of the sinusoidal CPR. The integer locking voltages are independent of the critical current and damping. The ideal width instead depends on $I_\mathrm{c}$ and on the local microwave voltage amplitude \cite{shapiro_josephson_1963}.

            The Josephson intervals and the PAT background in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:shapiro-iv} arise from different physics. The unsquared Bessel function in $\Delta I_n$ reflects coherent phase evolution and Cooper pair transfer. Quasiparticle PAT is incoherent. Averaging over one drive period removes cross terms between photon channels and produces the squared Bessel weights in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-iv}. The two contributions share only the prescribed junction voltage in this construction, with quasiparticles coupling through charge $e$ and Cooper pairs through charge $2e$.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Current Biased RCSJ Dynamics}
        %=========================================================

            Under current bias, the microwave drive enters the RCSJ current balance. Using the scales introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:rcsj}, we set $t'=\omega_\mathrm{p}t$, $i=I_\mathrm{dc}/I_\mathrm{c}$, $a'=I_\mathrm{ac}/I_\mathrm{c}$, and $\nu'=\nu/\nu_\mathrm{p}$. The driven RCSJ equation is
            \begin{equation}
                i+a'\sin(\nu't')
                =\sin\phi
                +\frac{1}{Q}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t'}
                +\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\phi}{\mathrm{d}t'^2}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:shapiro-rcsj}
            \end{equation}
            The voltage is not prescribed. It follows from the phase velocity through the ac Josephson relation and is obtained by averaging the driven trajectory. The corresponding washboard potential from Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:washboard-potential} is time dependent,
            \begin{equation}
                u(\phi,t')
                =-\cos\phi-\left(i+a'\sin(\nu't')\right)\phi\,.
                \label{eq:macro:shapiro-washboard}
            \end{equation}

            The ac current periodically raises and lowers the barriers of the tilted potential. In an underdamped junction, the capacitive term gives the phase particle sufficient inertia to continue moving after the instantaneous tilt has decreased. Locked trajectories can then compete with trapped and running states. Their stability depends on the initial condition and sweep history, and switching or retrapping can interrupt a locked branch. Underdamped Shapiro steps can therefore be less robust and protocol dependent, which makes them substantially more difficult to calculate as a unique stationary \textit{I--V} characteristic.

            We consequently use the strongly overdamped case for the illustrative current biased response. On the time scale of the driven motion, the capacitive contribution is then negligible compared with the damping. For Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:shapiro-rsj}, we set $C=0$ in the dimensional RCSJ current balance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr}, while retaining the linear resistance. The phase therefore follows first order driven dynamics within the same RCSJ framework. Frequency locking occurs when it advances by $2\pi n$ during one microwave period, producing plateaus in the time averaged voltage at $V_n$. The figure shows the resulting characteristic for different drive amplitudes. It is a dynamical current biased solution, not a reinterpretation of the voltage biased spectrum above.
