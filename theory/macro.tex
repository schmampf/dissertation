    The microscopic section introduced the complex pair potential $\widetilde{\Delta}(\vec r)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:complex-delta}, whose magnitude characterizes the local pairing strength and whose phase describes condensate coherence. When two superconductors are separated by a sufficiently thin weak link, superconducting pair correlations extend into the link from both sides and retain a finite overlap. The two condensates are therefore no longer independent. Since their magnitudes remain fixed by the bulk electrodes, their coherent coupling is governed by the phase difference
    \begin{equation}
        \phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2\,,
        \label{eq:macro:phase-difference}
    \end{equation}
    which becomes the central variable of the macroscopic description and determines the supercurrent through the weak link. Figure~\ref{fig:macro:schematic} depicts the resulting coherent Cooper pair transfer across an SIS junction.

    A Josephson junction denotes such a weak link between two superconductors. It can be formed by an insulating barrier, a metallic constriction, or a short normal region. In the low-transmission limit considered here, the overlap and resulting Josephson coupling are weak, but remain sufficient for coherent Cooper pair transfer. Finite-transmission weak links require the mesoscopic description of Sec.~\ref{sec:meso}. \cite{josephson_possible_1962,likharev_superconducting_1979}

    The following subsections introduce the Josephson relations and the RCSJ model for dissipative phase dynamics.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Josephson Effect}
    \label{subsec:macro:josephson}
    %=========================================================

        The phase difference $\phi$ defined in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:phase-difference} is the collective variable that describes the coherent coupling between the two superconductors. A static phase difference permits a supercurrent without a voltage drop, while its temporal evolution is linked directly to the voltage across the weak link. These two consequences are expressed by the Josephson relations and connect condensate coherence to the measurable current and voltage \cite{josephson_possible_1962}.

        \subsubsection*{Ac Josephson Relation}

            The ac Josephson relation connects the voltage across the weak link directly to the temporal evolution of the phase difference,
            \begin{align}
                \frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{2e}{\hbar}\,V(t)\,.
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-voltage-phase}
            \end{align}
            It follows from the condensate charge $2e$ and is independent of the detailed structure and transmission of the junction. The ac Josephson relation therefore applies to all junction regimes considered in this chapter. A voltage drives the phase difference rather than fixing the current through the weak link \cite{josephson_possible_1962}.

            For a constant voltage $V(t)=V_0$, the phase evolves linearly as
            \begin{align}
                \phi(t) = \phi_0 + 2\pi \nu_0t\,, &&
                \nu_0 = \frac{2e}{h}\,V_0\,,
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-phase-evolution}
            \end{align}
            where $\phi_0$ is the phase difference at $t=0$ and $\nu_0$ is the Josephson frequency. Thus, a dc voltage produces a phase that advances periodically modulo $2\pi$. An oscillating current follows only after this phase evolution is combined with the current-phase relation introduced below.

        \subsubsection*{Dc Josephson Relation}

            In the absence of a voltage, Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-voltage-phase} gives a stationary phase difference. The coherent coupling between the condensates can then support a time independent supercurrent. To leading order in the weak coupling, its dependence on phase is described by the sinusoidal current-phase relation (CPR)
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{J}(\phi) = I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi\,,
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-current-phase}
            \end{equation}
            where $I_\mathrm{c}$ is the maximum supercurrent supported by the junction. Figure~\ref{fig:macro:josephson}(a) shows this sinusoidal phase dependence. A finite phase difference can therefore drive a dissipationless current without a voltage drop. This is the dc Josephson effect \cite{josephson_possible_1962}.

            Under a constant finite voltage, inserting the phase evolution from Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-phase-evolution} into the CPR gives
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{J}(t)=I_\mathrm{c}\sin\!\left(\phi_0+2\pi\nu_0t\right)\,.
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-current-evolution}
            \end{equation}
            The supercurrent then oscillates at the Josephson frequency $\nu_0$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:josephson}(b). The dc and ac Josephson effects are therefore two bias conditions of the same phase dynamics. Under microwave irradiation, synchronization of this intrinsic oscillation with the external drive produces voltage plateaus. This effect is known as the inverse ac Josephson effect or as Shapiro steps and is discussed below.

        \subsubsection*{Ambegaokar--Baratoff Relation}

            The Josephson relations determine the phase dependence and temporal evolution of the supercurrent, but do not fix its magnitude. For an SIS junction formed by identical BCS superconductors, the Ambegaokar-Baratoff (AB) theory derives this magnitude from coherent pair tunneling through the barrier. The resulting critical current is
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{c}(T) = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{G_\mathrm{N}\Delta(T)}{e}\,
                \tanh\!\left(\frac{\Delta(T)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right),
                \label{eq:macro:critical-current}
            \end{equation}
            where $G_\mathrm{N}$ is the normal-state conductance of the junction. The temperature dependence combines the reduction of the superconducting gap from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:gap-temperature} with the thermal occupation factor, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:josephson}(c). At zero temperature, Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:critical-current} reduces to $I_\mathrm{c}=\pi G_\mathrm{N}\Delta_0/(2e)$ \cite{ambegaokar_tunneling_1963}.

            Together with the sinusoidal current-phase relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-current-phase}, the AB result determines the complete equilibrium supercurrent for the junction considered here.

        \subsubsection*{Josephson Coupling Energy}

            The strength of the coherent phase coupling is quantified by the Josephson energy
            \begin{equation}
                E_\mathrm{J}=\frac{\hbar I_\mathrm{c}}{2e}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:josephson-energy}
            \end{equation}
            which sets the energetic stiffness of the phase and the robustness of coherent Cooper pair transfer. Large $E_\mathrm{J}$ favors a well-defined phase difference, while competing fluctuations weaken this phase locking. The influence of thermal fluctuations is included in the RCSJ model introduced below. The charge dominated regime, in which the charging energy competes with or exceeds $E_\mathrm{J}$, is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:stochastic} \cite{tinkham_introduction_2004,grabert_single_2013}.

        \subsubsection*{Dc Josephson Spectroscopy}

            We first consider dc spectroscopy of an ideal voltage biased SIS Josephson junction. At finite voltage, the phase evolves at the Josephson frequency according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-phase-evolution}. The supercurrent consequently oscillates and averages to zero over one period, so the measured dc current follows the SIS quasiparticle characteristic derived in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel-sis}. At $V=0$, the phase remains at its initial value $\phi_0$ and the junction can support the finite current $I_\mathrm{J}=I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi_0$. In this idealized limit, the voltage fixes only the phase velocity, while the stationary value $\phi_0$ is set by the initial condition or the external circuit. Figures~\ref{fig:macro:Irsj}(a) and (b) show the resulting voltage biased spectrum and its dependence on the initial phase.

            Under current bias, the voltage is no longer fixed externally and instead becomes the dynamical response of the junction. We describe this response with a generalized resistively shunted junction (RSJ) model. Coherent Cooper pair transfer and dissipative quasiparticle tunneling occur in parallel and experience the same time dependent voltage, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:rsj}. In contrast to the conventional linear shunt $V/R$, the generalized model retains the microscopic nonlinear quasiparticle characteristic $I_\mathrm{qp}(V)$.

                \captionsetup{format=plain}%

                % \vspace{-0.5em}

                    Parallel-channel representation of the generalized RSJ model. The bias current divides between the coherent Josephson current $I_\mathrm{J}$ and the dissipative quasiparticle current $I_\mathrm{qp}$.
                }
                \label{fig:macro:rsj}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            The applied current therefore divides according to
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{bias}
                = I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi(t)
                + I_\mathrm{qp}\!\left(V(t)\right)\,.
                \label{eq:macro:rsj-current-balance}
            \end{equation}
            We denote the inverse of the BCS quasiparticle characteristic by $V_\mathrm{qp}(I)$. This construction assumes that the quasiparticle branch follows the instantaneous voltage and that its \textit{I--V} characteristic can be inverted on the branch considered. Together with the ac Josephson relation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rsj-current-balance} determines the time evolution of the phase and voltage. For $|I_\mathrm{bias}|\leq I_\mathrm{c}$, a stationary phase can carry the applied current and the voltage remains zero. Above this range, the phase runs through successive $2\pi$ periods. Its velocity changes during each rotation because the current available for quasiparticle tunneling depends on $\phi$.
            
            Consequently, the phase does not sample all values for equal durations. Although the unweighted phase average of $\sin\phi$ vanishes over one rotation, its time average need not vanish under current bias. This differs from ideal voltage bias, where the phase advances uniformly and the supercurrent averages to zero. The measured dc voltage is the time average over the nonuniform phase motion. For one complete phase rotation, it is
            \begin{equation}
                \overline{V}
                = 2\pi\,\left(
                    \displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}
                    \frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{
                        V_\mathrm{qp}\!\left(I_\mathrm{bias}-I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi\right)
                    }
                \right)^{-1}\,.
                \label{eq:macro:rsj-average-voltage}
            \end{equation}
            The current balance and Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rsj-average-voltage} thus convert the voltage biased quasiparticle spectrum into the current biased response shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:Irsj}(c). This generalized RSJ description retains the nonlinear superconducting spectrum instead of replacing the dissipative branch by a linear resistance. It remains deterministic and neglects capacitive phase inertia and thermal fluctuations, which are introduced with the RCSJ model below.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{RCSJ Model}
    \label{subsec:macro:rcsj}
    %=========================================================

        The generalized RSJ model above retains the nonlinear quasiparticle characteristic to describe dc spectroscopy. We now change the modeling goal from reconstructing this spectrum to describing the classical phase dynamics. For this purpose, the resistively and capacitively shunted junction (RCSJ) model approximates the dissipative branch by a linear shunt resistance $R$ and adds a capacitance $C$ in parallel with the Josephson element. The resistance accounts for damping, while the capacitance stores electrostatic energy and introduces phase inertia.

        We assume $E_\mathrm{J}\gg E_\mathrm{c}$, such that quantum fluctuations of the phase can be neglected. Thermal fluctuations may nevertheless perturb the classical dynamics and are included later through a fluctuating current source.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{35mm}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \import{theory/schema/rcsj}{rcsj-model.pdf_tex}
            \caption{
                Parallel Josephson, resistive, and capacitive branches of the RCSJ model.
            }
            \label{fig:macro:rcsj}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The three parallel branches are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:macro:rcsj}. Combining their current balance with the ac Josephson relation produces a second-order equation for $\phi$. It is mathematically equivalent to the motion of a damped particle in a tilted periodic potential. In this phase-particle analogy, the capacitance sets the effective mass, the linear shunt provides viscous damping, and the Josephson coupling generates the periodic potential. The analogy connects stationary and running phase states to switching, retrapping, phase diffusion, and microwave phase locking \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.

        We first derive the RCSJ phase equation and then use the phase-particle picture to organize these dynamical regimes.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Current Bias}
        %=========================================================

            The phase dynamics follow by summing the currents through the three parallel elements,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{bias} = I_\mathrm{c}\sin(\phi)
                    + \frac{V}{R}
                    + C\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t}\,.
            \end{equation}
            This current balance defines the standard RCSJ model \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.


            Using the ac Josephson voltage-phase relation, the current balance becomes
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{bias}
                =
                \underbrace{I_\mathrm{c}\sin\phi}_{\text{Josephson}}
                +
                \underbrace{\frac{\hbar}{2eR}\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}}_{\text{damping}}
                +
                \underbrace{\frac{\hbar C}{2e}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2\phi}{\mathrm{d}t^2}}_{\text{inertia}}\,.
                \label{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr}
            \end{equation}
            The three terms represent the Josephson, dissipative, and capacitive contributions, respectively. In the mechanical analogy developed below, the latter two provide viscous damping and inertia. Together with the bias current, the Josephson contribution gives rise to the tilted-washboard potential, discussed below \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr} is deterministic. The resistance introduces damping, but it does not add a fluctuating force. Thermal current fluctuations from the electromagnetic environment can perturb the phase dynamics, enabling thermally activated switching and phase diffusion. These effects are considered phenomenologically below without introducing a stochastic extension of the RCSJ equation.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Dimensionless RCSJ Model}
        %=========================================================

            A dimensionless form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr} separates inertia, damping, and the nonlinear Josephson term.

            The natural time scale of the junction is set by the Josephson plasma frequency,
            \begin{equation}
                \omega_\mathrm{p}
                    = \sqrt{\frac{2eI_\mathrm{c}}{\hbar C}}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:rcsj-plasma-frequency}
            \end{equation}
            which determines the small-oscillation angular frequency of the phase in a potential minimum. The corresponding ordinary frequency is $\nu_\mathrm{p}=\omega_\mathrm{p}/(2\pi)$. It is distinct from the AC Josephson frequency, which reflects voltage-driven phase evolution, and instead characterizes the intrinsic phase resonance in the absence of bias \cite{mccumber_effect_1968,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

            Introducing the rescaled time $t'=\omega_\mathrm{p} t$ and the normalized current $i = I_\mathrm{bias}/I_\mathrm{c}$ gives the compact dimensionless form
            \begin{equation}
                i - \sin\phi
                = \frac{1}{Q}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t'}
                + \frac{\mathrm{d}^2\phi}{\mathrm{d}t'^2}\,,
                \label{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr-norm}
            \end{equation}
            where the quality factor
            \begin{equation}
                Q = \sqrt{\frac{2eI_\mathrm{c}R^2C}{\hbar}}
            \end{equation}
            quantifies the damping of the phase dynamics \cite{mccumber_effect_1968}.

            The single parameter $Q$ distinguishes underdamped ($Q\gg 1$) from overdamped ($Q\ll 1$) junctions and sets the stage for the dynamical behavior discussed next.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Tilted Washboard Potential}
        %=========================================================

            The normalized RCSJ equation~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr-norm} has the form of Newton's equation for a particle moving in a potential and subject to viscous damping. After division by the particle mass, the mechanical equation of motion can be written as
            \begin{equation}
                - \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}x}
                = \frac{\zeta}{m}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t'}
                + \frac{\mathrm{d}^2x}{\mathrm{d}t'^2}\,, \quad
                u = \frac{U}{m}\,.
                \label{eq:macro:newtonian-eom}
            \end{equation}
            Here, $x$ is the particle coordinate, $U(x)$ its mechanical potential energy, $m$ its mass, and $\zeta$ the viscous damping coefficient.

            Comparison with Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr-norm} identifies the phase difference as the particle coordinate and the inverse quality factor as the normalized damping coefficient,
            \begin{align}
                x \longleftrightarrow \phi\,,&&
                U(x)\longleftrightarrow U(\phi)\,, &&
                \frac{\zeta}{m}\longleftrightarrow\frac{1}{Q}\,.
            \end{align}

            The remaining terms in the normalized RCSJ equation determine the force acting on this phase particle. Identifying this force with the negative gradient of the potential and integrating over $\phi$ gives
            \begin{equation}
                -\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\phi}u=i-\sin\phi\,,
                \qquad
                u(\phi)=-\cos\phi-i\phi\,.
                \label{eq:macro:washboard-potential}
            \end{equation}
            The Josephson coupling produces the periodic cosine landscape, while the normalized bias current $i$ produces its linear tilt. The corresponding dimensional phase potential is $U(\phi)=E_\mathrm{J}u(\phi)$. In the untilted case ($i=0$), adjacent minima are separated by a barrier $\Delta U=2E_\mathrm{J}$. Figure~\ref{fig:macro:rcsj-u-phi} shows how this tilted washboard potential evolves with increasing bias current \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering

                \caption{
                    Tilted washboard potential of the RCSJ model (Eq.~\ref{eq:macro:washboard-potential}).                 }
                \label{fig:macro:rcsj-u-phi}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            For $i=0$, the periodic potential has equivalent stable minima. A finite subcritical bias, $0<i<1$, tilts the potential and turns them into metastable minima separated by finite barriers. While the phase remains localized in one well, its average velocity and therefore the junction voltage are zero. Small oscillations about a minimum are governed by the plasma-frequency scale introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-plasma-frequency}.

            As the bias approaches $i=1$, the barrier in the direction of the tilt decreases and vanishes at the deterministic critical current. For $i>1$, no local minima remain and the phase runs continuously, corresponding to a finite-voltage state. Thermal and environmental fluctuations can induce escape already below this threshold and thereby cause premature switching. When the bias is subsequently reduced below $i=1$, local minima reappear. The return from the running state to a localized minimum is called retrapping.

            Not every escape leads to a sustained running state. If the phase is captured in an adjacent minimum, it has undergone a phase slip of approximately $2\pi$. Through the Josephson voltage--phase relation, each slip produces a voltage pulse with time integral $h/(2e)$. Forward and backward slips balance at zero bias, whereas a directional imbalance produces a finite average phase velocity. Repeated slips interrupted by retrapping constitute phase diffusion.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Macroscopic Quantum Dynamics}
        %=========================================================

            The phase-particle analogy provides the starting point for a quantum-mechanical description of the junction. Upon quantization, the phase difference and junction charge become conjugate observables. The capacitance supplies the kinetic-energy term, while the tilted washboard potential supplies the potential energy. The resulting Hamiltonian supports discrete phase states within each metastable minimum.The observation of these levels demonstrated energy quantization of the collective phase difference across a current-biased Josephson junction \cite{martinis_energy-level_1985}.

            Escape from a metastable minimum need not occur by thermal activation. At sufficiently low temperature, the phase can tunnel through the potential barrier and enter the running state. This process is known as macroscopic quantum tunneling because the tunneling coordinate is the collective phase difference across the junction. Its experimental observation, together with the quantized phase-particle levels, established that an electrical circuit can exhibit quantum behavior in a collective macroscopic degree of freedom \cite{devoret_measurements_1985}. These quantum effects lie outside the classical RCSJ treatment used below.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Switching Dynamics and Phase Diffusion}
        %=========================================================

            The switching current $I_\mathrm{SW}$ is the observed current at which a Josephson junction leaves the zero-voltage state and enters the resistive, running-phase regime. Unlike the intrinsic critical current $I_\mathrm{c}$, which is set by the Josephson coupling energy, the measured switching current can vary between sweeps. Its distribution is determined by the decreasing barrier height of the tilted washboard potential and by fluctuations that drive premature escape. Thermal activation, environmental current noise, and the bias ramp rate can reduce $I_\mathrm{SW}$ below $I_\mathrm{c}$. Only in the ideal, noise-free limit does $I_\mathrm{SW}$ approach $I_\mathrm{c}$. \cite{barone_physics_1982,tinkham_introduction_2004}

            Thermal and noise-induced phase fluctuations can give the supercurrent branch a finite slope. Stochastic phase slips produce a finite average phase velocity even below the intrinsic critical current and therefore generate a small nonzero voltage. This phase-diffusion regime is not contained in deterministic Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:rcsj-cpr}. It requires a Langevin source or an equivalent environmental model. Phenomenologically, the ideal vertical supercurrent step is replaced by a broadened low-voltage region whose slope depends on temperature, current noise, and environmental damping. \cite{duprat_phase_2005,barone_physics_1982,tinkham_introduction_2004}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Retrapping Dynamics}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{wrapfigure}[17]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1em} % fine-tune vertical position
                \import{theory/macro}{rcsj-iv.pgf}
                \caption{
                    Phenomenological \textit{I--V} characteristic of a Josephson junction with prescribed switching current $I_\mathrm{SW}$ and retrapping current $I_\mathrm{R}$. The finite slope represents phase diffusion, while the separation between $I_\mathrm{SW}$ and $I_\mathrm{R}$ represents hysteretic switching and retrapping.
                }
                \label{fig:macro:rcsj-iv}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            After the phase escapes into the running state, the junction develops a finite average voltage and the phase accelerates down the tilted washboard potential. When the bias current is reduced, the junction does not immediately return to the zero-voltage state. The phase continues to move until enough kinetic energy has dissipated for it to fall back into a metastable minimum. The return current is the retrapping current $I_\mathrm{R}$.

            In contrast to the switching current, which is set by escape from a minimum, the retrapping current reflects how efficiently damping removes kinetic energy accumulated during the running state. Thermal and current noise smooth this transition, producing a broadened return to the superconducting branch rather than a sharp jump \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968,tinkham_introduction_2004}.

            Figure~\ref{fig:macro:rcsj-iv} shows a representative phenomenological \textit{I--V} trace. It highlights the finite slope of the supercurrent branch, the switching transition at $I_\mathrm{SW}$, and the return at $I_\mathrm{R}$. These currents are prescribed observables in the plotted characteristic rather than outputs of the deterministic equation above.

            In the ideal overdamped limit ($Q\ll 1$), the phase has essentially no inertia and relocks as soon as stable minima reappear. Switching and retrapping therefore occur at similar currents in the deterministic model. Experimentally, stochastic fluctuations, frequency-dependent environmental damping, or heating can still produce distinct observed values of $I_\mathrm{SW}$ and $I_\mathrm{R}$. The overdamped regime is the relevant limit for the atomic aluminum contact studied in this work.

            In the underdamped limit ($Q\gg 1$), the phase retains significant kinetic energy after escape and cannot relock until the tilt is nearly removed. The retrapping current therefore approaches zero, giving rise to strong hysteresis in the \textit{I--V} characteristic \cite{stewart_currentvoltage_1968,mccumber_effect_1968}.
