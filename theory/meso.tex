%=========================================================
\section{Mesoscopic Description}
\label{sec:meso}
%=========================================================

    The microscopic section treated quasiparticle tunneling in the low-transmission limit, while the macroscopic section described phase dynamics of weakly coupled Josephson junctions. Atomic contacts require a mesoscopic description because their conductance is carried by a small number of phase-coherent channels with finite transmission. In this regime, the normal-state transmission eigenvalues, the mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$, determine both the equilibrium spectrum and nonlinear subgap transport.

    Finite transmission makes electron and hole degrees of freedom inseparable. Subgap current is no longer described only by independent quasiparticle tunneling or by a lumped Josephson element, but by coherent Andreev conversion at superconducting interfaces. We start from the Bogoliubov--de~Gennes (BdG) formalism, which provides the electron--hole amplitudes used throughout this section. We then quantify a single NS interface with the BTK model, extend the scattering picture to Andreev bound states in short SNS contacts, and finally treat finite-bias multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) and its charge-resolved response.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Mesoscopic Transport Regimes}
    \label{subsec:quasi:meso}
    %=========================================================

        Mesoscopic transport occurs when the conductor size $L$ becomes comparable to microscopic transport length scales. Finite-size effects and quantum interference can then require a description beyond the bulk Drude picture. The relevant hierarchy involves the elastic mean free path $\ell_\mathrm{qp}$, the phase-coherence length $\ell_\phi$, and the thermal length $\ell_T$. This hierarchy determines the applicable transport description.

        The phase-coherence length $\ell_\phi$ is set by inelastic and dephasing processes such as electron--electron and electron--phonon scattering. It quantifies how far a quasiparticle retains a well-defined quantum phase. At finite temperature, thermal averaging further limits observable interference on the scale $\ell_T$.

        In macroscopic conductors, where $L \gg \ell_\mathrm{qp}$ and $L \gg \ell_\phi$, quasiparticles scatter many times and lose phase coherence before crossing the sample. Transport is then described by bulk drift diffusion. When $L \lesssim \ell_\phi$, phase coherence extends across the conductor and quantum interference becomes observable. The mean free path distinguishes ballistic transport for $L \ll \ell_\mathrm{qp}$ from phase-coherent diffusive transport for $\ell_\mathrm{qp} \ll L \lesssim \ell_\phi$. Figure~\ref{fig:quasi:meso} summarizes this hierarchy. \cite{gross_festkorperphysik_2014,beenakker_random-matrix_1997}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Ballistic Transport and Scattering Formalism}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1em} % fine-tune vertical position
                \import{theory/schema/scattering}{scattering-matrix.pdf_tex}
                \vspace{-.25em} % fine-tune vertical position
                \caption{
                    Sketch of a two-terminal scatterer connecting ideal leads. Incoming and outgoing mode amplitudes $\vec{A}$, $\vec{B}$ are related by the scattering matrix $\mat{S}$.
                }
                \label{fig:quasi:scatter}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            In the ballistic regime, the constriction is shorter than the elastic mean free path, $L \ll \ell_\mathrm{qp}$, so quasiparticles traverse the contact without momentum-randomizing scattering. If $L \lesssim \ell_\phi$, transport is phase coherent across the entire constriction. A characteristic ballistic coherence length may be estimated from the Fermi velocity and a dephasing time $t_\phi$,
            \begin{equation}
                \ell_\phi = v_\mathrm{F} \, t_\phi\,.
            \end{equation}
            The numerical prefactor depends on the definition of the dephasing time.

            Ballistic transport is naturally formulated as an elastic scattering problem. A finite region connects ideal leads in which electronic modes propagate without dissipation. We denote the incoming amplitudes in the left and right leads by $A_1$ and $A_2$, and the outgoing amplitudes by $B_1$ and $B_2$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:scatter}.

            Linear scattering then gives
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{pmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{pmatrix} = \mat{S}\,\begin{pmatrix} A_1 \\ A_2 \end{pmatrix}\,.
            \end{equation}

            In the multi-mode case, $A_{1,2}$ and $B_{1,2}$ become vectors collecting all propagating modes in the corresponding lead. The scattering matrix decomposes into reflection blocks $\mat{r}$ and $\mat{r}'$ and transmission blocks $\mat{t}$ and $\mat{t}'$,
            \begin{equation}
                \mat{S}
                = \begin{pmatrix} \mat{r} & \mat{t}' \\ \mat{t} & \mat{r}' \end{pmatrix}\,.
            \end{equation}
            For purely elastic normal-state scattering, $\mat{S}$ is unitary because probability current is conserved.

            The central quantities governing transport are then the transmission eigenvalues
            \begin{equation}
                \{\tau_i\} = \operatorname{eig}\!\left(\mat{t}^\dagger\,\mat{t}\right)\,,\quad
                \tau_i\in[0,1]\,.
            \end{equation}
            Choosing a basis of transmission eigenchannels diagonalizes $\mat{t}^\dagger\mat{t}$. This basis is unique up to unitary rotations within degenerate subspaces.

            In linear response, the normal-state conductance is then given by the Landauer formula,
            \begin{equation}
                G_\mathrm{N} = G_0 \sum_{i=1}^N \tau_i\,,
                \quad
                G_0 = \frac{2e^2}{h}\,,
            \end{equation}
            where $\tau_i$ are the transmission eigenvalues and $G_0$ is the conductance quantum\footnote{
                Throughout this thesis the conductance quantum is defined as $G_0 \equiv 2e^2/h = 77.48\,\mu\mathrm{S}$. This value includes spin degeneracy and corresponds to the conductance of a fully transmitting normal-state channel. The corresponding resistance quantum is $R_0 = h/(2e^2) = 12.9\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$.
            } \cite{landauer_electrical_1970,buttiker_four-terminal_1986}.

            The normal-state conductance measures only the sum of the transmission eigenvalues. It does not determine their individual values. The set $\{\tau_i\}$, referred to throughout this thesis as the mesoscopic pincode, becomes observable through the channel-dependent superconducting \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} characteristics discussed later. This distinction connects the Landauer formula to experimental channel extraction. \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,beenakker_random-matrix_1997}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Atomic-Scale Contacts}
        %=========================================================

            A split-gate quantum point contact (QPC) in a high-mobility two-dimensional electron gas provides the clean reference for the channel picture. Its short ballistic constriction is tuned electrostatically, and the conductance increases in quantized steps as transverse modes open. In the Landauer picture, each nearly open mode contributes about one conductance quantum $G_0$. The resulting staircase $G(V_\mathrm{G})$ is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:atomic-contact}(a) \cite{van_wees_quantized_1988,wharam_one-dimensional_1988}.

            Semiconductor QPCs provide a clean and tunable example of conductance quantization, but this work focuses on metallic atomic contacts. In these contacts, atomic-scale structure and chemistry determine the number, symmetry, and transparency of the transmission channels.

            Metallic atomic contacts realize ballistic, phase-coherent transport. The constriction consists of one or a few atoms, with a length of order an interatomic spacing. The elastic mean free path in aluminum depends on disorder, thickness, and microstructure. For example, normal-state resistivity measurements on 30--50-nm-thick aluminum films give $\ell_\mathrm{qp}=1.8$--$5.0\,\mathrm{nm}$. These measured film values are sample-specific, but they still exceed the length of an atomic constriction \cite{steinberg_quasi-particle_2008}. At low temperature, the phase-coherence length is also much larger than the constriction, so atomic contacts typically satisfy $L \ll \ell_\mathrm{qp}$ and $L \ll \ell_\phi$. Experimentally, such contacts are tracked by recording conductance while mechanically separating the electrodes, producing an opening trace as in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:atomic-contact}(b) \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,agrait_quantum_2003}.

            The transmission eigenchannels of an atomic contact are set by the valence orbitals at the narrowest part of the constriction and by their hybridization with the electrodes. For aluminum, whose conduction states have predominantly $sp$ character, a typical single-atom contact has three dominant transport channels: one mainly $sp_z$-like channel and two transverse $p$-like channels. The exact number and transmission values depend on geometry and disorder, and weaker channels may appear \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,scheer_signature_1998}. Conductance histograms provide a statistical view, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:atomic-contact}(c), where peaks reflect preferred atomic configurations rather than proving conductance quantization \cite{yanson_histograms_1997,agrait_quantum_2003}.

            Experimentally, the mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$ can be extracted from superconducting transport measurements using multiple Andreev reflection and related techniques. These transmission eigenvalues serve as input for the finite-transmission description of superconducting transport in the following sections. \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,scheer_signature_1998}

            Together, opening traces and conductance histograms show how atomic configurations select reproducible conductance ranges. They motivate the channel description used throughout this thesis, while superconducting subgap spectroscopy supplies the additional information required to resolve the individual $\tau_i$.

    In summary, comparing $L$ with $\ell_\mathrm{qp}$, $\ell_\phi$, and $\ell_T$ determines whether transport is described by local bulk conductivity or by phase-coherent scattering. In the coherent regime, the normal-state conductance determines only $\sum_i\tau_i$. Channel-resolved superconducting spectroscopy supplies the additional information needed to determine the individual transmission eigenvalues. Section~\ref{sec:micro} now introduces superconducting pairing, the quasiparticle spectrum, and tunnel transport.



    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Andreev Reflection}
    \label{subsec:meso:ar}
    %=========================================================

        At a normal--superconductor (NS) interface, no propagating quasiparticle states are available in the superconducting continuum for $|E|<|\Delta|$. Ordinary single-particle transmission is therefore forbidden below the gap. Instead, an incident electron can be reflected as a hole while a charge $2e$ is transferred into the condensate. This coherent electron--hole conversion is Andreev reflection, distinct from independent Cooper-pair tunneling between two superconducting electrodes \cite{andreev_thermal_1964}.

        Figure~\ref{fig:meso:btk}(a) shows single-particle transmission into the superconducting quasiparticle continuum above the gap, while Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:btk}(b) shows subgap Andreev reflection and charge transfer into the condensate. These panels are energy-space schematics, not real-space trajectories. Normal reflection can coexist with Andreev reflection at a nonideal interface. Andreev reflection dominates subgap transport only when the interface transparency is sufficiently large.

        Andreev reflection is the microscopic building block for superconducting transport in mesoscopic weak links. Repeated conversion between two superconducting electrodes produces discrete Andreev bound states (ABS) in equilibrium and multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) under finite bias. For atomic-scale contacts, these processes are resolved by channels with transmissions $\tau_i$. The Blonder--Tinkham--Klapwijk (BTK) model introduced next quantifies the competition between normal reflection, quasiparticle transmission, and Andreev reflection at a single NS interface \cite{blonder_transition_1982,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            The Blonder--Tinkham--Klapwijk (BTK) model provides a quantitative scattering description of an NS interface with arbitrary transparency $\tau$. It yields the energy-resolved probabilities for Andreev reflection ($A$), normal reflection ($B$), and total quasiparticle transmission into the superconductor ($C$),
            \begin{equation}
                A(E) + B(E) + C(E) = 1\,.
            \end{equation}
            This probability balance follows from current conservation in the BTK scattering problem \cite{blonder_transition_1982}.

            To incorporate finite quasiparticle lifetimes phenomenologically, we follow Pleceník \textit{et al.} and replace the quasiparticle energy by the complex argument $E \rightarrow |E| + \ima\gamma$ \cite{plecenik_finite-quasiparticle-lifetime_1994}.

            Using the normalization factor $d$, the Andreev and normal reflection amplitudes ($a$, $b$) acquire the compact form,
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    A(E) &= aa^*\,,\quad
                    a= u_0v_0/d\,,\quad
                    d = \left(u^2_0-(1-\tau)v_0^2\middle)\right/\tau\,,\\
                    B(E) &= bb^*\,,\quad
                    b= -\left(u_0^2-v_0^2\right)\left( (1-\tau) + \ima \sqrt{\tau(1-\tau)} \middle)\,\right/\,\tau d\,,
                \end{aligned}
            \end{equation}
            with $u_0$ and $v_0$ as coherence factors, given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:coherence-factors} \cite{blonder_transition_1982,plecenik_finite-quasiparticle-lifetime_1994}.

            The probabilities determine how the NS current separates into single-particle and Andreev contributions. It is useful to collect these contributions into the energy-resolved BTK transport kernels
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    \rho_\mathrm{1e}(E) &= 1 - A(E) - B(E)\,,\\
                    \rho_\mathrm{2e}(E) &= 2 \, A(E) \,,
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:meso:btk-dos}
            \end{equation}
            The first kernel describes transmission into quasiparticle states, while the second includes the factor of two charge transferred by Andreev reflection. For ideal subgap transport at $\gamma=0$, the quasiparticle-transmission kernel vanishes because $C\to0$. For finite lifetime broadening, $\rho_\mathrm{1e}$ can acquire subgap weight. These functions resemble densities of states, but they are transport kernels entering the current integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:btk-iv}.

            The current through an NS interface then follows from these kernels,
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    I_\mathrm{1e}(V) &= \frac{G_0}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \rho_\mathrm{1e}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,,\\
                    I_\mathrm{2e}(V) &= \frac{G_0}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \rho_\mathrm{2e}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,,\\
                    I_\mathrm{NS}(V) &= I_\mathrm{1e}(V) + I_\mathrm{2e}(V)\,.
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:meso:btk-iv}
            \end{equation}
            The prefactor uses $G_0=2e^2/h$, so spin degeneracy is included in the conductance quantum. Increasing the transparency enhances the weight of Andreev processes, leading to a pronounced subgap conductance and a gradual reduction of the coherence-peak height. In the tunneling limit ($\tau \ll 1$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:btk-iv} reduces to the conventional quasiparticle-tunneling expression, while for $\tau \approx 1$ the transport approaches the Andreev limit, where charge is transferred predominantly in units of $2e$ \cite{blonder_transition_1982}.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:btk} summarizes the BTK description across the transparency range. Panels (c) and (d) show the transport kernels $\rho_{\mathrm{1e}}(E)$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{2e}}(E)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:btk-dos}. In the tunneling regime ($\tau\ll 1$), the response is dominated by the single-particle channel and shows coherence peaks at $|E|\approx\Delta$. With increasing transparency, spectral weight transfers to the Andreev channel, enhancing subgap conductance and reducing the peak height in \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV}.

        The BTK model describes one NS interface. When the normal region is bounded by two superconducting electrodes, successive Andreev conversions become phase coherent and depend on the superconducting phase difference. The resulting closed electron--hole trajectories form discrete subgap states controlled by the channel transparencies $\tau_i$. We therefore turn from interface probabilities to the spectrum and transport carried by Andreev bound states.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Andreev Bound States}
    \label{subsec:meso:abs}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sns-abs.pdf_tex}
            \caption{
                Andreev bound-state formation in an SNS weak link.
                }
            \label{fig:meso:abs-schematics}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        Andreev reflection becomes phase-coherent and spectrally quantized when two superconductors are connected by a mesoscopic weak link. In an SNS geometry, a subgap quasiparticle ($|E|<|\Delta|$) cannot escape into the superconducting continua and instead undergoes successive Andreev conversions at both interfaces, alternating between electron- and hole-like character as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:abs-schematics}. One cycle acquires the phase difference
        \begin{equation}
            \phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2\,.
        \end{equation}
        The resulting constructive-interference condition quantizes the motion into discrete subgap eigenstates localized around the junction, known as Andreev bound states (ABS) \cite{andreev_thermal_1964,ishii_josephson_1970,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

        These ABS form the microscopic origin of the dc Josephson effect in short mesoscopic junctions. Their phase-dependent energies $E_n(\phi)$ determine the equilibrium supercurrent through their occupation \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991, beenakker_quantum_1992}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Andreev Approximation and Limit}
        %=========================================================

            A transparent route from BdG to ABS is to treat Andreev reflection as an energy-dependent boundary condition at the superconducting leads, while representing the weak link by its normal-state scattering properties.

            In the Andreev approximation, $|E|,|\Delta|\ll E_\mathrm{F}$, only quasiparticles in a narrow shell around the Fermi surface contribute. For an isotropic parabolic band, the dispersion can be linearized in the radial direction,
            \begin{equation}
                \varepsilon(\vec{k})-\mu \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{m}\,\vec{k}_\mathrm{F}\cdot\left(\vec{k}-\vec{k}_\mathrm{F}\right)\,,
            \end{equation}
            so electron-like and hole-like components have nearly equal momentum magnitudes. For an excitation at energy $E$, the corresponding wave numbers satisfy
            \begin{equation}
                \delta k = \frac{k_\mathrm{e} - k_\mathrm{h}}{2} \approx \frac{k_\mathrm{F}}{2}\frac{E}{E_\mathrm{F}}\,,
            \end{equation}
            implying that the Andreev-reflected hole retraces the incoming trajectory up to corrections of order $E/E_\mathrm{F}$ \cite{andreev_thermal_1964,de_gennes_superconductivity_1966}.

            In the Andreev limit of a clean, specular, and highly transparent NS interface with negligible normal reflection, subgap conversion occurs with approximately unit probability. The corresponding electron-to-hole reflection amplitude can then be written as a pure phase factor,
            \begin{equation}
                r_\mathrm{A}(E) = e^{-\ima\phi_\mathrm{A}(E)}\,e^{-\ima\phi}\,,\qquad
                \phi_\mathrm{A}(E)\equiv\arccos\!\left(E/|\Delta|\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $\phi_\mathrm{A}(E)$ is the energy-dependent Andreev phase, while $\phi$ denotes the condensate phase of the electrode. For nonideal interfaces, finite backscattering reintroduces normal reflection and reduces the Andreev amplitude below unit \cite{andreev_thermal_1964,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Short-Junction Limit}
        %=========================================================

            The short-junction approximation requires the weak-link length $L$ to remain much smaller than the relevant superconducting coherence length, $L\ll\xi$. The clean-limit BCS coherence length of aluminum is
            \begin{equation}
                \xi_{\mathrm{clean}, 0} = \frac{\hbar v_\mathrm{F}}{\pi\Delta_0}\approx 2.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}\,.
            \end{equation}
            For a diffusive aluminum film, the corresponding zero-temperature decay length is
            \begin{equation}
                \xi_{\mathrm{dirty},0}
                = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar D}{\Delta_0}}\approx 67-111\,\mathrm{nm}\,.
            \end{equation}

            The parameters and source measurements entering the clean and dirty estimates are given in Secs.~\ref{subsec:quasi:normal-state-quasiparticles} and \ref{subsec:micro:superconducting-gap}. These are representative bulk and film estimates rather than device-specific coherence lengths. Both greatly exceed an atomic-scale constriction, placing the contacts considered here in the short-junction limit \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. The coherence lengths characterize superconducting propagation in clean or diffusive material. They do not determine whether transport through the constriction itself is ballistic or diffusive.

            In the short-junction limit, quasiparticles acquire only a negligible energy-dependent propagation phase while traversing the weak link. Its normal-state scattering properties may therefore be treated as energy independent over the gap. Each channel is characterized by one transmission eigenvalue $\tau_i$, and its ABS spectrum depends only on $\tau_i$, the gap, and the phase difference. This leads to the universal short-junction spectrum below.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Energy Spectrum and Current--Phase Relation}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \subfigure[
                    ABS Energy Spectra
                    ]{\import{theory/meso}{abs-Ephi.pgf}}
                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                    ABS Current--Phase Relation
                    ]{\import{theory/meso}{abs-Iphi.pgf}}
                \caption{
                    Andreev bound-state spectrum and equilibrium current--phase relation of a short single-channel contact for several transmissions $\tau$.
                    (a) Increasing $\tau$ strengthens the phase dispersion and reduces the minimum positive ABS energy at $\phi=\pi$, approaching a crossing for perfect transmission.
                    (b) The corresponding current--phase relation evolves from an approximately sinusoidal tunnel response to a strongly skewed ballistic response with a cusp at $\phi=\pi$.
                }
                \label{fig:meso:abs}
            \end{figure}

            A complete Andreev cycle through the junction converts an electron into a hole at one interface and back into an electron at the other. A bound state forms when this closed electron--hole trajectory reproduces itself, i.e.~when the total phase accumulated in one round trip is an integer multiple of $2\pi$.

            For a single channel of transmission $\tau$, the constructive-interference condition reduces to the compact form
            \begin{equation}
                \sin^2\!\phi_\mathrm{A}(E) = \tau\,\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)\,,
            \end{equation}
            which directly yields the familiar Andreev bound-state spectrum
            \begin{equation}
                E_{\pm}(\phi)=\pm|\Delta|\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)}\,.
                \label{eq:meso:abs-spectrum}
            \end{equation}
            This universal spectrum applies to a short single-channel junction with energy-independent normal scattering \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991,beenakker_quantum_1992}. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:abs}(a) shows these spectra for different transmissions. In the tunneling regime, the levels remain close to the gap edges and depend weakly on phase. Increasing $\tau$ strengthens the phase dispersion and lowers the positive-energy branch near $\phi=\pi$.

            The phase dependence of the ABS spectrum implies that these states carry a nondissipative supercurrent. In equilibrium, the current follows from the derivative of the junction free energy with respect to phase.

            Equivalently, one may express the contribution of each ABS branch through its occupation, which yields the standard spin-degenerate relation
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=-2\pi \frac{G_0}{e} \frac{\partial E(\phi)}{\partial \phi}\,\tanh\!\left(\frac{E(\phi)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $E(\phi)>0$ denotes the positive-energy ABS spectrum, and the hyperbolic tangent accounts for thermal occupation \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            Inserting Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:abs-spectrum} yields
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{G_0}{e}|\Delta|\,
                \frac{\tau\sin\phi}{\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)}}\,
                \tanh\!\left(\frac{|\Delta|\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)}}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:cpr-single}
            \end{equation}
            In the zero-temperature limit, $\tanh\!\left(E/2k_\mathrm{B}T\right)\to 1$ and the current is set solely by the phase dispersion of the occupied negative-energy branch \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991}. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:abs}(b) shows that increasing $\tau$ drives the current--phase relation away from a sinusoidal form and shifts its maximum towards $\phi=\pi$.

            In the tunneling limit ($\tau\ll 1$), the current--phase relation becomes sinusoidal and reduces to the standard Josephson form discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:macro}. In the same limit, the critical current is consistent with the Ambegaokar--Baratoff result when expressed in terms of the normal-state resistance.

            In the opposite ballistic limit ($\tau\to 1$), the bound-state spectrum becomes particularly simple,
            \begin{equation}
                E_{\pm}(\phi)=\pm|\Delta|\,\left|\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)\right|\,.
                \label{eq:meso:abs-ballistic}
            \end{equation}
            The corresponding current at $T=0$ follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-single},
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tfrac{G_0}{e}|\Delta|\,\sin\!\left(\phi/2\right)\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            At perfect transmission, the positive and negative equilibrium energies in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:abs-ballistic} meet in cusps at $\phi=\pi$. The underlying parity-resolved dispersions, proportional to $\pm|\Delta|\cos(\phi/2)$, cross at this phase. Any finite normal reflection opens an avoided crossing. The minimum positive ABS energy is
            \begin{equation}
                E_\mathrm{min}\equiv E_+(\pi)=|\Delta|\sqrt{1-\tau}\,.
            \end{equation}
            Deviations from perfect transmission lift the zero-energy crossing and smooth the cusp in the equilibrium current. Finite temperature further rounds this feature through the occupation factor.

            In summary, the transmission $\tau$ controls the phase dispersion, minimum energy, and equilibrium current--phase relation of a short single-channel contact. These quantities are the equilibrium observables that connect the mesoscopic pincode to Josephson measurements.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Multi-Channel Contacts}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \subfigure[
                    Current--Phase Relation $I(\phi)$
                    ]{\import{theory/meso}{ko-cpr.pgf}}
                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                    Critical Current $I_\mathrm{c}(T)$
                    ]{\import{theory/meso}{ko-ic.pgf}}
                \caption{
                    Equilibrium current--phase relation and critical current of three canonical short-junction reference limits, Ambegaokar-Baratoff (AB) and Kulik--Omel'yanchuk (KO1 \& KO2).
                    (a) The AB result is sinusoidal CPR, whereas KO1 for a diffusive contact and KO2 for a clean ballistic contact are progressively more skewed towards $\phi=\pi$.
                    (b) The corresponding critical currents follow from maximizing each current--phase relation with respect to $\phi$ using a BCS-like gap $\Delta(T)$.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:abs-ko}
            \end{figure}

            Real atomic contacts generally support a small number of discrete conduction channels. In the short-junction limit, each channel $i$ is characterized by a transmission eigenvalue $\tau_i$ and supports a pair of Andreev branches $E_{i,\pm}(\tau_i,\phi)$ given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:abs-spectrum}. The equilibrium supercurrent follows by summing the single-channel contribution from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-single} over all channels,
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi,T)=\sum_i I(\tau_i,\phi,T)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:cpr-multichannel}
            \end{equation}
            For a conductor with many modes, the discrete channel sum may instead be represented by a continuous density of transmission eigenvalues $\rho(\tau)$,
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi,T)\simeq \int_0^1 \rho(\tau)\, I(\tau,\phi,T)\,\mathrm d\tau\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $\rho(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau$ gives the number of channels with transmissions in the interval $[\tau,\tau+\mathrm d\tau]$. It is therefore a channel density rather than a normalized probability distribution. Its conductance normalization is $G_\mathrm N=G_0\int_0^1 \rho(\tau)\,\tau\,\mathrm d\tau$ \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{1.3in}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                % \vspace{-0.5em}
                \import{theory/meso}{ko-rho.pgf}
                \caption{
                    DMPK transmission density for a short diffusive wire.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:ko-rho}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            In the metallic diffusive limit, the transmission eigenvalues of a quasi-one-dimensional wire follow the bimodal Dorokhov--Mello--Pereyra--Kumar (DMPK) distribution \cite{dorokhov_transmission_1982,beenakker_random-matrix_1997},
            \begin{equation}
                \rho(\tau) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{G_0} \frac{1}{2\tau \sqrt{1-\tau}}\,,\quad 0<\tau<1\,,
                \label{eq:meso:dmpk}
            \end{equation}
            as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:ko-rho}. Its bimodal form represents a large population of almost-closed channels together with almost-open channels that contribute strongly to transport.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:meso:dmpk} gives the first Kulik--Omel'yanchuk (KO1) relation for a dirty diffusive contact. At $T=0$, the current is
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{KO1}(\phi) = \pi \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}|\Delta|\,\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)\,\operatorname{artanh}\!\left(\sin\!\left(\phi/2\right)\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Although $\operatorname{artanh}[\sin(\phi/2)]$ diverges as $\phi\to\pi$, the factor $\cos(\phi/2)$ vanishes more rapidly, such that $I_\mathrm{KO1}(\phi)\to0$ \cite{kulik_contribution_1975,golubov_current-phase_2004}.

            In the complementary clean ballistic limit, all modes are perfectly transmitting, $\tau_i=1$. Summing the ballistic single-channel result gives the second Kulik--Omel'yanchuk relation (KO2),
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{KO2}(\phi, T) = \pi\,\frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}\,\Delta(T)\,\sin\!\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right)\,
                    \tanh\!\left(\frac{\Delta(T)\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            This clean ballistic result is the KO2 reference limit \cite{kulik_contribution_1975,golubov_current-phase_2004}. Channels with finite normal reflection, $\tau_i<1$, are instead described by the general sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-multichannel}. KO1 and KO2 therefore provide many-channel reference limits rather than generic models of an individual atomic contact, whose discrete channel set $\{\tau_i\}$ should normally be retained.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:abs-ko} compares the current--phase relations and critical currents for the Ambegaokar--Baratoff, KO1, and KO2 limits. In each case, $I_\mathrm{c}(T)$ is found by maximizing $I(\phi,T)$ over phase with a BCS-like gap $\Delta(T)$. The channel set or its continuum density determines the observable current--phase relation and critical current, connecting microscopic transmission eigenvalues to equilibrium measurements.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Fractional Shapiro Steps}
        %=========================================================

            The transmission-dependent ABS spectrum also determines the harmonic content of the equilibrium current--phase relation:
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty} I_p\sin(p\phi)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:cpr-harmonics}
            \end{equation}
            In the tunnel limit, the first harmonic $I_1$ dominates and the current is approximately sinusoidal. Highly transmitting channels produce the skewed current--phase relations shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:meso:abs}(b) and \ref{fig:meso:abs-ko}(a), which contain appreciable higher harmonics $I_{p>1}$.

            Under microwave irradiation, these harmonics introduce phase-locking conditions beyond the integer Shapiro steps discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:shapiro}. We use the driven phase of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:shapiro-phase}, with Josephson frequency $\nu_\mathrm{J}=2eV/h$ and drive amplitude $\alpha_{\nu,2e}=2eA/(h\nu)$. Inserting this phase into Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-harmonics} and applying the Jacobi--Anger expansion gives
            \begin{equation}
                I(t)=\sum_{p\geq1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} I_pJ_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})
                \sin\!\left[p\phi_0+2\pi\left(p\nu_\mathrm{J}+n\nu\right)t\right]\,.
            \end{equation}
            The $p$-th CPR harmonic experiences the enlarged modulation $p\alpha_{\nu,2e}$, and its $n$-photon locking contribution is weighted by $I_pJ_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})$ rather than by a squared Bessel function. A dc contribution appears when $p\nu_\mathrm{J}+n\nu=0$. Relabeling the sideband index gives the rational locking voltages
            \begin{equation}
                V_{n/p}=\frac{n}{p}\frac{h\nu}{2e}\,,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad p\in\mathbb{N}^+\,.
            \end{equation}
            An appreciable second harmonic produces half-integer steps for odd $n$. Their visibility is expected to increase when highly transmitting channels contribute strongly to the supercurrent \cite{cuevas_subharmonic_2002,duprat_phase_2005,chauvin_superconducting_2006}.

            In an ideal overdamped junction whose response at $V_{n/p}$ is dominated by one CPR harmonic, the full locking interval in dc current is approximately
            \begin{equation}
                \Delta I_{n/p}\simeq2\left|I_pJ_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})\right|\,.
                \label{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width}
            \end{equation}
            The factor of two is the full interval obtained by varying the phase offset $\phi_0$. For $p=1$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width} reduces to the familiar integer-step modulation $\Delta I_n\simeq2|I_1J_n(\alpha_{\nu,2e})|$. The oscillations and zeros of $J_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})$ predict suppression and revival of a fractional plateau as the microwave amplitude is varied \cite{duprat_phase_2005}.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width} is a single-dominant-harmonic estimate, not a general addition rule. Several pairs $(p,n)$ can correspond to the same reduced rational voltage, and their phase-dependent contributions can interfere. Realistic plateau widths require driven RSJ or RCSJ dynamics and also depend on damping, capacitance, temperature, noise, calibration of $A$, and nonequilibrium ABS occupation. Fractional steps alone are therefore not evidence for a $4\pi$-periodic Josephson effect.

        In summary, repeated Andreev conversion quantizes the subgap spectrum of a short junction into transmission-dependent ABS. Their phase dispersion and minimum gap determine the equilibrium current--phase relation, while the channel set fixes the total supercurrent. Highly transmitting channels generate higher CPR harmonics, which become observable through fractional Shapiro steps under microwave irradiation. The ABS description therefore connects microscopic channel transmissions to equilibrium and phase-locked supercurrent measurements.

        A finite dc voltage changes this regime by driving the superconducting phase continuously in time. Repeated Andreev conversion then produces nonequilibrium charge transport rather than stationary bound-state supercurrent, leading to multiple Andreev reflection.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Multiple Andreev Reflection}
    \label{subsec:meso:mar}
    %=========================================================

        Multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) is the microscopic mechanism of subgap transport in voltage-biased SNS weak links and short superconducting point contacts. The energy-ladder picture fixes the voltage scales used throughout this section. For $|eV|<2|\Delta|$, a quasiparticle cannot directly enter the continuum of the opposite electrode. Instead, successive Andreev conversions alternate its electron- and hole-like character while each traversal changes its energy by $eV$, as illustrated for several orders in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:mar-schematic}. In the trajectory picture of Klapwijk, Blonder, and Tinkham (KBT), the threshold for a process requiring $m$ energy increments is
        \begin{equation}
            eV_m=\frac{2|\Delta|}{m}\,,\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}^+\,.
            \label{eq:meso:mar-sgs}
        \end{equation}
        We refer to these voltages as MAR thresholds. Here $m$ denotes the threshold order of the idealized energy-ladder process. The MAR thresholds generate the subharmonic-gap structure observed as slope changes in \textit{I--V} and peaks or kinks in \textit{dI--dV} \cite{klapwijk_explanation_1982,octavio_subharmonic_1983}. The same integer is related to the effective charge order in a charge-resolved description, but the two should not be identified without qualification because several MAR processes can contribute at a given bias.

            \centering
            \subfigure[
                Second-order MAR
                ]{\import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-mar2.pdf_tex}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[
                Third-order MAR
                ]{\import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-mar3.pdf_tex}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[
                Fourth-order MAR

                Lower voltage therefore requires a larger number of traversals to bridge the gap $2|\Delta|$.
                }
            \label{fig:meso:mar-schematic}
        \end{figure}

        The semiclassical OTBK model provides a historical quantitative realization of this ladder picture \cite{octavio_subharmonic_1983}. Short atomic contacts remain phase coherent, so we use the channel-resolved Hamiltonian approach of Cuevas \textit{et al.} for quantitative transport across the full transmission range.

        At high transmission, the same dynamics can be viewed through the ABS spectrum. A dc bias drives the phase through the avoided crossing near $\phi=\pi$, where nonadiabatic Landau--Zener transitions can change the ABS branch occupation. This two-level picture is useful for a nearly ballistic channel, while the coherent transport model below treats the complete MAR current at arbitrary transmission \cite{averin_ac_1995}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Coherent Channel-Resolved Transport}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \subfigure[
                    MAR \textit{I--V} characteristic
                    ]{\import{theory/meso}{ha-iv.pgf}}
                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                    MAR \textit{dI--dV} characteristic
                    ]{\import{theory/meso}{ha-didv.pgf}}
                \caption{
                    Coherent MAR transport through a short single-channel contact at selected transmissions.
                    (a) The subgap current increases strongly with $\tau$, and a finite excess current remains above the gap.
                    (b) The corresponding differential conductance displays subharmonic-gap features at the MAR thresholds of Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}, which broaden and overlap as transmission increases.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:mar-ha}
            \end{figure}

            We now replace the trajectory picture by the coherent single-channel model used for quantitative dc MAR. A short single-channel contact is modeled as two bulk BCS reservoirs coupled by a local hopping amplitude \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996}. This is the finite-voltage continuation of the ABS model: at $V=0$ it produces the equilibrium spectrum discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:abs}, while a running phase generates coherent MAR.

            The junction Hamiltonian is written as
            \begin{align}
                \hat{H} &= \hat{H}_L + \hat{H}_R + \hat{H}_T(t)\,,\\
                \hat{H}_T(t) &= \sum_\sigma \left( t\,e^{+\ima \phi(t)/2}\,\hat{c}^\dagger_{L\sigma}\hat{c}_{R\sigma}
                + t\,e^{-\ima \phi(t)/2}\,\hat{c}^\dagger_{R\sigma}\hat{c}_{L\sigma}\right)\,.
            \end{align}
            This local-hopping Hamiltonian is the starting point of the coherent channel-resolved MAR calculation \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996}.

            The phase factors in $\hat{H}_T(t)$ incorporate the dc voltage through the $q=2e$ phase of Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-phase}. The resulting time-periodic problem is solved with Keldysh Green functions in Nambu space and a Floquet representation in energy. For one channel, the calculation returns $I_\mathrm{HA}(V;\tau,T,\Delta,\gamma)$ from the transmission $\tau$, gap $\Delta$, temperature $T$, broadening $\gamma$, and voltage $V$.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:mar-ha}(a) shows that increasing $\tau$ enhances the subgap current because successive traversals are less suppressed. The MAR thresholds remain fixed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}, but the structures overlap and the current approaches a smooth high-transmission response. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:mar-ha}(b) shows the same evolution in differential conductance, where thresholds appear as peaks or kinks. Above the gap, the curves retain a transmission-dependent excess current generated by Andreev processes.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Multichannel Atomic Contacts}
        %=========================================================

            The single-channel result becomes experimentally useful when it is combined with the mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$ of an atomic contact. Independent channels contribute additively to the coherent dc current,
            \begin{equation}
                I(V,\{\tau_i\},T,\Delta,\gamma)
                =
                \sum_i I(V,\tau_i,T,\Delta,\gamma)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Each transmission produces a characteristic \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} line shape because MAR probabilities depend differently on $\tau_i$. Their sum therefore remains sensitive to the mesoscopic pincode. This transmission sensitivity makes nonlinear SIS spectra a channel-resolved probe of atomic contacts \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996,scheer_conduction_1997}.

            The normal-state conductance constrains only the total transmission, so different mesoscopic pincodes can have the same $G_\mathrm{N}$. Their nonlinear SIS spectra can nevertheless differ and can be fitted to extract the pincode within the coherent short-contact model. Channels with the same transmission produce identical spectral contributions and cannot be distinguished individually. The fitting procedure is described in the methods section.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Charge-Resolved Multiple Andreev Reflection}
        %=========================================================

            To connect dc MAR to charge transfer, we use the current decomposition provided by full counting statistics. Full counting statistics provides a process-resolved extension of the coherent MAR calculation \cite{levitov_charge_1993,levitov_electron_1996,cuevas_full_2003}. We use it only to decompose the dc current into contributions associated with transfer of an effective charge $q=me$,
            \begin{equation}
                I(V)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} I_m(V)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:fcs-decomposition}
            \end{equation}
            The index $m$ now labels effective charge order rather than the threshold order used in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}. The two meanings are physically connected because lower voltages require longer Andreev sequences and permit larger charge transfers, but several $I_m$ can coexist at the same bias \cite{cuevas_full_2003}.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:fcs} shows this redistribution for three transmissions. Near the gap edge, low-order processes carry most of the current. Lower bias requires larger transfer orders, although these processes are strongly suppressed in the tunneling regime. As $\tau$ increases, repeated traversal becomes more probable and several charge-transfer orders acquire appreciable weight.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Photon-Assisted Multiple Andreev Reflection}
        %=========================================================

            The final step is to add microwave irradiation to the charge-resolved dc MAR picture. Irradiated MAR is more complex than dc MAR because a voltage-biased superconducting contact already contains the Josephson frequency $\nu_J=2eV/h$. A microwave drive at frequency $\nu$ adds a second time scale, so the driven problem cannot generally be reduced to the single-Floquet structure used for dc transport.

            The full coherent ac theory was developed by Cuevas \textit{et al.} for superconducting point contacts of arbitrary transmission \cite{cuevas_subharmonic_2002}. It treats the Josephson oscillation and external microwave drive with a two-frequency Keldysh--Floquet calculation. This double-Floquet theory is the microscopic reference for subharmonic Shapiro structure and photon-assisted MAR in irradiated point contacts. Its numerical cost is much higher than that of the dc problem, making it impractical as the routine model for dense parameter scans and fitting workflows.

            For routine fitting, we use a Tien--Gordon dressing of charge-resolved MAR. Soerensen \textit{et al.} introduced this charge-dependent viewpoint for microwave-modified subgap structure, and recent experiments by Carrad \textit{et al.} used the same logic to identify high-order MAR processes from sideband spacings \cite{soerensen_microwave_1974,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. In the notation used here, the FCS decomposition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:fcs-decomposition} supplies the dc current components $I_m(V)$, and each component is dressed with Tien--Gordon weights for its effective charge order $m$.

            This gives the charge-resolved photon-assisted MAR current
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{PAMAR}(V,A)
                =
                \sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^+}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
                J_n^2\left(\frac{meA}{h\nu}\right)\,
                I_m\!\left(V+\frac{nh\nu}{me}\right)\,,
                \label{eq:meso:pamar-current}
            \end{equation}
            where $A$ is the effective microwave voltage amplitude at the junction, $m$ labels the effective charge order, and $n$ labels the photon sideband. The corresponding photon-assisted replicas of the MAR thresholds appear approximately at
            \begin{equation}
                eV_{m,n}\simeq\frac{2|\Delta|}{m}\pm\frac{nh\nu}{m}\,.
            \end{equation}
            The sideband spacing therefore scales as $h\nu/(me)$ in voltage. Photon-assisted MAR can identify the effective charge order associated with a subgap feature, complementing the dc charge resolution in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:fcs-m} \cite{soerensen_microwave_1974,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:meso:pamar-current} is a practical dressing of dc FCS components, not a replacement for the full double-Floquet theory. It neglects coherent two-frequency interference, microwave-induced changes of ABS occupation, heating, environmental energy exchange, and current-biased phase dynamics. Within these limits, it provides an interpretable model for connecting microwave-induced sidebands to the charge-resolved MAR processes used in this thesis. The environmental backaction omitted here can modify the MAR current itself through dynamical Coulomb blockade, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:stochastic:dcb}.

            The resulting hierarchy is used in the remainder of this thesis. The dc Hamiltonian approach describes channel-resolved MAR in a short coherent contact, channel sums connect this response to the mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$, FCS resolves the effective charge order, and charge-dependent Tien--Gordon dressing gives a practical PAMAR model. The full Cuevas double-Floquet theory remains the microscopic benchmark for irradiated point contacts.
