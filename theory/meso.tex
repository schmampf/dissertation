%=========================================================
\section{Mesoscopic Superconducting Transport}
\label{sec:meso}
%=========================================================

    The microscopic section treated quasiparticle tunneling in the low-transmission limit, while the macroscopic section described phase dynamics of weakly coupled Josephson junctions. Atomic contacts require a mesoscopic description because their conductance is carried by a small number of phase-coherent channels with finite transmission. In this regime, the normal-state transmission eigenvalues, the mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$, determine both the equilibrium spectrum and nonlinear subgap transport.

    Finite transmission makes electron and hole degrees of freedom inseparable. Subgap current is no longer described by independent quasiparticle tunneling or by a lumped Josephson element, but by coherent Andreev reflection at superconducting interfaces. We first define the mesoscopic transport regimes and introduce atomic contacts. We then quantify a single NS interface with the Blonder-Tinkham-Klapwijk (BTK) model, extend the scattering picture to Andreev bound states in short SNS contacts, and finally treat finite-bias multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) and its charge-resolved response.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Mesoscopic Regimes and Atomic Contacts}
    \label{subsec:quasi:meso}
    %=========================================================

        The transport description of a finite conductor is selected by comparing its length $L$ with the elastic mean free path $\ell_\mathrm{qp}$ and the phase-coherence length $\ell_\phi$. Elastic scattering randomizes momentum, while inelastic and dephasing processes limit phase coherence. A conductor is diffusive when $\ell_\mathrm{qp}\ll L$ and ballistic when $L\ll\ell_\mathrm{qp}$. In either case, transport remains phase coherent across the conductor when $L\lesssim\ell_\phi$. Figure~\ref{fig:quasi:meso} summarizes these regimes. \cite{gross_festkorperphysik_2014,beenakker_random-matrix_1997}

        The atomic-scale constrictions considered in this thesis are much shorter than both $\ell_\mathrm{qp}$ and $\ell_\phi$. They therefore lie in the ballistic and phase-coherent regime. Diffusive mesoscopic transport serves only as a reference, while the following discussion develops the transmission-channel description required for atomic contacts.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Ballistic Transport and Scattering Formalism}
        %=========================================================

            Ballistic transport is formulated as an elastic scattering problem. A finite scattering region connects ideal leads whose propagating modes carry the current. We denote the incoming amplitudes in the left and right leads by $A_1$ and $A_2$, and the outgoing amplitudes by $B_1$ and $B_2$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:scatter}.

            Linear scattering then gives
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{pmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{pmatrix} = \mat{S}\,\begin{pmatrix} A_1 \\ A_2 \end{pmatrix}\,.
                \label{eq:meso:scattering-matrix}
            \end{equation}

            \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                % \vspace{-1em} % fine-tune vertical position
                \import{theory/schema/scattering}{scattering-matrix.pdf_tex}
                % \vspace{-.5em} % fine-tune vertical position
                \caption{
                    Sketch of a two-terminal scatterer connecting ideal leads. Incoming and outgoing mode amplitudes $\vec{A}$, $\vec{B}$ are related by the scattering matrix $\mat{S}$.
                }
                \label{fig:quasi:scatter}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            In the multi-mode case, $A_{1,2}$ and $B_{1,2}$ become vectors collecting all propagating modes in the corresponding lead. 
            The scattering matrix decomposes into reflection blocks $\mat{r}$ and $\mat{r}'$ and transmission blocks $\mat{t}$ and $\mat{t}'$,
            \begin{equation}
                \mat{S}
                = \begin{pmatrix} \mat{r} & \mat{t}' \\ \mat{t} & \mat{r}' \end{pmatrix}\,.
                \label{eq:meso:scattering-blocks}
            \end{equation}
            For purely elastic normal-state scattering, $\mat{S}$ is unitary because probability current is conserved.

            The Hermitian matrix $\mat{t}^\dagger\mat{t}$ can be diagonalized by a unitary transformation. Its eigenvectors define independent transmission eigenchannels, and its eigenvalues
            \begin{equation}
                \{\tau_i\} = \operatorname{eig}\!\left(\mat{t}^\dagger\,\mat{t}\right)\,,\quad
                \tau_i\in[0,1]\,.
                \label{eq:meso:transmission-eigenvalues}
            \end{equation}
            give their transmission probabilities.

            In linear response, the normal-state conductance is then given by the Landauer formula,
            \begin{equation}
                G_\mathrm{N} = G_0 \sum_{i=1}^N \tau_i\,,
                \quad
                G_0 = \frac{2e^2}{h}\,,
                \label{eq:meso:landauer}
            \end{equation}
            where $\tau_i$ are the transmission eigenvalues and $G_0$ is the conductance quantum\footnote{
                Throughout this thesis the conductance quantum is defined as $G_0 \equiv 2e^2/h = \qty{77.48}{\micro\siemens}$. This value includes spin degeneracy and corresponds to the conductance of a fully transmitting normal-state channel. The corresponding resistance quantum is $R_0 = h/(2e^2) = \qty{12.9}{\kilo\ohm}$.
            } \cite{landauer_electrical_1970,buttiker_four-terminal_1986}.

            The normal-state conductance determines only the total transmission $\sum_i\tau_i$. Different channel sets can therefore produce the same $G_\mathrm{N}$. The set $\{\tau_i\}$ is referred to throughout this thesis as the mesoscopic pincode. In a superconducting contact, different pincodes produce distinct multiple Andreev reflection characteristics, which allows the individual transmissions to be extracted from measured \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} curves. \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,beenakker_random-matrix_1997}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Transmission Channels in Atomic Contacts}
        %=========================================================

            A split-gate quantum point contact (QPC) in a high-mobility two-dimensional electron gas provides a controllable model system for the channel description. Its ballistic constriction is tuned electrostatically, and the conductance increases in quantized steps as transverse modes open. Each fully open spin-degenerate mode defines a conduction channel and contributes one conductance quantum $G_0$. The resulting staircase $G(V_\mathrm{G})$ is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:atomic-contact}(a) \cite{van_wees_quantized_1988,wharam_one-dimensional_1988}.

            In a semiconductor QPC, the channels can be opened electrostatically. In a metallic atomic contact, their number, orbital character, and transmission probabilities are instead determined by the atomic geometry and electronic structure of the complete scattering region.

            Metallic atomic contacts are modeled as short, ballistic, and phase-coherent scatterers whose narrowest cross section contains one or a few atoms. During mechanical elongation, atomic rearrangements produce conductance changes such as those in the opening trace in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:atomic-contact}(b). Repeating this process yields the conductance histogram in Fig.~\ref{fig:quasi:atomic-contact}(c), whose peaks mark preferred conductance values. Opening traces and histograms characterize the mechanical evolution and statistical occurrence of atomic configurations, but they do not determine the individual transmission probabilities \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,yanson_histograms_1997,agrait_quantum_2003}.

            The eigenchannels are scattering states of the complete contact rather than isolated atomic orbitals. Their character nevertheless reflects the valence orbitals near the narrowest cross section and their hybridization with the electrodes. For aluminum, experiments and calculations commonly identify three dominant channels in a single-atom contact. These can be associated predominantly with one longitudinal $sp_z$-like contribution and two transverse $p$-like contributions. Their precise orbital composition and transmission probabilities depend on the contact geometry, and additional weakly transmitting channels may contribute \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,scheer_signature_1998}.

            Normal-state measurements determine only the sum $G_\mathrm{N}=G_0\sum_i\tau_i$. In the superconducting state, the nonlinear multiple Andreev reflection response depends separately on the individual $\tau_i$, which allows the mesoscopic pincode to be extracted from measured \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} curves. The resulting transmission eigenvalues provide the input for the finite-transmission superconducting models developed below \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,scheer_signature_1998}.

        In summary, atomic contacts lie in the ballistic and phase-coherent limit, where the transmission eigenvalues $\{\tau_i\}$ provide the natural transport variables. The normal-state conductance determines only the total transmission, while channel-resolved superconducting spectroscopy supplies the additional information needed to determine the individual transmissions. We therefore turn to Andreev reflection, which makes the superconducting response of each channel depend nonlinearly on its transmission.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Andreev Reflection}
    \label{subsec:meso:ar}
    %=========================================================

        At a normal--superconductor (NS) interface, no propagating quasiparticle states are available in the superconducting continuum for $|E|<\Delta$. An electron incident from the normal side therefore cannot enter the superconductor as a single quasiparticle at these energies. Instead, it can be reflected into the normal electrode as a hole while a Cooper pair with charge $2e$ enters the superconducting condensate. This coherent electron--hole conversion is Andreev reflection \cite{andreev_thermal_1964}.

        Figure~\ref{fig:meso:btk}(a) shows single-particle transmission into the superconducting quasiparticle continuum above the gap, while Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:btk}(b) shows subgap Andreev reflection. These panels are energy-space schematics rather than real-space trajectories. At a nonideal interface, normal reflection competes with Andreev reflection, and their relative probabilities depend on the normal-state transmission.

        Andreev reflection converts quasiparticle current in the normal electrode into Cooper pair current in the superconductor. In a junction with two superconducting electrodes, repeated coherent conversion produces discrete Andreev bound states (ABS) in equilibrium and multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) under finite bias. In atomic contacts, both processes are resolved by channels with transmission probabilities $\tau_i$. The Blonder--Tinkham--Klapwijk (BTK) model introduced next quantifies normal reflection, quasiparticle transmission, and Andreev reflection at a single NS interface \cite{blonder_transition_1982,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            The Blonder--Tinkham--Klapwijk (BTK) model provides a quantitative scattering description of an NS interface with normal-state transmission probability $\tau$. An electron incident from the normal electrode can undergo Andreev reflection with probability $A(E)$, normal reflection with probability $B(E)$, or quasiparticle transmission into the superconductor with probability $C(E)$. Conservation of probability current requires
            \begin{equation}
                A(E) + B(E) + C(E) = 1\,.
                \label{eq:meso:btk-probability}
            \end{equation}
            This relation follows from probability-current conservation in the BTK scattering problem \cite{blonder_transition_1982}.

            The BTK scattering amplitudes are expressed in terms of the electron and hole components of the Bogoliubov quasiparticles introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsubsec:micro:bdg-eigenproblem}. Solving the homogeneous BdG eigenproblem in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:bdg-homogeneous} gives the BCS coherence factors below. To incorporate finite quasiparticle lifetimes phenomenologically, we follow Pleceník \textit{et al.}~and evaluate them with the replacement $E\rightarrow|E|+\ima\gamma$ \cite{plecenik_finite-quasiparticle-lifetime_1994},
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    \xi(E) &= \sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}\,,\\
                    u_0(E) &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1+\xi(E)/E\right)}\,,\\
                    v_0(E) &= \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(1-\xi(E)/E\right)}\,.
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:meso:coherence-factors}
            \end{equation}

            Using the normalization factor $d$, the Andreev and normal reflection amplitudes $a$ and $b$ are
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    A(E) &= aa^*\,,\quad
                    a= u_0v_0/d\,,\quad
                    d = \left(u^2_0-(1-\tau)v_0^2\middle)\right/\tau\,,\\
                    B(E) &= bb^*\,,\quad
                    b= -\left(u_0^2-v_0^2\right)\left( (1-\tau) + \ima \sqrt{\tau(1-\tau)} \middle)\,\right/\,\tau d\,,
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:meso:btk-amplitudes}
            \end{equation}
            Here $u_0$ and $v_0$ are evaluated at $E$. The quasiparticle transmission probability then follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:btk-probability} as $C(E)=1-A(E)-B(E)$ \cite{blonder_transition_1982,plecenik_finite-quasiparticle-lifetime_1994}.

            The NS current can be decomposed into single-particle and Andreev contributions through the energy-resolved BTK transport kernels
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    \rho_\mathrm{1e}(E) &= 1 - A(E) - B(E) = C(E)\,,\\
                    \rho_\mathrm{2e}(E) &= 2 \, A(E) \,,
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:meso:btk-dos}
            \end{equation}
            The first kernel describes quasiparticle transmission. The factor of two in the second kernel accounts for the charge $2e$ transferred by one Andreev reflection event. For ideal subgap transport at $\gamma=0$, $C(E)=0$ and the single-particle kernel vanishes. Finite lifetime broadening can introduce subgap quasiparticle weight. Although the kernels resemble densities of states, they enter the current integral and should not be interpreted as densities of states.

            The current through an NS interface then follows from these kernels,
            \begin{equation}
                \begin{aligned}
                    I_\mathrm{1e}(V) &= \frac{G_0}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \rho_\mathrm{1e}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,,\\
                    I_\mathrm{2e}(V) &= \frac{G_0}{e} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \rho_\mathrm{2e}(E) \left(f(E) - f(E + eV)\right) \mathrm{d}E\,,\\
                    I_\mathrm{NS}(V) &= I_\mathrm{1e}(V) + I_\mathrm{2e}(V)\,.
                \end{aligned}
                \label{eq:meso:btk-iv}
            \end{equation}
            In the tunneling limit, $\tau\ll1$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:btk-iv} reduces to the conventional quasiparticle tunneling expression. As $\tau$ approaches unity, Andreev reflection dominates the subgap response and charge is transferred predominantly in units of $2e$ \cite{blonder_transition_1982}.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:btk} summarizes this evolution across the transmission range. Panels (c) and (d) show the transport kernels $\rho_{\mathrm{1e}}(E)$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{2e}}(E)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:btk-dos}. At low transmission, the single-particle contribution dominates and produces a quasiparticle peak near $|E|=\Delta$. Increasing $\tau$ transfers spectral weight to the Andreev contribution, which enhances the subgap conductance in \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV}.

        The BTK model describes scattering at one NS interface. When a normal region connects two superconducting electrodes, successive Andreev reflections at the two interfaces form coherent closed electron--hole trajectories. Their phase-dependent quantization produces Andreev bound states whose spectra are set by the channel transmission probabilities $\tau_i$.

    \subsection{Andreev Bound States}
    \label{subsec:meso:abs}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            % \vspace{-1.5em}
            \import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sns-abs.pdf_tex}
            \caption{
                Andreev bound-state formation in an SNS weak link.
                }
            \label{fig:meso:abs-schematics}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        Andreev reflection becomes phase-coherent and spectrally quantized when two superconductors are connected by a mesoscopic weak link. In an SNS geometry, a subgap quasiparticle ($|E|<\Delta$) cannot escape into the superconducting continua and instead undergoes successive Andreev reflections at both interfaces, alternating between electron- and hole-like character as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:abs-schematics}. One cycle acquires the phase difference
        \begin{equation}
            \phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2\,.
        \end{equation}
        The resulting constructive-interference condition quantizes the motion into discrete subgap eigenstates localized around the junction, known as Andreev bound states (ABS) \cite{andreev_thermal_1964,ishii_josephson_1970,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

        These ABS form the microscopic origin of the dc Josephson effect in short mesoscopic junctions. Their phase-dependent energies $E_n(\phi)$ determine the equilibrium supercurrent through their occupation \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991, beenakker_quantum_1992}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Andreev Approximation and Short-Junction Limit}
        %=========================================================

            A transparent route from BdG to ABS is to treat Andreev reflection as an energy-dependent boundary condition at the superconducting leads, while representing the weak link by its normal-state scattering properties.

            In the Andreev approximation, $|E|,\Delta\ll E_\mathrm{F}$, only quasiparticles in a narrow shell around the Fermi surface contribute. For an isotropic parabolic band, the dispersion can be linearized in the radial direction,
            \begin{equation}
                \varepsilon(\vec{k})-\mu \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{m}\,\vec{k}_\mathrm{F}\cdot\left(\vec{k}-\vec{k}_\mathrm{F}\right)\,,
            \end{equation}
            so electron-like and hole-like components have nearly equal momentum magnitudes. For an excitation at energy $E$, the corresponding wave numbers satisfy
            \begin{equation}
                \delta k = \frac{k_\mathrm{e} - k_\mathrm{h}}{2} \approx \frac{k_\mathrm{F}}{2}\frac{E}{E_\mathrm{F}}\,,
            \end{equation}
            implying that the Andreev-reflected hole retraces the incoming trajectory up to corrections of order $E/E_\mathrm{F}$ \cite{andreev_thermal_1964,de_gennes_superconductivity_1966}.

            In the Andreev limit of a clean, specular, and highly transparent NS interface with negligible normal reflection, subgap Andreev reflection occurs with approximately unit probability. The corresponding electron-to-hole reflection amplitude can then be written as a pure phase factor,
            \begin{equation}
                r_\mathrm{A}(E) = e^{-\ima\phi_\mathrm{A}(E)}\,e^{-\ima\phi}\,,\qquad
                \phi_\mathrm{A}(E)\equiv\arccos\!\left(E/\Delta\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $\phi_\mathrm{A}(E)$ is the energy-dependent Andreev phase, while $\phi$ denotes the condensate phase of the electrode. For nonideal interfaces, finite backscattering reintroduces normal reflection and reduces the Andreev amplitude below unit \cite{andreev_thermal_1964,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            The second assumption is the short-junction limit, $L\ll\xi$, where $\xi$ is the relevant superconducting length in the electrodes. The aluminum films can be diffusive even though the atomic constriction is a short coherent scatterer. Representative clean and dirty aluminum length estimates are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:theory:diffusive-transport}. These superconducting lengths remain much larger than the atomic scattering region \cite{steinberg_quasi-particle_2008,scheer_conduction_1997}.

            In this limit, the propagation phase acquired across the weak link varies negligibly over the superconducting gap. Its normal-state scattering matrix can therefore be treated as energy independent in the relevant energy window. Each channel is characterized by one transmission eigenvalue $\tau_i$, and its ABS spectrum depends only on $\tau_i$, $\Delta$, and the phase difference $\phi$. These assumptions lead to the universal short-junction spectrum introduced next.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Energy Spectrum and Current--Phase Relation}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \subfigure[
                    ABS Energy Spectra
                    ]{\thesisplot{theory/meso}{abs-Ephi}}
                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                    ABS Current--Phase Relation
                    ]{\thesisplot{theory/meso}{abs-Iphi}}
                \caption{
                    Andreev bound-state spectrum and equilibrium current--phase relation of a short single-channel contact for several transmissions $\tau$.
                    (a) Increasing $\tau$ strengthens the phase dispersion and reduces the minimum positive ABS energy at $\phi=\pi$, approaching a crossing for perfect transmission.
                    (b) The corresponding current--phase relation evolves from an approximately sinusoidal tunnel response to a strongly skewed ballistic response with a cusp at $\phi=\pi$.
                }
                \label{fig:meso:abs}
            \end{figure}

            A complete Andreev cycle through the junction converts an electron into a hole at one interface and back into an electron at the other. A bound state forms when this closed electron--hole trajectory reproduces itself, i.e.~when the total phase accumulated in one round trip is an integer multiple of $2\pi$.

            For a single channel of transmission $\tau$, the constructive-interference condition reduces to the compact form
            \begin{equation}
                \sin^2\!\phi_\mathrm{A}(E) = \tau\,\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)\,,
            \end{equation}
            which directly yields the familiar Andreev bound-state spectrum
            \begin{equation}
                E_{\pm}(\phi)=\pm\Delta\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)}\,.
                \label{eq:meso:abs-spectrum}
            \end{equation}
            This universal spectrum applies to a short single-channel junction with energy-independent normal scattering \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991,beenakker_quantum_1992}. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:abs}(a) shows these spectra for different transmissions. In the tunneling regime, the levels remain close to the gap edges and depend weakly on phase. Increasing $\tau$ strengthens the phase dispersion and lowers the positive-energy branch near $\phi=\pi$.

            The phase dependence of the ABS spectrum implies that these states carry a nondissipative supercurrent. In equilibrium, the current follows from the derivative of the junction free energy with respect to phase.

            Equivalently, one may express the contribution of each ABS branch through its occupation, which yields the standard spin-degenerate relation
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=-2\pi \frac{G_0}{e} \frac{\partial E(\phi)}{\partial \phi}\,\tanh\!\left(\frac{E(\phi)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $E(\phi)>0$ denotes the positive-energy ABS spectrum, and the hyperbolic tangent accounts for thermal occupation \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            Inserting Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:abs-spectrum} yields
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{G_0}{e}\Delta\,
                \frac{\tau\sin\phi}{\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)}}\,
                \tanh\!\left(\frac{\Delta\sqrt{1-\tau\sin^2\!\left(\phi/2\right)}}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:cpr-single}
            \end{equation}
            In the zero-temperature limit, $\tanh\!\left(E/2k_\mathrm{B}T\right)\to 1$ and the current is set solely by the phase dispersion of the occupied negative-energy branch \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991}. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:abs}(b) shows that increasing $\tau$ drives the current--phase relation away from a sinusoidal form and shifts its maximum towards $\phi=\pi$.

            In the tunneling limit ($\tau\ll 1$), the current--phase relation becomes sinusoidal and reduces to the standard Josephson form discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:macro}. In the same limit, the critical current is consistent with the Ambegaokar--Baratoff result when expressed in terms of the normal-state resistance.

            In the opposite ballistic limit ($\tau\to 1$), the bound-state spectrum becomes particularly simple,
            \begin{equation}
                E_{\pm}(\phi)=\pm\Delta\,\left|\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)\right|\,.
                \label{eq:meso:abs-ballistic}
            \end{equation}
            The corresponding current at $T=0$ follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-single},
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tfrac{G_0}{e}\Delta\,\sin\!\left(\phi/2\right)\,\mathrm{sgn}\!\left(\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            At perfect transmission, the positive and negative equilibrium energies in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:abs-ballistic} meet in cusps at $\phi=\pi$. The underlying parity-resolved dispersions, proportional to $\pm\Delta\cos(\phi/2)$, cross at this phase. Any finite normal reflection opens a gap between these dispersions. The minimum positive ABS energy is
            \begin{equation}
                E_\mathrm{min}\equiv E_+(\pi)=\Delta\sqrt{1-\tau}\,.
            \end{equation}
            Deviations from perfect transmission therefore remove the zero-energy crossing, while finite temperature rounds the equilibrium current through the occupation factor.

            In summary, the transmission $\tau$ controls the phase dispersion, minimum energy, and equilibrium current--phase relation of a short single-channel contact. For a multichannel contact, the pincode $\{\tau_i\}$ determines the complete set of ABS and their combined equilibrium supercurrent.

                    Equilibrium current--phase relation and critical current of three canonical short-junction reference limits, Ambegaokar-Baratoff (AB) and Kulik--Omel'yanchuk (KO1 \& KO2).
                    (a) The AB limit corresponds to a sinusoidal CPR, whereas KO1 for a diffusive contact and KO2 for a clean ballistic contact are progressively more skewed towards $\phi=\pi$.
                    (b) The corresponding critical currents follow from maximizing each current--phase relation with respect to $\phi$ using a BCS-like gap $\Delta(T)$.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:abs-ko}
            \end{figure}

            Real atomic contacts generally support a small number of discrete conduction channels. In the short-junction limit, each channel $i$ is characterized by a transmission eigenvalue $\tau_i$ and supports a pair of Andreev branches $E_{i,\pm}(\tau_i,\phi)$ given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:abs-spectrum}. The equilibrium supercurrent follows by summing the single-channel contribution from Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-single} over all channels,
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi,T)=\sum_i I(\tau_i,\phi,T)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:cpr-multichannel}
            \end{equation}
            For a conductor with many modes, the discrete channel sum may instead be represented by a continuous density of transmission eigenvalues $\rho(\tau)$,
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi,T)\simeq \int_0^1 \rho(\tau)\, I(\tau,\phi,T)\,\mathrm d\tau\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $\rho(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau$ gives the number of channels with transmissions in the interval $[\tau,\tau+\mathrm d\tau]$. It is therefore a channel density rather than a normalized probability distribution. Its conductance normalization is $G_\mathrm N=G_0\int_0^1 \rho(\tau)\,\tau\,\mathrm d\tau$ \cite{beenakker_josephson_1991,beenakker_quantum_1992}.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{1.3in}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1.0em}
                \thesisplot{theory/meso}{ko-rho}
                \caption{
                    DMPK transmission density for a short diffusive wire.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:ko-rho}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            In the metallic diffusive limit, the transmission eigenvalues of a quasi-one-dimensional wire follow the bimodal Dorokhov--Mello--Pereyra--Kumar (DMPK) distribution \cite{dorokhov_transmission_1982,beenakker_random-matrix_1997},
            \begin{equation}
                \rho(\tau) = \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{G_0} \frac{1}{2\tau \sqrt{1-\tau}}\,,\quad 0<\tau<1\,,
                \label{eq:meso:dmpk}
            \end{equation}
            as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:ko-rho}. Its bimodal form represents a large population of almost-closed channels together with almost-open channels that contribute strongly to transport.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:meso:dmpk} gives the first Kulik--Omel'yanchuk (KO1) relation for a dirty diffusive contact. At $T=0$, the current is
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{KO1}(\phi) = \pi \frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}\Delta\,\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)\,\operatorname{artanh}\!\left(\sin\!\left(\phi/2\right)\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Although $\operatorname{artanh}[\sin(\phi/2)]$ diverges as $\phi\to\pi$, the factor $\cos(\phi/2)$ vanishes more rapidly, such that $I_\mathrm{KO1}(\phi)\to0$ \cite{kulik_contribution_1975,golubov_current-phase_2004}.

            The KO1 limit applies when the weak link itself is diffusive. It does not follow merely from diffusive transport in the superconducting electrodes.

            In the complementary clean ballistic limit, all modes are perfectly transmitting, $\tau_i=1$. Summing the ballistic single-channel result gives the second Kulik--Omel'yanchuk relation (KO2),
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{KO2}(\phi, T) = \pi\,\frac{G_\mathrm{N}}{e}\,\Delta(T)\,\sin\!\left(\frac{\phi}{2}\right)\,
                    \tanh\!\left(\frac{\Delta(T)\cos\!\left(\phi/2\right)}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\,.
            \end{equation}
            This clean ballistic result is the KO2 reference limit \cite{kulik_contribution_1975,golubov_current-phase_2004}. Channels with finite normal reflection, $\tau_i<1$, are instead described by the general sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-multichannel}. KO1 and KO2 therefore provide many-channel reference limits rather than generic models of an individual atomic contact, whose discrete channel set $\{\tau_i\}$ should normally be retained.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:abs-ko} compares the current--phase relations and critical currents for the Ambegaokar--Baratoff, KO1, and KO2 limits. In each case, $I_\mathrm{c}(T)$ is found by maximizing $I(\phi,T)$ over phase with a BCS-like gap $\Delta(T)$. The channel set or its continuum density determines the observable current--phase relation and critical current, connecting microscopic transmission eigenvalues to equilibrium measurements.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Fractional Shapiro Steps}
        %=========================================================

            The transmission dependent ABS spectrum also determines the harmonic content of the equilibrium current--phase relation:
            \begin{equation}
                I(\phi)=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty} I_p\sin(p\phi)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:cpr-harmonics}
            \end{equation}
            In the tunnel limit, the first harmonic $I_1$ dominates and the current is approximately sinusoidal. Highly transmitting channels produce the skewed current--phase relations shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:meso:abs}(b) and \ref{fig:meso:abs-ko}(a), which contain appreciable higher harmonics $I_{p>1}$.

            Under microwave irradiation, these harmonics introduce phase locking conditions beyond the integer Shapiro steps discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:shapiro}. We use the driven phase of Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:shapiro-phase}, with Josephson frequency $\nu_\mathrm{J}=2eV/h$ and drive amplitude $\alpha_{\nu,2e}=2eA/(h\nu)$. Inserting this phase into Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:cpr-harmonics} and applying the Jacobi--Anger expansion gives
            \begin{equation}
                I(t)=\sum_{p\geq1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}} I_pJ_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})
                \sin\!\left[p\phi_0+2\pi\left(p\nu_\mathrm{J}+n\nu\right)t\right]\,.
            \end{equation}
            The $p$-th CPR harmonic experiences the enlarged modulation $p\alpha_{\nu,2e}$, and its $n$-photon locking contribution is weighted by $I_pJ_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})$ rather than by a squared Bessel function. A dc contribution appears when $p\nu_\mathrm{J}+n\nu=0$. Relabeling the sideband index gives the rational locking voltages
            \begin{equation}
                V_{n/p}=\frac{n}{p}\frac{h\nu}{2e}\,,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad p\in\mathbb{N}^+\,.
            \end{equation}
            An appreciable second harmonic produces half-integer steps for odd $n$. Their visibility is expected to increase when highly transmitting channels contribute strongly to the supercurrent \cite{cuevas_subharmonic_2002,duprat_phase_2005,chauvin_superconducting_2006}.

            In an ideal overdamped junction whose response at $V_{n/p}$ is dominated by one CPR harmonic, the full locking interval in dc current is approximately
            \begin{equation}
                \Delta I_{n/p}\simeq2\left|I_pJ_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})\right|\,.
                \label{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width}
            \end{equation}
            The factor of two is the full interval obtained by varying the phase offset $\phi_0$. For $p=1$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width} reduces to the familiar integer-step modulation $\Delta I_n\simeq2|I_1J_n(\alpha_{\nu,2e})|$. The oscillations and zeros of $J_n(p\alpha_{\nu,2e})$ predict suppression and revival of a fractional plateau as the microwave amplitude is varied \cite{duprat_phase_2005}.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width} is a single-dominant-harmonic estimate, not a general addition rule. Several pairs $(p,n)$ can correspond to the same reduced rational voltage, and their phase-dependent contributions can interfere. Realistic plateau widths require the driven RSJ or RCSJ phase dynamics introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:rcsj} and also depend on damping, capacitance, temperature, noise, calibration of $A$, and nonequilibrium ABS occupation. Conventional higher harmonics of a $2\pi$-periodic current--phase relation already produce fractional steps. Their observation alone therefore does not establish a $4\pi$-periodic Josephson effect.

        In summary, repeated Andreev reflection quantizes the subgap spectrum of a short junction into transmission dependent ABS. Their phase dispersion and minimum gap determine the equilibrium current--phase relation, while the channel set fixes the total supercurrent. Highly transmitting channels generate higher CPR harmonics, which become observable through fractional Shapiro steps under microwave irradiation. The ABS description therefore connects microscopic channel transmissions to equilibrium supercurrent and measurements of phase locking.

        A finite dc voltage changes this regime by driving the superconducting phase continuously in time. Repeated Andreev reflection then produces nonequilibrium charge transport rather than stationary bound-state supercurrent, leading to multiple Andreev reflection.

    \subsection{Multiple Andreev Reflection}
    \label{subsec:meso:mar}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{figure}[t]
            \centering
            \subfigure[
                Second-order MAR
                ]{\import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-mar2.pdf_tex}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[
                Third-order MAR
                ]{\import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-mar3.pdf_tex}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[
                Fourth-order MAR
                ]{\import{theory/schema/semiconductor}{sis-mar4.pdf_tex}}
            \caption{
                Energy-ladder picture of multiple Andreev reflection for processes of order $m=2$, $3$, and $4$.
                Successive electron--hole conversions at the two superconducting interfaces raise the quasiparticle energy by $eV$ per traversal until it reaches the continuum.
                Lower voltage therefore requires a larger number of traversals to bridge the gap $2\Delta$.
                }
            \label{fig:meso:mar-schematic}
        \end{figure}

        Multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) is the microscopic mechanism of subgap transport in voltage-biased SNS weak links and short superconducting point contacts. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:mar-schematic} shows the corresponding energy ladder for several MAR orders. For $|eV|<2\Delta$, a quasiparticle cannot directly enter the continuum of the opposite electrode. Successive Andreev reflections instead alternate its electron- and hole-like character, while each traversal changes its energy by $eV$. In the trajectory picture of Klapwijk, Blonder, and Tinkham (KBT), a process that reaches the opposite continuum after $m$ energy increments has the threshold
        \begin{equation}
            eV_m=\frac{2\Delta}{m}\,,\qquad m\in\mathbb{N}^+\,.
            \label{eq:meso:mar-sgs}
        \end{equation}
        We refer to these voltages as MAR thresholds. Here $m$ labels the MAR order. In the energy-ladder picture, it counts the energy increments required to connect the continua. In the charge-resolved description, the corresponding process transfers the effective charge $q_m=me$. The MAR thresholds generate the subharmonic-gap structure observed as slope changes in \textit{I--V} and peaks or kinks in \textit{dI--dV} \cite{klapwijk_explanation_1982,octavio_subharmonic_1983}.

        The semiclassical OTBK model provides an important historical realization of this ladder picture and explains the origin of the subharmonic gap structure \cite{octavio_subharmonic_1983}. It neglects phase coherence and therefore does not provide the quantitative description required for short atomic contacts. For these contacts, we use the channel-resolved Hamiltonian approach of Cuevas \textit{et al.}~which treats coherent transport across the full transmission range.

        At high transmission, the dynamics can be viewed through the ABS spectrum. A dc bias drives the phase through the avoided crossing near $\phi=\pi$, where nonadiabatic Landau--Zener transitions can change the ABS branch occupation. This two-level picture is useful for a nearly ballistic channel, while the coherent transport model below treats the complete MAR current at arbitrary transmission \cite{averin_ac_1995}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Coherent Channel-Resolved Transport}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \subfigure[
                    MAR \textit{I--V} characteristic
                    ]{\thesisplot{theory/meso}{ha-iv}}
                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                    MAR \textit{dI--dV} characteristic
                    ]{\thesisplot{theory/meso}{ha-didv}}
                \caption{
                    Coherent MAR transport through a short single-channel contact at selected transmissions $\tau$.
                    (a) The subgap current increases strongly with $\tau$, and a finite excess current remains above the gap.
                    (b) The corresponding differential conductance displays subharmonic-gap features at the MAR thresholds of Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}, which broaden and overlap as the transmission increases.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:mar-ha}
            \end{figure}

            We now replace the trajectory picture by the coherent single-channel model used for quantitative dc MAR. A short single-channel contact is modeled as two bulk BCS reservoirs coupled by a local hopping amplitude \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996}. This is the finite-voltage continuation of the ABS model: at $V=0$ it produces the equilibrium spectrum discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:abs}, while a running phase generates coherent MAR.

            The junction Hamiltonian is written as
            \begin{align}
                \hat{H} &= \hat{H}_L + \hat{H}_R + \hat{H}_T(t)\,,\\
                \hat{H}_T(t) &= \sum_\sigma \left( t\,e^{+\ima \phi(t)/2}\,\hat{c}^\dagger_{L\sigma}\hat{c}_{R\sigma}
                + t\,e^{-\ima \phi(t)/2}\,\hat{c}^\dagger_{R\sigma}\hat{c}_{L\sigma}\right)\,.
            \end{align}
            This local-hopping Hamiltonian is the starting point of the coherent channel-resolved MAR calculation \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996}.

            The phase factors in $\hat{H}_T(t)$ incorporate the dc voltage through the $q=2e$ phase of Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-phase}. The resulting time-periodic problem is solved with Keldysh Green functions in Nambu space and a Floquet representation in energy. For one channel, the calculation returns $I(V,\tau,T,\Delta,\gamma)$ from the transmission $\tau$, gap $\Delta$, temperature $T$, broadening $\gamma$, and voltage $V$.


            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:mar-ha}(a) shows that increasing $\tau$ enhances the subgap current because successive traversals are less suppressed. The MAR thresholds remain fixed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}, but the structures overlap and the current approaches a smooth high-transmission response. Figure~\ref{fig:meso:mar-ha}(b) shows the same evolution in \textit{dI--dV}, where thresholds appear as peaks or kinks.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[10]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1.em}
                \thesisplot{theory/meso}{ha-excess}
                \vspace{-0.5em}
                \caption{
                    Normalized excess current versus channel transmission.
                    }
                \label{fig:meso:mar-excess}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            At voltages far above the gap, the superconducting current approaches the normal-state asymptote with a finite offset. This offset defines the excess current,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{exc}(\tau)
                =
                \lim_{V\to\infty}\left[I(V,\tau)-\tau G_0V\right]\,.
                \label{eq:meso:mar-excess-current}
            \end{equation}
            It results from Andreev processes and therefore depends nonlinearly on the channel transmission. For a short single-channel contact between identical BCS superconductors at zero temperature, the excess current has the analytical form
            \begin{align}
                I_\mathrm{exc}(\tau)
                ={}& \frac{G_0\Delta_0}{e}\frac{\tau^2}{1-\tau}
                \left[
                    1-
                    \frac{\tau^2}{2(2-\tau)\sqrt{1-\tau}}
                    \ln\!\left(
                        \frac{1+\sqrt{1-\tau}}{1-\sqrt{1-\tau}}
                    \right)
                \right]\,.
                \label{eq:meso:mar-excess-analytical}
            \end{align}
            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:mar-excess} shows that the normalized excess current approaches $8/3$ in the ballistic limit $\tau\to1$ \cite{senkpiel_extracting_2022}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Multichannel Atomic Contacts}
        %=========================================================

            The single-channel result becomes experimentally useful when it is combined with the mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$ of an atomic contact. Independent channels contribute additively to the coherent dc current,
            \begin{equation}
                I(V,\{\tau_i\},T,\Delta,\gamma)
                =
                \sum_i I(V,\tau_i,T,\Delta,\gamma)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Each transmission produces a characteristic \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} line shape because MAR probabilities depend differently on $\tau_i$. Their sum therefore remains sensitive to the mesoscopic pincode. This transmission sensitivity makes nonlinear SIS spectra a channel-resolved probe of atomic contacts \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996,scheer_conduction_1997}.

            The normal-state conductance determines only the total transmission, so different mesoscopic pincodes can have the same $G_\mathrm{N}$. Their nonlinear SIS spectra can nevertheless differ and can be fitted to extract the pincode within the coherent short-contact model. Channels with the same transmission produce identical spectral contributions and cannot be distinguished individually. The fitting procedure is described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Charge-Resolved Multiple Andreev Reflection}
        %=========================================================

            To connect dc MAR to charge transfer, we use full counting statistics as a process-resolved extension of the coherent MAR calculation \cite{levitov_charge_1993,levitov_electron_1996,cuevas_full_2003}. Here we use it only to decompose the dc current into contributions associated with transfer of an effective charge $q=me$,
            \begin{equation}
                I(V)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} I_m(V)\,.
                \label{eq:meso:fcs-decomposition}
            \end{equation}
            The index $m$ is the MAR order introduced in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}, and $I_m(V)$ denotes the contribution associated with transfer of the effective charge $q_m=me$. Several MAR orders can contribute at the same bias, so $m$ characterizes an individual transport process rather than the total current \cite{cuevas_full_2003}.

            Figure~\ref{fig:meso:fcs} shows this redistribution for three transmissions. Near the gap edge, low-order processes carry most of the current. Lower bias requires larger transfer orders, although these processes are strongly suppressed in the tunneling regime. As $\tau$ increases, repeated traversal becomes more probable and several charge-transfer orders acquire appreciable weight.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Photon-Assisted Multiple Andreev Reflection}
        %=========================================================

            Irradiated MAR is more complex than dc MAR because a voltage-biased superconducting contact already contains the Josephson frequency $\nu_J=2eV/h$. A microwave drive at frequency $\nu$ adds a second time scale. A useful phenomenological description is the unified charge-dependent Tien--Gordon expression. Soerensen \textit{et al.}~introduced this viewpoint for microwave-modified subgap structure, and Carrad \textit{et al.}~used the charge-dependent sideband spacing to identify high-order MAR processes \cite{soerensen_microwave_1974,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. If the dc current is decomposed as $I(V)=\sum_m I_m(V)$ and each component is assigned the effective charge $q_m=me$, the expression reads
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{PAMAR}(V,A)
                =
                \sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}^+}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
                J_n^2\left(\frac{meA}{h\nu}\right)\,
                I_m\!\left(V+\frac{nh\nu}{me}\right)\,,
                \label{eq:meso:pamar-current}
            \end{equation}
            where $A$ is the effective microwave voltage amplitude at the junction and $n$ labels the photon sideband. The corresponding replicas of the MAR thresholds appear approximately at
            \begin{equation}
                eV_{m,n}\simeq\frac{2\Delta+nh\nu}{m}\,,
                \qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}\,.
            \end{equation}
            Photon-assisted MAR can identify the effective charge order associated with a subgap feature, complementing the dc charge resolution in Fig.~\ref{fig:meso:fcs-m} \cite{soerensen_microwave_1974,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:meso:pamar-current} does not determine how the measured or calculated dc current should be separated into the components $I_m(V)$. The FCS currents of Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:fcs-decomposition} are a natural candidate because they resolve transfer events by charge. Their independent Tien--Gordon dressing is nevertheless an additional assumption, not a consequence of the dc FCS theory. In Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}, we test this candidate and find that it does not reproduce the measured sideband pattern. We therefore use a phenomenological threshold-based decomposition for the practical analysis.

            The microscopic reference for irradiated coherent MAR is the two-frequency Keldysh--Floquet theory developed by Cuevas \textit{et al.}~for superconducting point contacts of arbitrary transmission \cite{cuevas_subharmonic_2002}. It treats the Josephson oscillation and the external microwave drive within the same calculation and therefore includes coherent interference between driven MAR paths. The unified Tien--Gordon expression remains an interpretable approximation for sideband positions and practical modeling, but it does not replace the double-Floquet theory.

        In summary, the transmission eigenvalues provide a common description of mesoscopic superconducting transport. Andreev reflection determines the interface process, ABS describe the equilibrium spectrum and supercurrent, and MAR governs finite-bias subgap transport. Channel-resolved models thereby connect the mesoscopic pincode to measured \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} characteristics.

        We now turn from phase coherent channel transport to the stochastic regime, where voltage fluctuations and energy exchange with the electromagnetic environment determine the charge-transfer dynamics.
