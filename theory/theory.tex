\chapter{Superconducting Transport}
\label{ch:superconducting-transport}
%=========================================================

    Superconductivity was first observed by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911, when the electrical resistance of mercury vanished abruptly upon cooling below 4.2\,K. Shortly thereafter, the Meissner--Ochsenfeld effect revealed that superconductors also expel magnetic fields, establishing the two defining macroscopic properties of this quantum state of matter, perfect conductivity and perfect diamagnetism. \cite{onnes_further_1991, meissner_neuer_1933}

    At its core, superconductivity originates from an effective attraction between electrons near the Fermi surface. When an electron moves through the metal, it slightly distorts the ion lattice, creating a transient region of enhanced positive charge. A second electron can be attracted to this distortion, resulting in a weak but cooperative pairing mechanism mediated by lattice vibrations. Despite the underlying Coulomb repulsion, this mechanism allows electrons with opposite momentum and spin to form Cooper pairs. When many such pairs form simultaneously, they condense into a coherent macroscopic quantum state capable of carrying electrical current without resistance.

    The remainder of this chapter develops superconducting transport from four complementary viewpoints. These descriptions emphasize different degrees of freedom: quasiparticle spectra, condensate phase dynamics, transmission channels, and environmental fluctuations. Together, they provide a consistent framework for the transport regimes and observables studied experimentally. The models are introduced in general form, while aluminum provides the experimental material platform and the concrete scales used for numerical examples. Its material parameters are introduced locally when they become relevant. Unless stated otherwise or varied explicitly, all numerical simulations in this chapter use aluminum parameters, zero temperature, and zero Dynes broadening, $T=\gamma=0$. Irradiated simulations use a drive frequency of $\nu=10\,\mathrm{GHz}$.

    The microscopic description introduces Bardeen--Cooper--Schrieffer theory. It describes pairing in the weak-coupling limit, the resulting energy gap and quasiparticle density of states, and their signatures in tunneling spectroscopy and photon-assisted transport.

    The macroscopic description treats the order-parameter phase as the relevant dynamical variable in low-transmission weak links. This leads to the Josephson relations, microwave-induced Shapiro steps, and the resistively and capacitively shunted junction model used to interpret measured \textit{I--V} characteristics.

    The mesoscopic description connects the quasiparticle spectrum to phase-coherent scattering at finite transmission. Andreev reflection, Andreev bound states, and multiple Andreev reflection then govern subgap transport, and the resulting \textit{I--V} characteristics encode the transmission eigenvalues of the contact.

    Finally, the stochastic description treats energy exchange and phase fluctuations produced by the electromagnetic environment. The \textit{P(E)} framework describes how the surrounding impedance modifies tunneling rates and the measured \textit{I--V} characteristics.

    The applicable description is selected by four main criteria: conductor size and coherence, junction transparency, external drive, and coupling to the electromagnetic environment. Before treating the four superconducting descriptions in detail, Sec.~\ref{sec:quasi} introduces normal-state quasiparticles, bulk transport, and the coherent scattering framework used to distinguish transport regimes. The microscopic description then introduces superconducting spectral scales and the common electromagnetic-drive convention used throughout the later driven-transport sections.
