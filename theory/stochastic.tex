%=========================================================
\section{Stochastic Transport}
\label{sec:stochastic}
%=========================================================

    The stochastic transport description applies when fluctuations of the electromagnetic environment destroy phase memory between successive tunneling events. The voltage across the junction then contains a fluctuating contribution $\delta V(t)$ around its deterministic bias value. This fluctuation produces a random phase accumulation and allows the environment to absorb or supply energy during a tunneling event. Transport is therefore described by transition probabilities and charge state occupations rather than by deterministic phase evolution.

    The crossover from phase coherent to stochastic transport follows from the conjugacy of the superconducting phase difference $\phi$ and the junction charge operator $Q$,
    \begin{equation}
        \left[\phi, Q\right] = 2e\ima\,,
    \end{equation}
    so charge localization is accompanied by fluctuations of the condensate phase difference. More generally, a tunneling process that transfers charge $q$ acquires the fluctuating phase
    \begin{equation}
        \delta \vartheta_q(t) = \frac{q}{\hbar}\int_0^t \delta V(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'\,.
    \end{equation}
    Here $\delta V(t)$ is the fluctuating part of the voltage across the junction relative to its mean bias value. For Cooper-pair transfer, $q=2e$, and $\delta\vartheta_{2e}$ is the fluctuating contribution to the condensate phase difference obtained from the ac Josephson relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:josephson-voltage-phase}. For single-electron tunneling, $\delta\vartheta_e$ instead multiplies the single-electron tunneling amplitude. It represents the environmental phase acquired by transferring charge $e$ and is not an independent superconducting condensate phase \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

    Strong phase fluctuations suppress phase memory. They broaden coherent Josephson and Andreev processes. When independent tunneling events dominate, their rates are governed by energy exchange with the environment. This crossover depends on the frequency dependent impedance, junction parameters, temperature, and transferred charge.

    In this incoherent limit, \textit{P(E)} theory gives the probability density for energy exchange between a discrete tunneling event and the environment. We first construct this environmental kernel, then apply it to dynamical Coulomb blockade of single-electron tunneling, incoherent Cooper-pair tunneling and its photon assisted counterpart, and superconducting single-electron transistors.

        The circuit impedance determines the voltage fluctuations seen by the junction. These fluctuations produce the phase correlator and the energy exchange probability that dresses the tunneling rate. The label $q$ denotes the net charge transferred in one tunneling event, for example $q=e$ for quasiparticle tunneling and $q=2e$ for Cooper-pair transfer. Because the phase acquired from a voltage fluctuation is proportional to $q$, the environmental coupling in the phase correlator scales as $q^2$. We use the environmental tunneling formulation of Falci \textit{et al.} and the conventions summarized by Ingold and Nazarov \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        The relevant impedance is the effective impedance $Z(\omega)$ seen by the junction rather than the impedance of an isolated circuit element. For a junction capacitance $C$ in parallel with an environmental impedance $Z_\mathrm{env}(\omega)$, it is
        \begin{equation}
            Z(\omega)=\left[\ima\omega C+Z_\mathrm{env}^{-1}(\omega)\right]^{-1}\,.
        \end{equation}
        The junction capacitance suppresses the high-frequency response and supplies a physical cutoff. For a more complex circuit, $Z(\omega)$ denotes the corresponding network impedance evaluated at the junction terminals \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        The effective impedance sets the spectrum of voltage fluctuations. Since voltage fluctuations integrate into the tunneling phase, the impedance enters through the charge dependent phase correlation function
        \begin{equation}
            J_q(t) = \frac{2q^2}{h} \int_0^\infty
            \underbrace{\vphantom{\coth\!\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\left(\cos(\omega t) - 1\right)}\frac{\mathrm{Re}\,Z(\omega)}{\textstyle \omega}}_{\text{coupling}}
            \left[
            \underbrace{\coth\!\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\left(\cos(\omega t) - 1\right)}_{\text{diffusion}}
            -
            \underbrace{\vphantom{\coth\!\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)\left(\cos(\omega t) - 1\right)}\ima\sin(\omega t)}_{\text{winding}}
            \right]\mathrm{d}\omega\,,
            \label{eq:stochastic:phase-correlation}
        \end{equation}
        This equilibrium correlator follows from the fluctuation spectrum of a linear impedance \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        Equation~\eqref{eq:stochastic:phase-correlation} can be read from left to right through the three annotated factors. The coupling factor gives the dissipative environmental spectrum seen by the junction. The division by frequency appears because phase is the time integral of voltage, making low-frequency noise especially effective. The diffusion factor is the real part of the correlator and describes loss of phase memory. The winding factor is the imaginary part and tracks the deterministic phase shift generated by the environment.

        The energy exchange probability is obtained by Fourier transforming this phase correlator,
        \begin{equation}
            P_q(E) = \frac{1}{h} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\left(J_q(t) + \ima E t/\hbar\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,.
            \label{eq:stochastic:pe-definition}
        \end{equation}
        A narrow peak near zero energy describes nearly elastic tunneling. A broad or structured distribution produces suppressed onsets, shifted thresholds, and satellites in measured \textit{I--V} and \textit{dI--dV} \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        For a passive linear environment in thermal equilibrium, this probability density is nonnegative, normalized, and obeys detailed balance,
        \begin{equation}
            P_q(E)\geq0\,, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_q(E)\,\mathrm{d}E=1\,, \qquad P_q(-E)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{E}{k_\mathrm{B}T}\right)P_q(E)\,.
            \label{eq:stochastic:pe-equilibrium}
        \end{equation}
        Detailed balance fixes the relative probabilities for environmental emission and absorption. A coherently driven or nonequilibrium environment does not generally satisfy this relation and requires an explicitly time dependent or nonequilibrium kernel \cite{ingold_charge_1992}.

        The direct connection to transport is obtained by separating the environmental and electronic parts of the tunneling process. The general forward rate is
        \begin{equation}
            \Gamma_q^{\rightarrow}(V)
            = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P_q(E)F_q^{\rightarrow}(E,V)\,\mathrm{d}E\,.
            \label{eq:stochastic:pe-tunnel-rate}
        \end{equation}
        The electronic kernel $F_q^{\rightarrow}(E,V)$ is the bare tunneling rate for a process in which the environment absorbs the fixed energy $E$. It contains the electrode DOS, Fermi occupations, tunneling matrix elements, bias dependence, and the initial and final electronic states of the process. Its form distinguishes quasiparticle tunneling, incoherent Cooper-pair tunneling, and incoherent Andreev reflection, while $P_q(E)$ describes the environment. The reverse rate has the same structure with the corresponding reverse electronic kernel. The net current is
        \begin{equation}
            I(V)=q\left[\Gamma_q^{\rightarrow}(V)-\Gamma_q^{\leftarrow}(V)\right]\,.
            \label{eq:stochastic:pe-rate-current}
        \end{equation}
        This factorization separates the electronic tunneling kernel from environmental energy exchange \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        Thus $P_q(E)$ redistributes the available energy, while the electrode DOS and occupations remain in $F_q(E,V)$. Environmental dressing changes transport characteristics without redefining the electronic DOS, which is the key distinction from phenomenological broadening.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Elastic Limit}
        %=========================================================
            The elastic limit is the weak-coupling limit $\alpha_q\to0$ of the Ohmic family. When the impedance is much smaller than the relevant resistance quantum over the frequencies involved in tunneling, voltage and phase fluctuations are weak and the environment exchanges little energy with the transferred charge. The energy exchange probability approaches a delta function,
            \begin{equation}
                P_\mathrm{low}(E) \approx \delta(E)\,, \qquad \mathrm{Re}\,Z(\omega) \ll \frac{h}{q^2}\,.
            \end{equation}
            In the limit $P_q(E)=\delta(E)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-tunnel-rate} reduces to
            \begin{equation}
                \Gamma_q^{\rightarrow}(V)=F_q^{\rightarrow}(0,V)\,.
                \label{eq:stochastic:elastic-rate}
            \end{equation}
            The environment exchanges no energy with the tunneling charge. For single-electron tunneling, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:elastic-rate} recovers the elastic microscopic tunnel rate introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:tunnel-current}. A finite but small $\alpha_q$ broadens the delta peak and introduces weak inelastic corrections \cite{ingold_charge_1992}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Finite Ohmic Coupling}
        %=========================================================

            As the Ohmic coupling increases, phase fluctuations broaden the elastic peak into a continuous energy exchange spectrum. A simple zero-temperature cutoff model gives the normalized form
            \begin{equation}
                P_\mathrm{Ohmic}(E)
                = \frac{1}{E_\mathrm{c}\Gamma(\alpha_q)}
                \left(\frac{E}{E_\mathrm{c}}\right)^{\alpha_q-1}
                \exp\!\left(-\frac{E}{E_\mathrm{c}}\right)\,,
                \quad
                \alpha_q=\frac{2q^2R}{h}\,,
                \quad
                E>0\,.
                \label{eq:stochastic:pe-ohmic}
            \end{equation}

            The cutoff energy is $E_\mathrm{c}=\hbar\omega_\mathrm{c}$. The Euler Gamma function $\Gamma(\alpha_q)$ normalizes the distribution. The power law describes the universal Ohmic low-energy behavior, while the exponential factor regularizes the high-energy tail of an environment with finite bandwidth. Equation~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-ohmic} is therefore a convenient cutoff model and not the exact kernel of every Ohmic circuit \cite{ingold_charge_1992}.

            At zero temperature this form has support only for $E>0$, because the environment can absorb energy but cannot emit energy from its ground state. At finite temperature, thermal modes add a negative-energy tail to $P(E)$, corresponding to energy emitted by the environment into the tunneling process. The full finite-temperature kernel is usually evaluated from Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-definition}. The low-energy suppression of $P(E)$ underlies dynamical Coulomb blockade and also sets the scale for incoherent Cooper-pair tunneling.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Resonant Environment}
        %=========================================================

            If the impedance contains discrete resonances from inductive elements or cavity modes, the real part of the impedance acquires sharp features at the corresponding frequencies. These features imprint themselves onto $P(E)$, which develops additional weight at the energies of the environmental modes.

            The simplest analytic case is a single harmonic mode at frequency $\omega_0$ added to an Ohmic background. A tunneling event linearly displaces this oscillator. For a mode initially in its ground state, the number of excited oscillator quanta is Poisson distributed, which gives
            \begin{equation}
                P_\mathrm{resonant}(E) = e^{-\alpha_{0,q}} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha_{0,q}^{\,n}}{n!}\, P_\mathrm{Ohmic}(E - n\hbar\omega_0)\,,
            \end{equation}
            where $\alpha_{0,q}$ quantifies the coupling between charge $q$ and the resonant mode. The shifted kernels describe tunneling events accompanied by excitation of $n$ mode quanta. The Poisson weights are not a generic property of any peak in $Z(\omega)$, but a consequence of a linearly coupled harmonic mode. Damping, thermal occupation, nonlinearities, or strong driving modify the sideband weights and line shapes. In the measured current, this structured $P_q(E)$ dresses the electronic kernel in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-tunnel-rate} and produces satellites whose voltage spacing depends on the transferred charge \cite{ingold_charge_1992}.

        Figure~\ref{fig:stochastic:pe} summarizes the three environmental limits introduced above. Weak coupling concentrates the probability near zero exchanged energy. Ohmic coupling broadens this contribution and suppresses low-energy tunneling, while a resonant mode produces discrete inelastic contributions. After convolution with the electronic kernel, these structures appear in measured transport as zero-bias suppression, broadened thresholds, and resonant satellites.

        The transferred charge selects the environmental kernel because $J_q(t)\propto q^2$. Dynamical Coulomb blockade uses $P_e(E)$ to describe environmental dressing of single-electron tunneling. Incoherent Cooper-pair tunneling uses $P_{2e}(E)$ and is therefore more sensitive to the same impedance. In a superconducting single-electron transistor, these charge specific rates connect different island charge states. A master equation then combines the rates with their stationary occupation probabilities.

        Dynamical Coulomb blockade (DCB) applies $P_e(E)$ to the elastic electronic kernel from the microscopic tunnel description. Figure~\ref{fig:stochastic:dcb-schematic} shows the energy balance for an SIS tunneling event. The electron creates quasiparticle excitations in the electrodes. At zero temperature, any energy not carried by the final electronic states must be absorbed by the environment. At finite temperature, thermally occupied environmental modes can also emit energy into the tunneling process. If $P_e(E)$ is small at the required exchange energy, this channel is suppressed \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        A single electron crossing a junction with capacitance $C$ changes its electrostatic energy by an amount of order $E_\mathrm{C}=e^2/2C$. The environment carries this energy away through its voltage fluctuations. Small low-energy weight in $P_e(E)$ therefore appears as a zero-bias suppression in \textit{dI--dV} \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        The environmental dressing is most cleanly written for directional tunneling branches. The total current is obtained after dressing both directions,
        \begin{align}
            I(V) &= I_{1\to 2}(V) - I_{2\to 1}(V)\,,\\
            I_{1\to 2}(V) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P_e(\varepsilon)\, I_{1\to 2}^{(0)}\!\left(V - \frac{\varepsilon}{e}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\,,\\
            I_{2\to 1}(V) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} P_e(\varepsilon)\, I_{2\to 1}^{(0)}\!\left(-V - \frac{\varepsilon}{e}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\,.
        \end{align}
        Here $I_{i\to j}^{(0)}$ is the corresponding elastic branch without environmental energy exchange. It contains the electrode density of states, occupation factors, and tunneling matrix element for that direction, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:tunnel-1to2}. This branch-wise form makes the voltage symmetry transparent. For a passive symmetric junction in equilibrium, the negative-bias current is the sign-reversed positive-bias current even though the zero-temperature kernel has support only for positive exchanged energy. The environment absorbs energy according to the favored tunneling direction at either voltage polarity \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Photon Assisted Dynamical Coulomb Blockade}
        %=========================================================

            The photon assisted tunneling mechanism and its Bessel weights were derived in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}. For DCB, the same time averaged dressing is applied to the complete undriven current $I_{\mathrm{DCB},0}(V)$, which already contains environmental energy exchange. The resulting photon assisted DCB current is
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{PADCB}(V_0) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} J_n^2\!\left(\alpha_{\nu,e}\right) I_{\mathrm{DCB},0}\!\left(V_0 - \frac{nh\nu}{e}\right)\,,\qquad
                \alpha_{\nu,e} = \frac{eA}{h\nu}\,.
                \label{eq:stochastic:padcb}
            \end{equation}
            This current replica formulation applies to coherently driven tunnel junctions in the stationary DCB approximation \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,grabert_dynamical_2015,parlavecchio_fluctuation_2015,houle_photon-assisted_2019}. Here $A$ is the effective ac voltage amplitude after environmental filtering and voltage division. The replicas are separated by $h\nu/e$.

            Equation~\eqref{eq:stochastic:padcb} assumes weak tunneling, a coherent classical drive, and an environment whose response can be absorbed into the stationary DCB characteristic and effective amplitude $A$. It neglects drive induced changes of the DOS, heating, nonequilibrium electrode occupations, and nonlinear environmental backaction.

            Houle \textit{et al.} found that current and photon assisted noise required different effective ac amplitudes. The stationary replica model therefore misses part of the driven junction and environment dynamics and is limited as a quantitative microwave calibration~\cite{houle_photon-assisted_2019}.

            Figures~\ref{fig:stochastic:dcb-resonant} and~\ref{fig:stochastic:dcb-pat} compare passive resonant and photon assisted DCB in an SIS junction. Both generate satellites separated by $\hbar\omega_0=h\nu$, but passive modes produce Poisson weighted inelastic channels and coherent drives produce Bessel weighted current replicas. At zero temperature, passive mode satellites lie on the higher-$|V|$ side of the quasiparticle threshold because the mode can only absorb energy. A coherent drive permits absorption and stimulated emission, creating replicas on both sides. Thermal mode occupation weakens this distinction through detailed balance.

            DCB and ICPT are two charge specific consequences of the same environmental interaction. Small low-energy weight in $P_e(E)$ suppresses stochastic $q=e$ tunneling, while finite-energy weight in $P_{2e}(E)$ enables the inelastic $q=2e$ transfer discussed next.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Dynamical Coulomb Blockade of Multiple Andreev Reflection}
        %=========================================================

            The tunnel junction treatment above assigns one elementary charge to each stochastic event. MAR instead consists of correlated electron and hole traversals through a finite-transmission superconducting contact. Its charge resolved description in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:mar} associates a transfer of effective charge $q=me$ with each current component $I_m(V)$. This suggests an enhanced environmental sensitivity at low voltage because the phase correlator in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:phase-correlation} scales with $q^2$. However, DCB of MAR is not generally obtained by independently convolving every $I_m(V)$ with a kernel $P_{me}(E)$. The traversals within one MAR process remain coherent, different charge transfer orders overlap at a given voltage, and the environment can also renormalize the elastic MAR probabilities.

            Levy Yeyati, Cuevas, and Martín-Rodero calculate the leading environmental correction for a superconducting quantum point contact coupled to a weak macroscopic impedance \cite{levy_yeyati_dynamical_2005}. In the low-transmission limit, the relative blockade of an $m$th-order process is enhanced approximately as $m^2$, consistent with the environmental coupling to a transferred charge $me$. At higher transmission, Pauli correlations reduce this enhancement and elastic renormalization becomes important. The correction can then change sign in parts of the subgap region, so environmental coupling enhances rather than suppresses the MAR current. This antiblockade shows that the effective charge alone does not determine the environmental correction.

            The experimental work by Cron \textit{et al.} established the two ingredients separately. Shot noise measurements in superconducting atomic contacts resolved the enhanced effective charge associated with MAR, while normal-state measurements on the same class of contacts showed that DCB decreases as a channel approaches perfect transmission \cite{cron_multiple-charge-quanta_2001,cron_contacts_2001}. The latter experiment used a characterized series impedance and provided the platform proposed by Levy Yeyati \textit{et al.} for testing their superconducting prediction. It did not, however, measure DCB of MAR. Cron's thesis explicitly identifies the blockade of MAR processes as an open problem.

            The expected observables are changes in subgap current and in the amplitude and line shape of the MAR structures in \textit{dI--dV}. Their interpretation requires comparison with a coherent MAR reference at the same channel transmissions. A suppression near a MAR threshold is not by itself evidence for DCB because temperature, lifetime broadening, voltage noise, and imperfect knowledge of the pincode can produce similar changes. Conversely, environmental enhancement between thresholds would be missed by a model that treats DCB only as a positive charging energy cost.

            In summary, DCB of MAR couples environmental fluctuations to coherent multiparticle transfer through a finite-transmission contact. The low-transmission effective charge picture explains the enhanced blockade, but it is incomplete once several MAR orders overlap and elastic renormalization becomes important. A quantitative interpretation must therefore retain the channel transmissions, coherent MAR dynamics, and electromagnetic environment together. The resulting transmission dependent blockade and possible antiblockade distinguish this problem from a simple charging energy shift or an independent dressing of each charge order. A complete quantitative theory that treats arbitrary channel transmissions, overlapping MAR orders, and environmental backaction on equal footing remains an open problem.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Incoherent Cooper Pair Tunneling (ICPT)}
    \label{subsec:stochastic:icpt}
    %=========================================================

        In a voltage-biased SIS junction, environmental phase fluctuations can suppress coherent Josephson transport while retaining discrete Cooper-pair transfer with $q=2e$. The Josephson coupling energy then enters perturbatively at second order, and the environment supplies or absorbs the energy associated with each pair-transfer event.

        The standard forward and backward incoherent Cooper pair tunneling (ICPT) rates follow directly from the charge-$2e$ kernel introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:stochastic:pe-theory},
        \begin{equation}
            \Gamma_\mathrm{ICPT}^{\rightarrow/\leftarrow}(V) = \frac{\pi E_\mathrm{J}^{2}}{2\hbar}P_{2e}(\pm 2eV)\,.
        \end{equation}
        where the upper sign describes forward pair transfer and the lower sign describes backward transfer \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}.

        For an equilibrium environment, the current can therefore be written as
        \begin{equation}
            I_\mathrm{ICPT}(V)
            = \frac{\pi eE_\mathrm{J}^{2}}{\hbar}\left[1-\exp(-2eV/k_\mathrm{B}T)\right]P_{2e}(2eV)\,.
            \label{eq:stochastic:icpt:current}
        \end{equation}
        This form follows from the rate difference and the equilibrium detailed-balance relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-equilibrium} \cite{ingold_charge_1992}.

        The voltage axis therefore samples the environmental kernel through $E=2eV$. For an Ohmic environment, its low-energy behavior determines the low-bias ICPT line shape. A resonant mode produces satellites whenever $2eV=n\hbar\omega_0$, so neighboring features are separated by $\hbar\omega_0/(2e)$ in voltage.

        This perturbative description assumes weak $E_\mathrm{J}$, statistically independent pair-transfer events, and a linear equilibrium environment. It therefore predicts charge-$2e$ environmental sidebands, but not coherent Shapiro locking.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Photon-Assisted Incoherent Cooper Pair Tunneling}
        %=========================================================

            A coherent microwave drive adds photon-assisted replicas of the stationary ICPT characteristic. Using the PAT result from Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}, the effective charge $2e$ gives
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{PAICPT}(V_0)
                = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}J_n^2\!\left(\alpha_{\nu,2e}\right)
                I_\mathrm{ICPT}^{(0)}\!\left(V_0-\frac{nh\nu}{2e}\right)\,,
                \qquad \alpha_{\nu,2e}=\frac{2eA}{h\nu}\,.
            \end{equation}
            The squared Bessel factor is the probability for an incoherent pair-transfer event to exchange $|n|$ drive photons, producing replicas separated by $h\nu/(2e)$ \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,roychowdhury_microwave_2015}.

            This differs from the coherent Josephson response in Sec.~\ref{subsec:macro:shapiro}, where Bessel coefficients enter linearly at the amplitude level and determine frequency-locked Shapiro steps. Linear and squared Bessel dependences describe the coherent and incoherent limits. In the intermediate phase-diffusion regime, broadened Shapiro features and stochastic sidebands can coexist, so neither limit is complete. Their common spacing reflects the transferred charge $2e$, while the weights distinguish probabilistic tunneling from phase locking. Photon-assisted ICPT with squared-Bessel weights has been observed in Josephson scanning tunneling microscopy~\cite{roychowdhury_microwave_2015}.

        Incoherent Andreev reflection is a related but microscopically distinct $q=2e$ process at an NS interface. It couples to the same $P_{2e}(E)$ kernel because the net transferred charge is identical, but its electronic kernel describes second-order electron tunneling between a normal-metal continuum and a superconducting condensate. We mention it for completeness, but it is not a central transport mechanism here.

        In summary, $P_{2e}(E)$ governs stochastic Cooper-pair transfer and fixes the voltage spacing of its environmental and photon-assisted satellites. Squared Bessel weights characterize the fully incoherent microwave limit and distinguish it from phase-locked Josephson transport.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Superconducting Single-Electron Transistors}
    \label{subsec:stochastic:sset}
    %=========================================================

        A superconducting single-electron transistor combines charge quantization with quasiparticle and Cooper-pair transport. We first establish the conventional tunnel-junction device and its charge-state master equation, then discuss tunable and hybrid extensions \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Conventional Tunnel-Junction SETs}
        %=========================================================

            A conventional SET consists of a metallic island connected to source and drain by two tunnel junctions and coupled capacitively to a gate. The barriers permit charge quantization, while the gate shifts the charge-state energies, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:stochastic:set}(a). Replacing the normal metals by superconductors gives the conventional SSET.

                \centering

                    SET circuit schematic

                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                    Sequential tunneling

Blue and gray indicate occupied and empty states, while arrows indicate single-electron transfer.
                    }

            \end{figure}

            Adding one electron to an isolated metallic island requires electrostatic energy. For junction capacitances $C_1$ and $C_2$ and gate capacitance $C_\mathrm{G}$, the charging energy is
            \begin{equation}
                E_\mathrm{C} = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}\,, \qquad C_\Sigma = C_1 + C_2 + C_\mathrm{G}\,.
                \label{eq:stochastic:charging-energy}
            \end{equation}
            This is the orthodox charging scale for the lumped-capacitance island model \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

            At finite bias, the junction capacitances determine how strongly the applied voltage changes the island energy for each tunneling step. A transport onset occurs when the initial and final free energies become degenerate. The gate and bias dependence of this condition produces lines in a gate--bias map, with slopes fixed by capacitance ratios. The transition rates and stationary occupations determine the intensity along these lines.

            Charge quantization requires weak thermal activation and weak quantum charge fluctuations. Thus $E_\mathrm{C}\gg k_\mathrm{B}T$, and in the tunnel limit each normal-state junction resistance must be large compared with $R_\mathrm{K}=h/e^2$. The island can then be described by an integer excess-electron number $n$. A gate voltage induces the offset charge $n_\mathrm{G}=C_\mathrm{G}V_\mathrm{G}/e$ and changes the electrostatic energy according to
            \begin{equation}
                E_n(V_\mathrm{G}) = E_\mathrm{C}\left(n-n_\mathrm{G}\right)^2\,.
            \end{equation}
            The expression omits a charge-independent constant and assumes that other induced charges are absorbed into $n_\mathrm{G}$ \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

            Neighboring charge states become degenerate at half-integer gate charge. Between these points, one state has the lowest electrostatic energy and charge transfer costs energy. This gate-controlled cost is the origin of static Coulomb blockade \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

            In normal-state sequential transport, an electron enters through one junction and leaves through the other, restoring the initial island charge. A dc current flows when both steps are energetically favorable, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:stochastic:set}(b). The gate opens or closes this cycle by shifting the charge-state energies.

            If either step is inaccessible, the sequential current is blocked, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:stochastic:set}(c). Conductance is largest near charge degeneracy and periodic in gate-induced charge. Far from degeneracy, weaker cotunneling can bypass the intermediate charge state \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

            We now restore superconductivity. In a conventional SSET, the island and both leads are superconducting, while the two junctions remain in the tunnel limit. The electrostatic charge states remain those of the normal SET, but the gap changes which transitions are available. Quasiparticle tunneling changes the island charge by one elementary charge and requires occupied and empty quasiparticle states. Cooper-pair tunneling changes the island charge by two elementary charges and connects charge states of the same parity. The measured current can therefore contain competing single-electron and pair-transfer cycles.

            The superconducting island distinguishes even and odd electron number. An odd charge state contains an unpaired quasiparticle and carries an additional free-energy cost governed by the gap. At low temperature, this parity effect favors even charge states and can change the gate periodicity from $e$-periodic to $2e$-periodic. Nonequilibrium quasiparticles weaken this distinction and can restore single-electron periodicity. Here we retain parity as an energy cost for odd charge states rather than develop a separate quasiparticle-population model \cite{grabert_single_2013}.

            Quasiparticle and Cooper-pair steps can combine into a JQP cycle, where JQP denotes Josephson--quasiparticle transport. A Cooper pair first tunnels resonantly through one junction and changes the island charge by two elementary charges. Two quasiparticle transitions then restore the initial island charge while transferring net charge through the device. Because the pair transition is resonant, the JQP cycle produces a localized feature in bias and gate voltage \cite{grabert_single_2013}.

            Treating the resonant pair step as an incoherent rate is controlled when dephasing or lifetime broadening destroys coherence between the coupled charge states faster than the Josephson coupling can establish it. If $E_\mathrm{J}/\hbar$ becomes comparable to the dephasing rate, off-diagonal density-matrix elements must be retained and a generalized master equation replaces the population-only equation below.

            Orthodox theory describes the island by classical charge-state probabilities and independent junction transitions. This population-only description assumes well-defined charge states, Markovian events, and negligible off-diagonal coherences. The available transition energy is the initial free energy minus the final free energy, including work by the bias source. At zero temperature, a positive value permits the transition, while a negative value requires thermal activation or environmental energy supplied through $P(E)$. Each junction and direction contributes a rate set by this energy and the microscopic junction properties. In an SSET, these rates include the superconducting DOS and incoherent Cooper-pair transfer. \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}

            Let $\Gamma_{n\rightarrow m}$ denote the total rate for all junction processes that change the island from charge state $n$ to charge state $m$. In the stationary state, probability flow into every charge state balances probability flow out of it,
            \begin{equation}
                \sum_m \left(\Gamma_{m\to n}p_m-\Gamma_{n\to m}p_n\right) = 0\,, \qquad \sum_n p_n=1\,.
            \end{equation}
            This is the orthodox stationary population master equation \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

            Solving this linear system gives the occupation probability $p_n$ of each charge state. The current at junction $i$ follows by summing all signed charge-transfer events through that junction,
            \begin{equation}
                I_i = \sum_n p_n\sum_{m\neq n}q_{i,n\to m}\,\Gamma_{i,n\to m}\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $q_{i,n\to m}$ is the signed charge transferred through junction $i$, while $\Gamma_{i,n\to m}$ is its rate. Charge conservation gives the same stationary current through both junctions. The master equation is a population-balance method that can use orthodox single-electron rates or additional superconducting rates \cite{ingold_charge_1992,grabert_single_2013}.

            The rates specify the allowed transport steps, while the master equation supplies their initial-state occupations. Together they determine \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and gate-dependent transport maps.

            The orthodox description is controlled when both junctions remain in the tunnel limit, charge states are well defined, and memory between successive events can be neglected. It does not describe a highly transmitting contact, strong environmental backaction, large quantum charge fluctuations, or coherent multiparticle transfer.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Tunable and Hybrid SSETs}
        %=========================================================

            Replacing one tunnel junction by a mechanically tunable contact extends the SSET beyond the tunnel limit. Increasing transmission enables Andreev reflection and MAR and enhances quantum charge fluctuations. Charge-state probabilities can remain useful over part of this crossover if the rates include multiparticle transfer through the contact. The microscopic MAR mechanism is developed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:mar}, while its environmental correction is discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:stochastic:dcb}.

            Tunable SSETs place a few-channel superconducting contact in a charge-sensitive circuit and therefore provide access to the crossover between island Coulomb blockade, coherent MAR, and environmental backaction on multiparticle transfer. This device concept motivates part of the experimental approach and fabrication developed in this thesis. It also provides an instructive framework for combining charge-resolved MAR rates with stochastic island occupations and for identifying where the separation into independent contact events and well-defined island charge states can fail.

            Thomas Lorenz and Susanne Sprenger study all-superconducting SETs in which an oxide tunnel barrier and a tunable superconducting break junction define the island. Their measurements span the weak-coupling to atomic-contact regimes. Coulomb blockade suppresses low-bias transport, while increasing contact transmission activates higher-order MAR. They extend orthodox theory by adding MAR rates to the charge-state transitions and thereby describe the principal gate-dependent transport features qualitatively. The approach also reveals where the tunnel-limit picture becomes incomplete, including reduced effective charging energy toward strong coupling and transport features that cannot be assigned uniquely. \cite{lorenz_sset_mar_2018,lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,sprenger_interplay_2019}

            Sobral Rey \textit{et al.} use a hybrid SSN SET to separate the roles of the two junctions more clearly. Here a superconducting island is connected to a superconducting lead by the tunable break junction and to a normal-metal lead by an oxide tunnel barrier. Multiparticle superconducting transport is concentrated at the break junction, while the tunnel barrier mainly provides single-quasiparticle transfer. This asymmetry reduces the number of competing cycles and makes JQP, Andreev, and MAR contributions easier to identify. Their simulations retain the stationary master equation for the island occupations and use charge-resolved, time-averaged MAR rates obtained from full counting statistics. \cite{sobral_rey_hybrid_sset_2024,sobral-rey_interplay_2022}

            The strong-coupling limit illustrates why the charge-state picture remains useful but is not sufficient by itself. A low-impedance superconducting contact can suppress charge quantization by pinning the island potential, while Coulomb-blockade signatures can return after the contact switches to a resistive state. The observed transport then reflects competition between charging energy, superconducting multiparticle transport, and contact transmission rather than a simple disappearance of Coulomb blockade. \cite{sobral_rey_hybrid_sset_2024,sobral-rey_interplay_2022}

            In summary, the charge-state master equation combines device-specific quasiparticle, JQP, Andreev, and MAR rates when successive events are separable and charge-state coherence is negligible. Coherent mixing requires a generalized master equation with off-diagonal density-matrix elements. At stronger coupling, sharply defined island charge states and the charge-state description itself break down.
