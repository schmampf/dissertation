\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:methods:setup}
%=========================================================

    The experimental setup combines a dilution refrigerator, a mechanically controlled break junction, and a filtered low-noise wiring environment. Together, these subsystems define how superconducting atomic contacts and tunnel devices are formed, tuned, and measured. The cryostat provides the millikelvin environment required to resolve superconducting energy scales. The break-junction mechanics adjusts the contact conductance in situ from the tunneling regime to few-channel and atomic-scale contacts. The wiring, filtering, microwave lines, and grounding scheme determine the bias condition, noise environment, thermalization, and high-frequency coupling. This section follows the order in which these elements constrain the measurement: cryogenic environment, mechanical contact tuning, and electrical measurement chain.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Cryostat}
    \label{subsec:setup:cryostat}
    %=========================================================

        The measurements were carried out in an LD400 Bluefors dry dilution refrigerator. The cryostat combines several cooling stages, each of which removes heat in a different temperature range and prepares the next stage for operation. The following description keeps the physical principle close to the implementation, because each cooling step also defines an experimental constraint. The pulse-tube cryocooler provides closed-cycle precooling to the few-kelvin range and sets part of the mechanical vibration environment. The Joule--Thomson stage condenses and further cools the circulating helium mixture. The dilution unit then reaches the millikelvin regime by exploiting the enthalpy of mixing of \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He.

        \subsubsection*{Pulse Tube Cryocooler}
        %=========================================================

            The first cooling stage of the dry cryostat is a two-stage Gifford--McMahon-type pulse-tube cryocooler. It is a closed-cycle refrigerator in which helium is periodically compressed and expanded, but the cold head contains no mechanical displacer. The pressure oscillation is generated at room temperature by a compressor package and rotary valve. Regenerators, heat exchangers, pulse-tube volumes, and flow impedances then convert this oscillating gas flow into refrigeration. In the Bluefors LD400 system, the pulse tube precools the radiation shields, thermalizes wiring, and brings the helium mixture to the few-kelvin range.

            The basic pulse-tube principle is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:pulsetube}(a) for a single-stage orifice pulse-tube refrigerator, following Refs.~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011, bar-cohen_low_2019}. From the warm end to the cold end, the stage contains an aftercooler, a regenerator, a cold heat exchanger, the pulse-tube volume, a warm heat exchanger, and a flow impedance connected to a buffer volume. The compressor imposes a periodic pressure variation, so the helium oscillates through the cold head rather than circulating in one direction. In the regenerator, the gas exchanges heat with a porous matrix of high heat capacity, which maintains the temperature gradient between the warm and cold ends.

            The pulse tube replaces the mechanical displacer by a gas column that acts as a virtual piston. Near the warm end, gas enters from the buffer when the pulse-tube pressure is low and leaves again when the pressure is high. Because compression heats the gas and expansion cools it, gas parcels near the warm end reject heat to the warm heat exchanger. At the cold end, the phase timing is such that gas returns to the cold heat exchanger at lower temperature and absorbs heat from the cold stage. The useful cooling is therefore associated with a net enthalpy flow through the pulse-tube gas column.

            The phase relation between pressure and volume flow is the central design parameter. The warm-end impedance, realized by an orifice, an inertance tube, a reservoir, or a combination of these elements, delays gas exchange with the buffer volume and shifts the gas displacement relative to the pressure oscillation. The compressor and rotary valve provide the pressure drive, while the impedance network determines when gas moves. This phase shift replaces the function of the cold displacer in a conventional Gifford--McMahon cooler.

            In a Gifford--McMahon-type pulse tube, the compressor maintains high- and low-pressure helium reservoirs, and a rotary valve alternately connects the cold head to these pressure levels. The drive is therefore similar to that of a Gifford--McMahon cryocooler, while the cold head remains a pulse-tube cold head. This distinction matters experimentally. The absence of a moving displacer at low temperature reduces wear and direct vibration close to the sample, but the rotary valve, pressure oscillations, and gas dynamics still generate vibrations that can couple into sensitive transport measurements.

            To reach the few-kelvin range, two pulse-tube stages are cascaded, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:pulsetube}(b) and described in Ref.~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011}. The first stage removes the dominant heat load at intermediate temperatures and provides a thermal anchor near 40\,K. The second stage is precooled by the first stage and reaches the few-kelvin range. In the idealized U-shaped two-stage geometry, the cold ends are placed conveniently for cryostat integration, while heat from the warm end of the second pulse tube is not imposed on the first stage.

            Mechanical decoupling is therefore part of the cryostat design. To reduce the transmission of pulse-tube and building vibrations to the sample stage, the cryostat frame is supported by pneumatic air springs at the four corners, operated at about 6\,bar. The suspended cryostat assembly has a large mass, even before accounting for the support frame and experimental wiring. The integrated 7\,T magnet alone has a specified weight of about 26\,kg~\cite{bluefors_bluefors_nodate-1}. This large suspended mass provides additional inertial filtering of external vibrations, while the air springs lower the mechanical coupling between the laboratory floor and the MCBJ. The damping does not remove all pulse-tube-driven motion, but it reduces the sensitivity of the atomic contact to laboratory vibrations and improves the stability of slow conductance tuning. In practice, contacts could remain stable on the scale of weeks as long as the cryostat was not mechanically disturbed. A small fraction of contacts still showed atomic rearrangements during individual measurements, which is an intrinsic limitation of atomic-scale junctions rather than only a cryostat-stability issue.

            The pulse-tube system is driven by an oil-lubricated room-temperature helium compressor. Low-pressure helium returning from the cold head is compressed, cooled, purified, and supplied again as high-pressure helium. Oil lubricates the moving parts, improves sealing, and helps remove heat generated during compression. Before the helium returns to the cold head, oil separators, coalescing filters, and adsorbers remove oil droplets, mist, and residual contaminants. This purification chain is essential because impurities can freeze out at cryogenic temperatures and degrade the regenerator or block small flow impedances.

            Several maintenance interventions on the compressor package were required during this work. The compressor adsorber was replaced as part of the oil-management and helium-purification chain, and the compressor heat exchanger required repeated repairs due to leaks. A practical issue was the cooling-water supply. The compressor was cooled with water from Lake Constance, which is very soft and can corrode copper cooling-water circuits if it is not chemically conditioned. Dirt particles and shell debris in insufficiently filtered lake water can additionally abrade the piping~\cite{haltiner_distribution_2022}. Eventually, a leak between the cooling-water circuit and the helium circuit led to replacement of the compressor package. This maintenance history matters for the experiment because degraded compressor cooling or contaminated helium flow can reduce pulse-tube performance before any problem is visible at the sample stage.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Joule--Thomson Cooling}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-2.0em}
                \import{methods/setup/cryo}{joule-thomson.pgf}
                \vspace{-2.0em}
                \caption{Joule--Thomson coefficient of \textsuperscript{4}He as a function of temperature. The curve was calculated for a pressure of $p = 1\,\mathrm{bar}$ using CoolProp~\cite{bell_pure_2014} and illustrates the change of sign at the inversion temperature.}
                \label{fig:setup:joule-thomson}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            After the pulse tube has cooled the cryostat to the few-kelvin range, the circulating helium mixture still has to be condensed before it can enter the dilution circuit. Condensation cannot be performed efficiently at room temperature, because the incoming gas would carry too much enthalpy into the cold part of the cryostat. The high-pressure mixture is therefore thermalized at the pulse-tube stages and then expanded through a flow impedance. This expansion stage is the Joule--Thomson stage of the refrigerator~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011,pobell_matter_nodate}.

            Joule--Thomson cooling is based on the temperature change of a real gas during expansion at approximately constant enthalpy $H$, given by
            \begin{equation}
                H = U + pV\,,\qquad \Delta H \approx 0\,,
                \label{eq:setup:jt-enthalpy}
            \end{equation}
            with internal energy $U$, pressure $p$, and volume $V$. During throttling through a narrow restriction, no mechanical work is extracted and ideally no heat is exchanged within the impedance. The gas therefore changes pressure without changing its enthalpy appreciably.

            For a small pressure drop, the corresponding temperature change can be written as
            \begin{equation}
                \Delta T \approx \mu_\mathrm{JT}\, \Delta p\,,
                \label{eq:setup:jt-temperature-change}
            \end{equation}
            where $\mu_\mathrm{JT}$ is the Joule--Thomson coefficient, which is given by
            \begin{equation}
                \mu_\mathrm{JT} = \left( \tfrac{\partial T}{\partial p} \right)_H = \tfrac{V}{C_\mathrm{p}} (\alpha T -1)\,.
                \label{eq:setup:jt-coefficient}
            \end{equation}
            Here $C_\mathrm{p}$ is the heat capacity at constant pressure and $\alpha$ is the thermal expansion coefficient. The Joule--Thomson coefficient is the slope of an isenthalpic path in the temperature-pressure plane, and its sign determines whether a pressure drop cools or heats the gas. For an ideal gas, the enthalpy depends only on temperature, so an isenthalpic expansion would not change the temperature. Joule--Thomson cooling is therefore a real-gas effect. In the expression above, this appears through the factor $\alpha T - 1$, which vanishes for an ideal gas.

            The sign of $\mu_\mathrm{JT}$ changes at the inversion temperature, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:joule-thomson} for \textsuperscript{4}He. On one side of this point, an isenthalpic pressure drop cools the gas. On the other side, it heats it. Helium has an unusually low inversion temperature, so room-temperature helium would not be cooled by a Joule--Thomson expansion. The exact values differ for the circulating \textsuperscript{3}He\,/\,\textsuperscript{4}He mixture, but the practical requirement is the same: the gas must be precooled before throttling can be used as a cooling and condensation step. The pulse tube is therefore essential because it brings the mixture into the regime where the subsequent expansion removes heat instead of adding it.

            In the dry dilution refrigerator, the incoming high-pressure mixture is cooled stepwise before it reaches the Joule--Thomson impedance. It is anchored at the pulse-tube stages and exchanges heat with colder return flows in the lower-temperature part of the dilution unit. After this precooling, the pressure drop across the impedance lowers the temperature further and condenses part of the mixture. The Joule--Thomson stage thus links pulse-tube precooling to dilution refrigeration by converting a precooled, pressurized gas flow into a cold liquid mixture for continuous circulation.

            A practical distinction must be made between initial condensation and regular circulation. During condensation, the mixture line is operated at overpressure to drive gas through the impedance and condense it. Leaks are then critical because mixture can be lost to the surrounding vacuum or laboratory environment. During regular circulation, by contrast, large parts of the return path are below atmospheric pressure. Small leaks are less likely to lose mixture, but they can draw residual gas or contamination into the circulation path.

            This contamination is problematic because the Joule--Thomson impedance is narrow and can become clogged by residual gases or moisture that freeze out at cryogenic temperatures. For this reason, the mixture handling line is passed through a liquid-nitrogen trap before condensation or circulation. During continuous circulation, the same restriction still defines part of the pressure drop and the counterflow heat exchangers recover cooling from the outgoing stream, but the pressure conditions and failure modes are different.

            Once the mixture has condensed and circulation is established, the Joule--Thomson stage becomes part of the continuous circulation path that supplies liquid mixture to the lower-temperature stages. The actual millikelvin cooling is produced in the mixing chamber, as discussed in the following subsection.

        %=========================================================

                \label{fig:setup:dilution-fridge}

            The dilution refrigerator uses the phase separation of liquid \textsuperscript{3}He\,/\,\textsuperscript{4}He mixtures to reach the millikelvin regime. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:phase-diagram}, a liquid mixture below the phase-separation boundary separates into a \textsuperscript{3}He-rich concentrated phase and a \textsuperscript{4}He-rich dilute phase. In the low-temperature limit, the concentrated phase approaches pure \textsuperscript{3}He, while the dilute phase retains a finite \textsuperscript{3}He concentration of about 0.066. This finite solubility is the key property that allows continuous refrigeration: \textsuperscript{3}He can keep entering the dilute phase even as the temperature approaches zero.

            The cooling mechanism is based on the enthalpy difference of \textsuperscript{3}He in the two phases. In the mixing chamber, the phase boundary separates the concentrated phase from the dilute phase. As \textsuperscript{3}He crosses this boundary from the concentrated phase into the dilute phase, energy is required. This energy is absorbed from the mixing chamber and provides the cooling power of the dilution stage. The process is not a one-time mixing event, but is maintained by continuously removing \textsuperscript{3}He from the dilute phase and returning it to the concentrated phase through the circulation loop.

            The circulation path is illustrated schematically in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:dilution-fridge}. The incoming \textsuperscript{3}He-rich stream is first precooled by the outgoing cold stream in the counterflow heat exchangers and then enters the mixing chamber as part of the concentrated phase. At the phase boundary, \textsuperscript{3}He enters the dilute phase and produces cooling. The dilute mixture then flows back toward the still. Since \textsuperscript{3}He has a much higher vapor pressure than \textsuperscript{4}He at the still temperature, heating the still preferentially evaporates \textsuperscript{3}He and maintains the circulation. The evaporated \textsuperscript{3}He-rich gas is pumped away, purified and compressed by the room-temperature gas-handling system, and finally returned to the condensing line. The cooling power of the dilution refrigerator is therefore set primarily by the \textsuperscript{3}He circulation rate and by the efficiency of the heat exchangers, which determine how well the incoming stream is precooled before it reaches the mixing chamber~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011,pobell_matter_nodate}.

            Typical preparation and cooldown values are useful diagnostics for later operation. Before cooldown, the cryostat vacuum space was pumped for up to 24\,h and reached pressures of about $6\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mbar}$. During cryogenic operation, the insulating vacuum reached about $2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mbar}$. A full cooldown to the few-kelvin regime, including the magnet, took about 36\,h. Under typical operating conditions the nominal 40\,K stage stabilized near 47\,K, while the 4\,K stage reached about 3.2\,K. Once the system was at 4\,K, condensation of the mixture took about 1\,h, and the subsequent cooldown from 4\,K to base temperature took about 3\,h.

            The LD400 system is specified with a guaranteed base temperature of 10\,mK and an expected base temperature of 8\,mK. The specified cooling powers at the mixing-chamber flange are 14\,\textmu W at 20\,mK, 400\,\textmu W at 100\,mK, and 575\,\textmu W at 120\,mK~\cite{bluefors_ld_dilution_nodate}. These values are manufacturer specifications for the cryostat and were not remeasured for the installed experiment. With the installed MCBJ experiment, wiring, filters, thermometers, and microwave infrastructure, the sample and mixing-chamber stages could reach about 30\,mK, while the still was operated at about 1\,K. A normal automated warm-up with the Bluefors warm-up script took about 2--3 days.

            I did not optimize the \textsuperscript{3}He circulation rate for the absolute lowest mixing-chamber temperature during this work, because the limiting factor for the transport measurements was not the final few millikelvin of lattice temperature. The setup could be operated comfortably between about 30\,mK and 40\,mK, and the MCBJ could be moved step by step without leaving this temperature regime. The largest temperature excursions instead occurred during microwave irradiation. Depending on the applied microwave power and coupling path, the sample-stage temperature could rise to several hundred millikelvin, and in extreme cases approach 1\,K. Improving the last part of the base-temperature performance was therefore less relevant than controlling microwave heating and maintaining a well-thermalized, well-filtered electrical environment. These conditions were more important for obtaining sharp superconducting spectra and stable low-noise \textit{I--V} curves. Earlier optimization of the same cryostat and MCBJ platform by Martin Prestel reached temperatures below 30\,mK before the modifications described here~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}.             Together, these stages define the thermal environment of the experiment. The pulse tube provides the dominant cooling power at intermediate temperatures, the Joule--Thomson stage condenses the helium mixture and enables continuous circulation, and the dilution unit provides the millikelvin cooling power at the mixing chamber. The sample, filters, thermometers, and wiring are thermally anchored to the corresponding cryostat stages. The cryostat therefore does not only set the lattice temperature of the device. It also determines how well electrical lines are thermalized, how much external radiation reaches the contact, and how much mechanical motion is transmitted to the break junction. These limits directly affect the sharpness of superconducting spectra, the stability of \textit{I--V} curves, and the reproducibility of contact tuning.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Mechanically Controlled Break Junction}
    \label{subsec:setup:mcbj}
    %=========================================================
        
        \begin{wrapfigure}[32]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{bruchmechanik.pdf_tex}
            \caption{Rendering of the differential screw, central pushing element, counter supports, and sample holder used for the cryogenic MCBJ mechanics.}
            \label{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The mechanically controllable break-junction (MCBJ) setup is used to tune the conductance of the atomic contact in situ at low temperature. The sample is mounted in a three-point bending geometry, where the substrate is supported by two fixed counter supports and bent by a central pushing element, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}. In many MCBJ implementations, the counter supports are moved with respect to a fixed pushing element. In the present design, by contrast, the counter supports remain fixed and the central pushing element is displaced. Because the metallic constriction is patterned as a suspended bridge on an elastic substrate, a macroscopic displacement of the bending mechanism is converted into a much smaller elongation of the atomic contact. This mechanical reduction is the key reason why atomic-scale contacts can be formed and held over extended measurement times. I did not independently calibrate the absolute conversion from motor motion to junction elongation in this work. The contact position is therefore treated operationally through relative mechanical displacement and through the measured conductance response.

        The break mechanics used in this work is based on the LD400-compatible design developed by A. Fischer and M. Prestel~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}, and build by the scientific workshop\footnote{Scientific workshop project \texttt{07062016}} which follows the same general MCBJ concept as earlier implementations in the group. The relative displacement between the pushing element and the counter supports is generated by a differential screw, which converts the rotary motion of the drive train into the linear motion of the bending mechanism. The screw combines an M4 and an M5 thread. Since the two threads have slightly different pitches, one full revolution does not move the bending mechanism by either pitch individually, but only by their difference. This results in an effective feed of 0.1\,mm per revolution. The differential screw therefore forms the mechanical interface between the rotary drive train and the three-point bending mechanism.

        For operation, I used a relative displacement coordinate rather than the raw encoder value of the motor. The encoder reports large integer positions over an inconvenient range, up to about $1.8\times10^9$, which is not useful for describing contact tuning in the thesis. The laboratory control software therefore maps the motion to a relative scale from $0$ to $18$. This range corresponds to 30 revolutions of the differential screw and therefore to 3\,mm of macroscopic screw feed. The number should not be interpreted as the absolute elongation of the atomic contact. It is a reproducible control coordinate for opening, closing, and returning to contact configurations.

        The central design constraint of the cryogenic drive train is the transmission of a controlled rotary motion from room temperature to the sample space while keeping the heat load on the lower cryostat stages small and avoiding excessive mechanical stress. For this reason, the drive train is mechanically segmented and thermalized at several cryostat stages. Hollow stainless-steel tubes connect the individual gear stages, while the gearboxes simultaneously transmit the motion and provide thermal interruption of the drive shaft.

        In the original implementation, four commercial gearboxes with equal reduction were used along the drive train. For the measurements presented here, this distribution was modified after repeated gearbox failures. The gearhead close to the motor was replaced by a 1:9.7 reduction, and the first two cryogenic gearboxes were replaced by 1:5.4 reductions. The remaining gear stages (1:3.1) were left unchanged. The motivation for this modification was to redistribute the required torque more favorably along the drive train. If too much reduction is placed above the first cryogenic gearbox, that gearbox already has to transmit a large torque. A blockage or increased friction farther down the drive train can then deform the thin drive rod or decouple the gearbox from the following shaft. This failure mode occurred most often at the first gearbox, which was mechanically the most exposed stage in the previous distribution. By spreading the reduction over several stages, the drive reaches the lower mechanics with sufficient torque, but no single upper gearbox has to absorb the full load of a blocked cold mechanism. The increased number of rotations at the 40\,K and 4\,K stages is acceptable, because these stages provide substantially larger cooling power than the millikelvin stages.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{motorhalterung.pdf_tex}
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \caption{Rendering of the redesigned room-temperature mounting plates for the motor and vacuum feedthrough.}
            \label{fig:setup:motorhalterung}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        Several mechanical components of the room-temperature drive were redesigned during this work. In the previous motor mount, the clamping element consisted of a massive plastic disk with a central bore and a narrow radial slit that was closed by a screw. In practice, most of the applied force was spent on elastically deforming the plastic body instead of reducing the bore diameter. In addition, the thread was cut directly into the plastic and was damaged by the high forces required to close the slit. As a result, the mount did not clamp reliably to the motor or to the vacuum feedthrough. This part was replaced by a two-piece clamp manufactured by the scientific workshop\footnote{Scientific workshop project \texttt{13042026}}, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:motorhalterung}. Separate clamps are now used for the motor and the vacuum feedthrough and are connected by rods, which provides a much stiffer and more reproducible alignment of the room-temperature drive. The motor and gearhead are also connected by a 3D-printed clamp instead of being glued in place.

        The vacuum feedthrough is another critical component of the room-temperature drive. To keep the rotational friction low and to avoid stick--slip motion or excessive load on the motor, the feedthrough was lubricated regularly with Fomblin vacuum grease.

        The slip clutch between the motor and the vacuum feedthrough was also modified. In the original part, the clamping threads were cut into plastic. During operation these threads were repeatedly compressed and deformed, which shifted the clutch away from the rotation axis. This misalignment increased the load on the motor and led to premature motor failure. The plastic threads were therefore replaced by brass inserts. This change improves the centering of the clutch and makes the coupling mechanically more stable under repeated clamping.

        Careful alignment of the cryogenic drive train is essential. The rods inside the cryostat were fixed while already rotating slowly, so that small angular or lateral offsets could relax during assembly. This procedure reduces the probability that the completed drive train locks at low temperature because of a residual misalignment between adjacent stages. The gearboxes were replaced several times during this work before the modified reduction distribution proved sufficiently reliable. Before cooldown, each gearbox was prepared by removing the original lubricant. It was cleaned overnight in an ultrasonic acetone bath, subsequently rinsed with isopropanol, and heated with a hot-air gun immediately before cooldown to remove residual solvent as well as moisture adsorbed after cleaning. This preparation is necessary because frozen lubricant, solvent, or water residues can block the gear motion at cryogenic temperatures.

        A useful low-temperature check of the MCBJ mechanics is the heat generated during intentional motion. If the drive train is connected and the cold mechanism moves, stepwise actuation typically produces a visible increase of the sample-stage temperature, in some cases up to about 1\,K. This heating is not desirable during sensitive measurements, but it is a practical diagnostic that mechanical work reaches the cold break mechanism. During such tests the gas-handling system has to be monitored, because the additional heat load on the still can increase the return pressure and may lead to overpressure if the motion is too aggressive or repeated too quickly.

        The final configuration was reached through an iterative commissioning process rather than through a single modification. Each mechanical change had to be tested in a full cryogenic cycle, so the feedback time was long: a failed test typically meant another warm-up, repair or replacement of the affected component, pump-down, and cooldown. Replacement parts also introduced delays when commercial components were not immediately available. This made the mechanical optimization one of the more time-consuming parts of the experiment. The resulting procedure, however, substantially improved the reliability of the drive train. After the gearbox distribution, motor mount, slip clutch, feedthrough lubrication, and assembly procedure had been revised, the mechanics could be operated reliably during cooldown and at base temperature. In the final cooldown, I could break and tune the sample without further mechanical problems.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Electrical Measurement Environment}
    \label{subsec:setup:electrical}
    %=========================================================

        The electrical setup connects a nanoscale device at millikelvin temperature to room-temperature sources, amplifiers, microwave equipment, and control electronics. This makes the wiring more than a passive connection. It defines the bias condition of the sample, sets the noise environment, determines how well the electronic degrees of freedom are thermalized, and limits which microwave amplitudes can be applied without excessive heating. In the following, I first introduce the measurement schematic used to reconstruct the sample voltage and current. I then describe the cryostat wiring, filtering, microwave lines, thermometry, and the grounding strategy of the complete setup.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Measurement Schematic}
        %=========================================================

            The transport measurement is based on a quasi-dc bias circuit with simultaneous readout of sample voltage and current. A programmed bias voltage $V_\mathrm{bias}$ is applied to the bias line, but the voltage arriving at the device is not assumed to be identical to this source voltage. The finite resistance of filters, leads, reference resistors, and contacts creates a voltage divider together with the sample. Therefore both relevant quantities are measured directly: $V_\mathrm{sample}$ is measured across the device, and the current is inferred from the voltage drop $V_\mathrm{ref}$ across a known reference resistor.

            In the standard configuration, two cold reference resistors are placed symmetrically on the two sides of the sample. This symmetric arrangement keeps the sample potential close to the cryostat ground and reduces common-mode offsets during bias sweeps. The voltage $V_\mathrm{ref}$ is measured across one of these cold reference resistors. Its measured resistance at base temperature is
            \begin{equation}
                R_\mathrm{ref} = 101.473\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\,.
                \label{eq:setup:cold-reference-resistance}
            \end{equation}
            The second cold reference resistor contributes to the total series resistance of the bias path, but it is not included in the measured reference-voltage drop.

            A useful way to characterize the bias condition is the ratio
            \begin{equation}
                \eta = R_\mathrm{series}\,/\, R_\mathrm{sample}\,,
                \label{eq:setup:bias-eta}
            \end{equation}
            where $R_\mathrm{sample}$ is the instantaneous device resistance and $R_\mathrm{series}$ is the remaining resistance in the bias path,
            \begin{equation}
                R_\mathrm{series}
                % = R_\mathrm{total}-R_\mathrm{sample}
                \simeq 2R_\mathrm{ref} + R_\mathrm{filters} + R_\mathrm{leads} + \ldots\,.
                \label{eq:setup:series-resistance}
            \end{equation}
            In the limit $\eta \ll 1$, the circuit approaches an ideal voltage bias because most of the applied voltage drops across the sample. In the opposite limit, $\eta \gg 1$, the series resistance dominates and the circuit approaches a current-bias condition. The present setup is deliberately flexible, but this also means that the bias condition changes with the state of the MCBJ. Typical sample resistances range from about 100\,\textohm{} for a closed break junction to values of order $R_0 = 1/G_0 \simeq 12.9\,\mathrm{k}\Omega$ for a few-channel atomic contact. The tunnel regime starts roughly around 100\,k\textohm, corresponding to about $0.1\,G_0$, and can extend to much higher resistances. The standard filtered bias environment contains about 200\,k\textohm from the two cold reference resistors alone. The experiment therefore moves between voltage-biased, mixed, and more current-biased regimes depending on the junction configuration.

            This is the reason why both $V_\mathrm{sample}$ and $I_\mathrm{sample}$ are reconstructed from measured voltages rather than inferred from the programmed source value. Since $V_\mathrm{ref}$ is measured across one cold reference resistor, the current is obtained as
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{sample} = V_\mathrm{ref}\,/\,R_\mathrm{ref}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:sample-current}
            \end{equation}
            In a linearized resistive approximation, the voltage and current would follow
            \begin{align}
                V_\mathrm{sample} &\simeq V_\mathrm{bias}\,R_\mathrm{sample}\,/\,R_\mathrm{total}\,, \\
                I_\mathrm{sample} &\simeq V_\mathrm{bias}\,/\,R_\mathrm{total}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:bias-divider}
            \end{align}
            These expressions are useful for intuition, but the actual analysis uses the simultaneously measured voltage drops. This is essential for nonlinear superconducting transport, where the sample resistance changes with bias and where small deviations from an ideal bias condition can distort the apparent \textit{I--V} curve.

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema}(a) shows the standard configuration used for high-impedance tunnel and few-channel measurements. For very low sample impedances, however, the large cold series resistance limits the voltage that can be applied across the sample. A substantial part of the programmed bias then drops across the reference resistors instead of the device, so that the accessible sample-voltage range can become too small to drive the junction out of the zero-voltage supercurrent branch or to reach spectroscopic features such as the superconducting gap.

        In this regime I use the modified configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema}(b). The voltage-sense and bias wiring are exchanged, and the symmetric cold resistors in the bias line are reduced to nominally 100\,\textohm{} each. The current is then read out using an additional warm reference resistor. This configuration increases the accessible sample-voltage range, but it moves part of the current readout to room temperature and therefore exposes the measurement to additional Johnson--Nyquist noise from the warm environment. The low-impedance configuration is therefore used only when the larger accessible sample-voltage range outweighs this added noise contribution.

        \subsubsection*{Wiring inside Cryostat}
        %=========================================================

        \begin{figure}[t!]
            \centering
            {\fontsize{7}{9}\selectfont
            \import{methods/setup/electric}{setup.pdf_tex}
            }
            \caption{Schematic overview of the custom wiring inside the dilution fridge. All components are mounted at the base-temperature stage, except for the high-frequency line attenuators, which are mounted at the 4\,K stage (indicated with ``-10\,dB''). The \legend{seeblau65} box indicates the sample and the corresponding schematic. The \legend{seeblau100} circle indicates the island, which is coupled to the MCBJ (left) and the tunnel barrier (bottom), and capacitively coupled to the DC gate (right) and the AC gate (top). These four components are bonded with thin copper cables, which are soldered to pin headers (\legend{seegrau100} circles). The \legend{seegrau65} box indicates the extra shielded part around the reference resistors ($R_\mathrm{ref}$). The pin headers are connected either to coaxial twisted-pair cables or to a high-frequency coaxial cable. The custom-built copper-powder (CP) filters and the commercial MFT25 filter suppress high-frequency components and act as thermal anchors.}
            \label{fig:setup:setup}
        \end{figure}

        The electrical wiring inside the cryostat has to satisfy two competing requirements. First, the wiring must provide a sufficiently well-defined electrical environment for low-noise transport measurements on high-impedance tunnel contacts and few-channel atomic contacts. This requires filtering of external noise, suppression of unwanted high-frequency components, and a symmetric wiring geometry, so the sample is close to zero potential \cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,sprenger_interplay_2019,sobral-rey_interplay_2022}. Second, the same wiring must not introduce an excessive heat load from room temperature to the millikelvin stage and must thermalize the electronic degrees of freedom close to the sample temperature. The wiring concept therefore follows the same general strategy as the earlier MCBJ and SET setups in the group~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018, prestel_magnetic_2020}: low-thermal-conductance wiring is used between temperature stages, the lines are thermalized and filtered repeatedly, and the most sensitive components are mounted close to the sample at the base-temperature stage.

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:setup} shows the custom wiring used in this work. The low-frequency wiring contains six functional lines: sample-voltage readout, bias, current readout, DC gate, thermometer, and heater. The DC gate is implemented with two physical lines that are shorted at the lowest practical point, which provides diagnostic and backup access while appearing as a single gate terminal electrically. From room temperature to the base-temperature region, these lines use the commercial Bluefors cryostat cabling. At the base-temperature stage, the lines are continued with GVLZ169 low-temperature coaxial cable from GVL,\footnote{GVL Cryoengineering GVLZ 169, connected with two-pole LEMO FGG.00.302.CLAD35 plugs and EGG.00.302.CLL sockets.} a double-twisted coaxial cable with brass inner and outer conductors. These base-stage cables are thermally anchored locally before they enter the sample wiring.

        The sample is mounted at the base-temperature stage and connected through pin headers. The local sample contacts are made from 90\,\textmu m insulated copper wires, which are soldered to the pin headers and attached to the sample pads with conductive silver paste. This short copper section provides low contact resistance and good thermal anchoring near the device. The voltage and current leads define a four-terminal measurement with respect to the cryostat wiring, but not an ideal Kelvin measurement of the junction alone, because the local copper wires, silver-paste contacts, and sample pads remain inside the measured sample region. The high-frequency drive lines are routed separately as coaxial microwave lines.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{DC Filtering}
        %=========================================================

        The DC wiring carries the slowly varying bias and gate voltages as well as the voltage signals used to determine the current and the sample voltage. The bias circuit is operated in a quasi-DC mode. The externally applied voltage is swept slowly, while the actual sample voltage and the current through the device are recorded simultaneously. This is important because the MCBJ conductance can change over several orders of magnitude and because the voltage division between reference resistors, filters, wiring impedance, and sample depends on the instantaneous device resistance. Measuring both voltage and current directly avoids relying only on the programmed source voltage.

        The low-frequency lines are filtered and thermalized in several stages. Six copper-powder filters are mounted at the base-temperature stage, one each for the voltage, bias, current, heater, thermometer, and DC-gate lines. These filters are inherited group-built components based on the filter concept described in Ref.~\cite{thalmann_comparison_2017}. In this filter type, a copper signal wire is embedded in compacted copper powder inside a gold-plated copper housing. The filter therefore has a low resistance for DC and low-frequency signals, but becomes increasingly lossy at high frequency. The relevant mechanism is the skin effect: microwave currents are confined closer to the surface of the signal wire, and the surrounding copper powder provides a dissipative electromagnetic environment. The resulting attenuation is broadband and is not set by a single RC cutoff or lumped resonance. The copper-powder filters therefore act as a robust broadband safety stage against microwave leakage, while the large metal--metal contact area between wire, powder, and housing also provides an efficient thermal anchor for the electronic degrees of freedom in the line. The important point for the present setup is their role in the filter stack. They add broadband high-frequency loss without adding a large DC resistance to the measurement circuit. Using them in series with a commercial filter--thermalizer makes the electrical environment more robust than relying on a single low-pass element.

        In addition to the copper-powder filters, a commercial MFT25\footnote{Basel Precision Instruments, MFT25-100\textohm{}, SN0219. The unit is connected through a 25-way Glenair MWDM Micro-D panel-mount receptacle.} microwave filter--thermalizer is used in series with the copper-powder filters on the true transport lines and the DC gate, namely the voltage, current, bias, and DC-gate lines. According to the manufacturer, the MFT25 embeds the signal conductors in a highly conductive silver matrix, which acts as a heat exchanger, and the compact package contains 25 filter lines in a gold-plated non-magnetic copper box. The manufacturer specifies strong microwave attenuation above 130\,MHz together with low capacitance and good thermal contact to the millikelvin stage. In this setup, the MFT25 provides the compact commercial filter--thermalizer stage, while the preceding copper-powder filters provide additional broadband protection against high-frequency leakage.

        The change to the MFT25 also improved the practical reliability of the DC wiring. Earlier versions of the setup used fragile steel-capillary wiring, similar to the configuration described in Ref.~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. These lines could break during assembly or thermal cycling, and inherited copper-powder filters also failed intermittently. The MFT25 replaced most of this fragile capillary-filter chain on the transport and DC-gate lines while adding a well-thermalized commercial filtering stage. The resulting wiring was mechanically more robust and better suited to maintaining a low electronic temperature at the sample.

        The reference resistors are mounted in the shielded base-temperature region close to the sample. In an earlier configuration they were located at the still stage. Moving them to the base-temperature stage reduces their Johnson--Nyquist voltage noise, whose spectral density scales as $\sqrt{T}$, and keeps the current readout in the same cold filtered environment as the sample. The design was also made mechanically more robust. An earlier through-hole metal-oxide resistor developed intermittent contacts, while the revised design uses an SMD resistor on a small chip, which provides better thermal contact and a less fragile electrical connection. Before cooldown, the wiring was checked by measuring the resistance matrix between the relevant ports and by verifying the ground connections of the lines. The reference-resistor region is additionally shielded, as indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:setup}, to reduce capacitive pickup from neighboring lines. This is particularly relevant for superconducting subgap measurements, where small current contributions must be resolved on top of a strongly nonlinear current--voltage characteristic.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Thermometry and Heating}
        %=========================================================

        The sample space is also equipped with dedicated thermometer and heater wiring to monitor and control the local temperature of the base-temperature stage. These lines are routed in a separate cable tree and through a separate break-out box from the transport wiring, so that heater or thermometer signals do not share the sensitive sample circuit. This separation is useful because thermometer readout and heater operation can otherwise interfere with low-current transport measurements. The thermometer arrangement follows the earlier MCBJ setup used by Martin Prestel~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. In the present setup, a Rox resistor thermometer was mounted on the sample holder next to the sample.\footnote{Lake Shore Cryotronics Inc., RX102B-CB, serial number \texttt{U06030}, control-software curve name \texttt{MCBJ Sample ORI}.}

        The heater is a local resistive heater made from a thin insulated manganin wire with a resistance of about 100\,\textohm{}. It is wrapped around one of the posts that connects the sample holder to the mechanical break-junction assembly. This geometry heats the sample holder locally while remaining mechanically simple and robust. I used the heater for controlled temperature-dependent measurements.

        The thermometer reading should be interpreted as the local sample-holder temperature, not automatically as the electronic temperature of the junction. The electron temperature can be higher because of imperfect line thermalization, residual high-frequency radiation, dissipated bias power, or heat deposited by microwave excitation. This distinction is particularly important under AC irradiation. A change in the measured temperature can indicate real heating, but it can also result from direct microwave-induced artifacts.

        The thermometer and heater lines are filtered and thermalized for the same reason as the transport lines: they connect room-temperature electronics to the millikelvin stage and can therefore act as both thermal and electrical noise channels. Their requirements are less restrictive than those of the transport lines with respect to voltage noise at the sample, but they still have to be sufficiently well anchored to avoid parasitic heating. In practice, the thermometer and heater wiring is therefore treated as part of the same cryogenic wiring system rather than as auxiliary cabling that can be ignored once the electrical transport circuit is defined.\footnote{The thermometer and heater cable tree connects to the Bluefors cabling through LEMO FGG.0S.302.ZLAT plugs.}

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{AC Cabling}
        %=========================================================

        In addition to the DC bias and gate lines, the setup contains two high-frequency paths for microwave excitation. I refer to them as the AC-gate line and the antenna line. The AC-gate line terminates on the on-chip AC gate, which is capacitively coupled to the sample. The antenna line is a stripped coaxial cable placed close to the sample. This naming describes the intended hardware geometry. At GHz frequencies the local field distribution is not a lumped circuit element, and both paths can couple microwave power into the device region.

        Both microwave paths are routed as coaxial lines, because impedance control, shielding, and reproducible transmission are more important at GHz frequencies than the low-frequency symmetry used for the DC transport wiring. The commercial AC cabling inside the cryostat uses superconducting cables to keep the transmission high. At each temperature stage, the microwave line passes through a connector that also provides a thermalization point. The cryostat was initially delivered with attenuators installed at these positions, but I replaced them by pass-through connectors\footnote{Amphenol SV Microwave, SF1597-6003} except at the 4\,K flange. The remaining 10\,dB attenuators at 4\,K provide a controlled thermalization point while keeping the heat load at a stage where it can still be handled efficiently. For future measurements, moving these attenuators to the MXC pass-through would likely be preferable, because the setup has sufficient remaining cooling power and the electronic temperature at the device is the more important limitation.

        The base-temperature part of the microwave cabling\footnote{Elspec, DA50047-821, 50\,\textohm{}, CuAg/PTFE/Cu, 1.19\,mm + Rosenberger, 02S141-270L5} was installed together with Patrick Raif~\cite{raif_electronic_2024}. Frequencies up to about 20\,GHz were transmitted reliably in the installed configuration. This statement is based on the observed response of SIS spectra and photon-assisted tunneling features, not on an absolute calibration of the microwave voltage at the junction. The two paths also have different relative transmission as a function of frequency, which is useful because it provides access to different effective power windows for different microwave tones.

        Together with Patrick Raif, we showed that both paths act, in practice, like antennas for the device~\cite{raif_electronic_2024}. Part of the microwave power is launched already near the pin headers, and from the point of view of the sample the two paths behave more like two different antennas than like two sharply separated drive mechanisms. The coupling to the sample is not impedance matched. Achieving a well-defined matched microwave environment would require a substantially different break-junction architecture, closer to the dedicated microwave design used by Bretheau~\cite{bretheau_localized_2013}. I therefore did not attempt an absolute calibration of the local microwave voltage. Instead, the microwave amplitude is calibrated effectively from known microwave-induced spectral signatures, such as SIS photon-assisted tunneling features, as described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}. What can be stated reliably is the relative transmission difference between the paths and the presence or absence of microwave-induced spectral features. The absolute local microwave amplitude at the junction remains unknown and is therefore treated as an effective parameter unless it is calibrated directly from the data.

        \subsubsection*{Instrumentation and Grounding Schematic}
        %=========================================================
        
        \begin{figure}[t!]
            \centering
            {\fontsize{6}{9}\selectfont
            \import{methods/setup/electric}{grounding-schema.pdf_tex}
            }
            \caption{Grounding and instrument schematic of the room-temperature setup. Building ground and cryostat ground are kept separate except for a 13\,M\textohm{} connection~(G) through the temperature controller. The motor controller (B) and LAN switch (W) are floating because their plugs do not provide a protective-earth connection. The magnet power supply (Y) was also operated floating, which minimized the observed noise in the measured spectra.}
            \label{fig:setup:grounding}
        \end{figure}

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:grounding} summarizes the room-temperature instruments and their grounding connections. The cryostat was treated as the local ground reference of the experiment. This is practical because the cryostat is a large metallic body with a stable electrical potential, while the setup did not provide a separate measurement ground comparable to the P5 setup~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018}. The practical grounding rule was to connect each instrument to this cryostat reference only through its intended signal path or through a deliberately isolated control link. Uncontrolled parallel connections between building ground, computer interfaces, instrument shields, and the cryostat body were avoided.

        The control interfaces were therefore part of the grounding strategy rather than merely a convenience layer. The control computer was connected to the laboratory network, while GPIB and RS-232 instruments were interfaced through USB adapters. USB connections can introduce additional shield and ground paths, so they were isolated either by USB-over-LAN links or by galvanic USB isolators. The cryostat control unit\footnote{Bluefors LD400, connected by USB} was included in this isolated control chain. LAN-connected instruments were used where available, because this allowed remote control without adding direct USB connections to the measurement computer. The motor controller and LAN switch were floating in the final configuration because their plugs did not provide a protective-earth connection, as indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:grounding}.

        The voltage signals were first amplified by voltage preamplifiers\footnote{Femto, Pre-Amplifier DLPVA, power supply \texttt{WW-98000627}, communication Luci10, connected by USB}. These amplifiers provide selectable gains and a low-pass bandwidth of about 100\,kHz. Their output voltage is referenced to ground and is digitized by the analog inputs of the measurement card\footnote{ADwin, Gold II, connected by LAN}. Although the ADwin can sample at rates up to 100\,kHz, much faster than required for the quasi-dc sweeps used here, the data stream was reduced on the ADwin to 137\,Hz.

        The bias voltage was generated by an analog output of the same measurement card. Before entering the cryostat wiring, it passed through a warm symmetric voltage divider and a low-pass filter\footnote{Thorlabs, EF120}. The divider reduced the full output range of the card to the voltage window relevant for the sample, thereby improving the effective bias resolution. Its resistances were chosen low enough that their thermal noise and their contribution to the total source impedance remained limited. The divider was kept at room temperature, in contrast to the cold shunt used in Ref.~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. This choice preserved access to filter, wiring, and grounding diagnostics during cooldown and low-temperature operation, following the same practical motivation as in Ref.~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018}. A shunt mounted inside the cryostat would have fixed part of the bias environment locally, but it would also have made several grounding and continuity checks inaccessible during a cooldown.

        Several combinations of available voltage sources, amplifiers, and multimeters were tested before settling on the configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:grounding}. The practical optimization criterion was the low-frequency noise in the recorded sample- and reference-voltage channels under the same filtering and grounding conditions used for transport measurements. The relevant observable was therefore not only an electrical continuity check, but the stability and cleanliness of the measured \textit{I--V} curves and superconducting spectra. Using one measurement card for both voltage channels also ensured that the sample-voltage and reference-voltage readings were acquired as synchronized pairs. During a typical measurement, the bias was applied symmetrically around ground, so that the common-mode voltage of the sample remained close to zero. The voltage was then ramped slowly down and up, with full periods of 5\,min or longer.

        The remaining instruments can be grouped by function. The DC gate voltage was applied with respect to ground by a high-precision voltage source\footnote{Yokogawa, GS200, connected by GPIB}, and all DC lines were routed through a break-out box\footnote{Major Tom \texttt{WW-2280090}} into the commercial cryostat cabling. The microwave excitation paths were driven by the two ports of a vector network analyzer\footnote{Rohde \& Schwarz, ZNB40, connected by LAN}, which were connected to the AC-gate and antenna lines. Temperature monitoring and local heating were handled by an AC resistance bridge\footnote{Lake Shore Cryotronics, 372AC and 3726, connected by USB}, which read out the sample heater, the sample thermometer, and auxiliary thermometers through the separate thermometry cable tree. I replaced the previously used Lake Shore 370AC by the 372AC because it can continuously read the sample thermometer while the other channel is used together with the channel scanner to read out the auxiliary thermometers. This continuous sample-temperature readout allowed PID control of the local sample temperature to a chosen setpoint. Mechanical actuation of the break junction was provided by an encoded motor and driver\footnote{Faulhaber, Motor 2232R024S, Encoder R IE2-1024 4613, Driver MCDC3002S RS, Gear Head 22/2S, connected by RS-232}. A one-axis 7\,T magnet supply\footnote{American Magnetics Inc., AMI430, connected by LAN} was used when superconductivity had to be suppressed. Additional diagnostic access was provided by a precision source\footnote{Keysight, B2962A, connected by LAN} and several digital multimeters\footnote{Keysight, 34461A, connected by LAN}. These diagnostic instruments were connected only when their information was needed, because each additional instrument could introduce another pickup or ground path.

        For the lowest-noise spectra, devices that were not required during the actual sweep were disconnected from the setup. In particular, the magnet controller and magnet power supply were usually disconnected when not in use. This mattered because the magnet supply is a large high-current instrument and can couple low-frequency pickup or ground offsets into the cryostat environment even when the magnetic field is not being changed. The communication cables of the preamplifiers were removed whenever remote gain control was not needed. This reduced the number of possible ground-loop and pickup paths connected to the sensitive voltage-readout circuit.

        The final grounding configuration was therefore not imposed by the nominal instrument grounds alone, but selected empirically from configurations that preserved a well-defined cryostat reference while minimizing pickup in the measured voltage channels. It should be understood as the working grounding solution for this specific cryostat, instrument rack, and wiring configuration. It is not a universal grounding model, but it documents which choices produced stable \textit{I--V} traces and clean superconducting spectra in this experiment.
