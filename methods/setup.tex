\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{section:setup}
%=========================================================

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Cryostat}
    %=========================================================

        The measurements were carried out in an LD400 Bluefors dry dilution refrigerator. The cryostat combines several cooling stages, each of which removes heat in a different temperature range and prepares the next stage for operation. In the following, the cooling process is discussed along this sequence: first, the pulse-tube cryocooler provides closed-cycle precooling to the few-kelvin range; second, Joule--Thomson cooling is used to condense and further cool the circulating helium mixture; and third, the dilution refrigerator reaches the millikelvin regime by exploiting the enthalpy of mixing of \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Pulse Tube Cryocooler}
        %=========================================================

            The first cooling stage of the dry cryostat is provided by a two-stage Gifford--McMahon-type pulse-tube cryocooler. It is a closed-cycle refrigerator in which helium is periodically compressed and expanded, but, in contrast to a conventional Gifford--McMahon cooler, the cold head contains no mechanical displacer. The pressure oscillation is generated at room temperature by a compressor package and a rotary valve, while the low-temperature part uses regenerators, heat exchangers, pulse-tube volumes, and flow impedances to convert this oscillating gas flow into refrigeration. In the Bluefors LD400 system, the pulse tube precools the radiation shields, thermalizes wiring at intermediate stages, and brings the helium mixture of the dilution circuit to the few-kelvin range, where condensation and the subsequent dilution process become possible.

            The basic pulse-tube principle is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:setup:pulsetube}(a) for a single-stage orifice pulse-tube refrigerator, following the descriptions in Refs.~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011, bar-cohen_low_2019}. Starting from the warm end, such a stage consists of an aftercooler, a regenerator, a cold heat exchanger, the pulse-tube volume itself, a warm heat exchanger, and a flow impedance connected to a buffer volume. The compressor imposes a periodic pressure variation on the helium. The gas therefore oscillates back and forth rather than circulating through the cold head in one direction. In the regenerator, the oscillating helium is in good thermal contact with a porous matrix with high heat capacity. Gas moving from the warm side toward the cold side gives up heat to this matrix, while gas moving back takes heat up again. Ideally, the regenerator stores and returns heat locally during each cycle, thereby maintaining the temperature gradient between the warm and cold ends without producing a net heat load by itself.

            The pulse tube replaces the mechanical displacer by a column of gas. A part of this gas column remains inside the pulse tube during the entire cycle and acts as a virtual piston. Near the warm end, gas enters the pulse tube from the buffer when the pressure in the pulse tube is below the buffer pressure and leaves it again when the pulse-tube pressure is above the buffer pressure. Because compression heats the gas and expansion cools it, gas parcels near the warm end leave the pulse tube at a higher temperature than they had when entering. This heat is rejected at the warm heat exchanger. At the cold end, the timing is such that gas entering the pulse tube from the cold heat exchanger is returned at a lower temperature, so that heat is absorbed from the cold stage. In this view, the useful cooling is associated with a net enthalpy flow through the pulse-tube gas column: gas motion toward the warm end occurs, on average, at higher temperature than gas motion back toward the cold end.

            The phase relation between pressure and volume flow is the central design parameter. If the cold-end volume flow did not have the proper phase relation to the pressure oscillation, the pulse tube would not provide efficient heat pumping. The flow impedance at the warm end, realized by an orifice, an inertance tube, a reservoir, or combinations of these elements, delays the exchange of gas with the buffer volume and thereby shifts the phase of the gas displacement relative to the pressure oscillation. Equivalently, the pulse tube and its termination form an acoustic circuit: the compressor and rotary valve provide the pressure drive, while the impedance network determines when gas moves. This phase shift is the element that replaces the function of the cold displacer in a conventional Gifford--McMahon cooler.

            In a Gifford--McMahon-type pulse tube, the pressure oscillation is not produced by a piston directly attached to the pulse tube. Instead, the compressor maintains high- and low-pressure helium reservoirs, and a rotary valve alternately connects the cold head to these pressure levels. This makes the drive similar to that of a Gifford--McMahon cryocooler, while the cold head remains a pulse-tube cold head. This distinction is important experimentally: the absence of a moving displacer at low temperature reduces mechanical wear and direct vibration close to the experiment, but the rotary valve, pressure oscillations, and gas dynamics still generate vibrations that can couple into sensitive transport measurements.

            For reaching the few-kelvin range, two pulse-tube stages are cascaded, as shown schematically in Figure~\ref{fig:setup:pulsetube}(b) and described in Ref.~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011}. The first stage removes the dominant heat load at intermediate temperatures and typically provides a thermal anchor near 40\,K. The second stage is precooled by the first stage and reaches the few-kelvin range. In the idealized U-shaped two-stage geometry, the cold ends are placed conveniently for integration into the cryostat, while heat rejected at the warm end of the second pulse tube is not imposed as a direct load on the first stage.

            The pulse-tube system is driven by an oil-lubricated room-temperature helium compressor. Low-pressure helium returning from the cold head is compressed, cooled, purified, and supplied again to the cold head as high-pressure helium. Oil is useful inside the compressor because it lubricates the moving parts, improves the sealing of small clearances, and helps to remove heat generated during compression. Before the helium returns to the cold head, oil separators, coalescing filters, and adsorbers remove oil droplets, oil mist, and residual contaminants. This purification chain is essential because oil or other impurities would freeze out at cryogenic temperatures and could degrade the regenerator or block small flow impedances in the pulse-tube circuit.\footnote{During this work, several maintenance interventions were required on the pulse-tube compressor package. The compressor adsorber was replaced as part of the oil-management and helium-purification chain, and the compressor heat exchanger required repeated repairs due to leaks. One contributing practical issue is that the compressor cooling water is supplied from Lake Constance. The lake water is very soft and can therefore be corrosive to copper cooling-water circuits if not chemically conditioned. Over time, this can promote corrosion and gradual dissolution of copper piping. Due to insufficient filtering, the water may contain dirt particles with diameters of up to 1\,mm, which can further abrade the piping. In addition, Lake Constance has been affected by the invasive quagga mussel, which can contribute shell and mussel debris to technical water circuits~\cite{haltiner_distribution_2022}. Eventually, a leak between the cooling-water circuit and the helium circuit led to replacement of the compressor package.}


        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Joule--Thomson Cooling}
        %=========================================================

            After the pulse tube has cooled the cryostat to the few-kelvin range, the circulating helium mixture still has to be condensed before it can be used in the dilution circuit. This step cannot be performed efficiently at room temperature, because the incoming gas would carry too much enthalpy into the low-temperature part of the cryostat. Instead, the high-pressure mixture is first thermalized at the pulse-tube stages and then expanded through a flow impedance. This expansion stage is the Joule--Thomson stage of the refrigerator~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011,pobell_matter_nodate}.

            Joule--Thomson cooling is based on the temperature change of a real gas during expansion at approximately constant enthalpy $H$, given by
            \begin{equation}
                H = U + pV\,,\qquad \Delta H \approx 0\,,
            \end{equation}
            with internal energy $U$, pressure $p$, and volume $V$. The first term describes the energy stored inside the gas, while the pressure-volume contribution accounts for the work associated with displacing gas into or out of a volume. During throttling through a narrow restriction, no mechanical work is extracted and ideally no heat is exchanged directly within the impedance. The gas therefore changes pressure without changing its enthalpy appreciably.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[17]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-2em}
                \import{methods/setup/cryo}{joule-thomson.pgf}
                \caption{Joule--Thomson coefficient of \textsuperscript{4}He as a function of temperature. The curve was calculated for a pressure of $p = 1\,\mathrm{bar}$ using CoolProp~\cite{bell_pure_2014} and illustrates the change of sign at the inversion temperature.}
                \label{fig:setup:joule-thomson}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            Although the enthalpy remains approximately constant across the restriction, the temperature does not necessarily remain constant. For a small pressure drop, the corresponding temperature change can be written as
            \begin{equation}
                \Delta T \approx \mu_\mathrm{JT}\, \Delta p\,,
            \end{equation}
            where $\mu_\mathrm{JT}$ is the Joule--Thomson coefficient, which is given by
            \begin{equation}
                \mu_\mathrm{JT} = \left( \tfrac{\partial T}{\partial p} \right)_H = \tfrac{V}{C_\mathrm{p}} (\alpha T -1)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $C_\mathrm{p}$ is the heat capacity at constant pressure and $\alpha$ is the thermal expansion coefficient. The Joule--Thomson coefficient is the slope of an isenthalpic path in the temperature-pressure plane. It therefore determines whether a pressure drop through the impedance cools or heats the gas. For an ideal gas, the enthalpy depends only on temperature, so an isenthalpic expansion would leave the temperature unchanged. Joule--Thomson cooling is therefore a real-gas effect. In the form above, this is reflected by the factor $\alpha T - 1$: for an ideal gas, the thermal expansion coefficient satisfies $\alpha T = 1$, and the Joule--Thomson coefficient vanishes.

            The sign of $\mu_\mathrm{JT}$ changes at the inversion temperature, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:setup:joule-thomson}. On one side of this point, an isenthalpic pressure drop lowers the temperature. On the other side, the same pressure drop raises it. Helium has an unusually low inversion temperature, so room-temperature helium would not be cooled by a Joule--Thomson expansion. This is why the pulse tube is essential: it brings the gas into the thermodynamic regime in which the subsequent Joule--Thomson expansion cools rather than heats the mixture.

            In the dry dilution refrigerator, the incoming high-pressure helium mixture is therefore cooled stepwise before it reaches the Joule--Thomson impedance. It is anchored thermally at the pulse-tube stages and exchanges heat with colder return flows in the lower-temperature part of the dilution unit. After this precooling, the pressure drop across the impedance lowers the temperature further and allows part of the mixture to condense. The condensed liquid mixture can then continue toward the still, heat exchangers, and mixing chamber of the dilution refrigerator. The Joule--Thomson stage thus forms the link between pulse-tube precooling and dilution refrigeration: it converts a precooled, pressurized gas flow into a cold liquid mixture suitable for continuous circulation through the millikelvin stage.

            A practical distinction must be made between the initial condensation procedure and regular circulation. During condensation, the mixture line is operated at overpressure in order to drive the gas through the Joule--Thomson impedance and condense the mixture. In this regime, leaks are particularly critical because helium mixture can be lost from the system to the surrounding vacuum or laboratory environment. During regular circulation, by contrast, large parts of the return path are operated below atmospheric pressure. Loss of mixture through small leaks is then less likely, but leaks can instead draw residual gas or contamination into the circulation path.

            Such contamination is problematic because the Joule--Thomson impedance is narrow and can become clogged by residual gases or moisture that freeze out at cryogenic temperatures. For this reason, the mixture handling line is passed through a liquid-nitrogen trap before condensation or circulation. The overpressure operation of the Joule--Thomson stage is mainly relevant for condensation. During continuous circulation, the same restriction still defines part of the pressure drop and the counterflow heat exchangers recover cooling from the outgoing stream, but the pressure conditions and practical failure modes are different.

            Once the mixture has condensed and circulation is established, the refrigerator enters its dilution-cooling mode. The Joule--Thomson stage then no longer marks the end point of the cooldown procedure, but becomes part of the continuous circulation path that supplies liquid mixture to the lower-temperature stages. The actual millikelvin cooling is produced in the mixing chamber, as discussed in the following subsection.

            The dilution refrigerator uses the phase separation of liquid \textsuperscript{3}He\,/\,\textsuperscript{4}He mixtures to reach the millikelvin regime. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:setup:phase-diagram}, a liquid mixture below the phase-separation boundary separates into a \textsuperscript{3}He-rich concentrated phase and a \textsuperscript{4}He-rich dilute phase. In the low-temperature limit, the concentrated phase approaches pure \textsuperscript{3}He, while the dilute phase retains a finite \textsuperscript{3}He concentration of about 0.066. This finite solubility is the key property that allows continuous refrigeration: \textsuperscript{3}He can keep entering the dilute phase even as the temperature approaches zero.

            The cooling mechanism is based on the enthalpy difference of \textsuperscript{3}He in the two phases. In the mixing chamber, the phase boundary separates the concentrated phase from the dilute phase. As \textsuperscript{3}He crosses this boundary from the concentrated phase into the dilute phase, energy is required. This energy is absorbed from the mixing chamber and provides the cooling power of the dilution stage. The process is not a one-time mixing event, but is maintained by continuously removing \textsuperscript{3}He from the dilute phase and returning it to the concentrated phase through the circulation loop.

            The circulation path is illustrated schematically in Figure~\ref{fig:setup:dilution-fridge}. The incoming \textsuperscript{3}He-rich stream is first precooled by the outgoing cold stream in the counterflow heat exchangers and then enters the mixing chamber as part of the concentrated phase. At the phase boundary, \textsuperscript{3}He enters the dilute phase and produces cooling. The dilute mixture then flows back toward the still. Since \textsuperscript{3}He has a much higher vapor pressure than \textsuperscript{4}He at the still temperature, heating the still preferentially evaporates \textsuperscript{3}He and maintains the circulation. The evaporated \textsuperscript{3}He-rich gas is pumped away, purified and compressed by the room-temperature gas-handling system, and finally returned to the condensing line. The cooling power of the dilution refrigerator is therefore set primarily by the \textsuperscript{3}He circulation rate and by the efficiency of the heat exchangers, which determine how well the incoming stream is precooled before it reaches the mixing chamber~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011,pobell_matter_nodate}.

            Together, these stages define the thermal environment of the experiment. The pulse tube provides the dominant cooling power at intermediate temperatures, the Joule--Thomson stage condenses the helium mixture and enables continuous circulation, and the dilution unit provides the millikelvin cooling power at the mixing chamber. The sample, filters, thermometers, and wiring are thermally anchored to the corresponding cryostat stages, so that electrical measurements can be performed while maintaining the sample close to the base-temperature stage.

    \subsection{Mechanically Controlled Break Junction} \label{subsec:mcbj}
    %=========================================================
        
        \begin{wrapfigure}[32]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{bruchmechanik.pdf_tex}
            \caption{Rendering of the differential screw, stamp, counterrods and sample holder. Project~\texttt{07062016}.}
            \label{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The mechanically controllable break-junction (MCBJ) setup is used to tune the conductance of the atomic contact in situ at low temperature. The sample is mounted in a three-point bending geometry, where the substrate is supported by two fixed counter supports and bent by a central pushing element, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:bruchmechanik}. In many MCBJ implementations, the counter supports are moved with respect to a fixed pushing element. In the present design, by contrast, the counter supports remain fixed and the central pushing element is displaced. Because the metallic constriction is patterned as a suspended bridge on an elastic substrate, a comparatively large displacement of the bending mechanism is converted into a much smaller elongation of the atomic contact. This mechanical reduction provides the stability required to form and maintain atomic-scale contacts over extended measurement times.

        The break mechanics used in this work is based on the LD400-compatible design developed by A. Fischer and M. Prestel~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}, which follows the same general MCBJ concept as earlier implementations in the group. The relative displacement between the pushing element and the counter supports is generated by a differential screw, which converts the rotary motion of the drive train into the linear motion of the bending mechanism. The screw combines an M4 and an M5 thread. Since the two threads have slightly different pitches, one full revolution does not move the bending mechanism by either pitch individually, but only by their difference. This results in an effective feed of 0.1\,mm per revolution. The differential screw therefore forms the mechanical interface between the rotary drive train and the three-point bending mechanism.

        The central design constraint of the cryogenic drive train is the transmission of a controlled rotary motion from room temperature to the sample space while keeping the heat load on the lower cryostat stages small and avoiding excessive mechanical stress. For this reason, the drive train is mechanically segmented and thermalized at several cryostat stages. Hollow stainless-steel tubes connect the individual gear stages, while the gearboxes simultaneously transmit the motion and provide thermal interruption of the drive shaft.

        In the original implementation, four commercial gearboxes with equal reduction were used along the drive train. For the measurements presented here, this distribution was modified. The gearhead close to the motor was replaced by a 1:9.7 reduction, and the first two cryogenic gearboxes were replaced by 1:5.4 reductions. The remaining gear stages (1:3.1) were left unchanged. The motivation for this modification was to redistribute the required torque more favorably along the drive train. Instead of generating a large torque already at the uppermost cryogenic gearbox, the reduction is distributed over several stages, so that the mechanical load is transferred more gradually toward the actual break mechanics. The increased number of rotations at the 40\,K and 4\,K stages is acceptable, because these stages provide substantially larger cooling power than the millikelvin stages.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{motorhalterung.pdf_tex}
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \caption{Rendering of mounting plates for vacuum feed through and motor. Project~\texttt{13042026}.}
            \label{fig:setup:motorhalterung}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        Several mechanical components of the room-temperature drive were redesigned during this work. In the previous motor mount, the clamping element consisted of a massive plastic disk with a central bore and a narrow radial slit that was closed by a screw. In practice, most of the applied force was spent on elastically deforming the plastic body instead of reducing the bore diameter. In addition, the thread was cut directly into the plastic and was damaged by the high forces required to close the slit. As a result, the mount did not clamp reliably to the motor or to the vacuum feedthrough. This part was replaced by a two-piece clamp manufactured by the scientific workshop, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:motorhalterung}. Separate clamps are now used for the motor and the vacuum feedthrough and are connected by rods, which provides a much stiffer and more reproducible alignment of the room-temperature drive. The motor and gearhead are also connected by a 3D-printed clamp instead of being glued in place.

        The vacuum feedthrough is another critical component of the room--temperature drive. To keep the rotational friction low and to avoid stick--slip motion or excessive load on the motor, the feedthrough was lubricated regularly with Fomblin vacuum grease.

        The slip clutch between the motor and the vacuum feedthrough was also modified. In the original part, the clamping threads were cut into plastic. During operation these threads were repeatedly compressed and deformed, which shifted the clutch away from the rotation axis. This misalignment increased the load on the motor and led to premature motor failure. The plastic threads were therefore replaced by brass inserts. This change improves the centering of the clutch and makes the coupling mechanically more stable under repeated clamping.

        Careful alignment of the cryogenic drive train is essential. The rods inside the cryostat were fixed while already rotating slowly, so that small angular or lateral offsets could relax during assembly. This procedure reduces the probability that the completed drive train locks at low temperature because of a residual misalignment between adjacent stages. The gearboxes were prepared before cooldown by removing the original lubricant. They were cleaned overnight in an ultrasonic acetone bath, subsequently rinsed with isopropanol, and heated with a hot-air gun immediately before cooldown to remove residual solvent as well as moisture adsorbed after cleaning. This preparation is necessary because frozen lubricant, solvent, or water residues can block the gear motion at cryogenic temperatures.

        With these modifications, the break mechanics provides a slow and reproducible conversion from motor rotation to sample bending while keeping the dissipated heat and the mechanical load on the cold stages manageable. The setup therefore allows the contact to be opened and closed repeatedly and to be positioned in the tunneling, few-channel, or atomic-contact regime during low-temperature transport measurements.

        The electrical wiring inside the cryostat has to satisfy two competing requirements. First, the wiring must provide a sufficiently well-defined electrical environment for low-noise transport measurements on high-impedance tunnel contacts and few-channel atomic contacts. This requires filtering of external noise, suppression of unwanted high-frequency components, and a symmetric wiring geometry, so the sample is on zero potential \cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,sprenger_interplay_2019,sobral-rey_interplay_2022}. Second, the same wiring must not introduce an excessive heat load from room temperature to the millikelvin stage and must thermalize the electronic degrees of freedom close to the sample temperature. The wiring concept therefore follows the same general strategy as the earlier MCBJ and SET setups in the group~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018, prestel_magnetic_2020}: resistive low-thermal-conductance wiring is used between temperature stages, the lines are thermalized and filtered repeatedly, and the most sensitive components are mounted close to the sample at the base-temperature stage.

        Figure~\ref{fig:methods:setup} shows the custom wiring used in this work. The sample is mounted at the base-temperature stage and connected by short copper wires with pin heads. Those pinheads separates the different electrical terminals of the device: the bias contacts across the MCBJ--tunnel-barrier series circuit, the DC gate, the AC gate, the antenna line, thermometer wiring, and heater wiring. Short copper wires are used only in the immediate sample region, where good thermalization and low contact resistance are required. From the pin heads onward, the low-frequency lines are routed as coaxial twisted pairs. This geometry reduces inductive pickup, keeps the two wires of a differential signal close to the same electromagnetic environment, and provides additional shielding through the outer conductor. The high-frequency drive lines are routed separately as coaxial microwave lines.

        \subsubsection*{DC Filtering}
        The DC wiring carries the slowly varying bias and gate voltages as well as the voltage signals used to determine the current and the sample voltage. The bias circuit is operated in a quasi-DC mode. The externally applied voltage is swept slowly, while the actual sample voltage and the current through the device are recorded simultaneously. This is important because the MCBJ conductance can change over several orders of magnitude and because the voltage division between reference resistors, filters, wiring impedance, and sample depends on the instantaneous device resistance. Measuring both voltage and current directly avoids relying only on the programmed source voltage.

        The low-frequency lines are filtered and thermalized in several stages. The custom-built copper-powder filters are mounted at the base-temperature stage and suppress high-frequency radiation that would otherwise couple to the sample through the DC leads. In these filters, the signal wire is embedded in compacted copper powder inside a metallic housing. The wire and the surrounding powder form a lossy distributed transmission line: at high frequencies, currents are increasingly confined to the wire surface by the skin effect, while the electromagnetic field couples dissipatively into the surrounding metallic powder. The resulting attenuation is broadband and does not rely on a single lumped resonance. At the same time, the large metal--metal contact area between wire, powder, and housing provides an efficient thermal anchor for the electronic degrees of freedom in the line. This combination of microwave attenuation and thermalization is the main reason why copper-powder filters are commonly used in cryogenic low-level transport measurements~\cite{thalmann_comparison_2017}.

        The attenuation of such distributed filters depends on the conductor material, wire diameter, packing and geometry of the powder, and the thermalization of the filter body. Measurements on comparable cryogenic filter types show that capillary wiring and powder filters can provide strong attenuation in the GHz range, but also that the detailed transmission depends on the conductor material and can contain frequency windows with reduced attenuation. Using several filtering and thermalization stages therefore makes the filtering more robust than relying on a single component. In addition to the copper-powder filters, commercial MFT25 microwave filter--thermalizers are used on selected lines. According to the manufacturer, these filters embed the signal conductor in a highly conductive silver matrix, which acts as a heat exchanger, and the compact MFT25 package contains 25 such filter lines in a gold-plated non-magnetic copper box. The manufacturer specifies strong microwave attenuation above 130\,MHz together with low capacitance and good thermal contact to the millikelvin stage. In this setup, the MFT25 filters are therefore used as compact commercial microwave filter--thermalizers rather than as the only low-pass element of the DC measurement chain.

        The combination of resistive wiring, twisted-pair geometry, copper-powder filters, commercial MFT25 filter--thermalizers, and cold thermal anchoring reduces externally injected noise and helps to keep the electronic temperature of the sample close to the base-temperature environment.

        The reference resistors are mounted in the shielded base-temperature region close to the sample. Their voltage drop is used to infer the current through the device. Placing these resistors cold and close to the sample reduces the length of the high-impedance part of the circuit and keeps the current measurement in the same filtered environment as the sample. The reference-resistor region is additionally shielded, as indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:setup}, to reduce capacitive pickup from neighboring lines. This is particularly relevant for superconducting subgap measurements, where small current contributions must be resolved on top of a strongly nonlinear current--voltage characteristic.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{AC Cabling}
        %=========================================================

        In addition to the DC bias and gate lines, the setup contains two high-frequency paths for microwave excitation. One path drives the AC gate on the sample chip, while the other drives the on-chip antenna or stripline. Both are routed as coaxial lines, because at GHz frequencies impedance control, shielding, and reproducible attenuation matter more than the low-frequency symmetry that is useful for the DC wiring. The microwave lines are thermalized by attenuators mounted at cryogenic stages. In the present setup, the attenuators indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:setup} are mounted at the 4\,K stage, where they suppress room-temperature microwave noise and define part of the total transmission to the sample.

        The important point is not that these two paths realize fundamentally different excitation schemes. Rather, they exhibit different transmission to the sample, and that transmission depends on frequency. That difference is useful, because it gives access to different effective power windows for different microwave tones without requiring an absolute calibration of the local field. Together with Patrick Raif~\cite{raif_electronic_2024}, we showed that both paths act, in practice, like antennas for the device: part of the microwave power is launched already at the pin head, and from the point of view of the sample the two paths behave more like two different antennas than like two sharply separated drive mechanisms. The AC gate couples capacitively to the island region, while the stripline or antenna couples through the local microwave field in the sample vicinity. What we can state reliably is the relative transmission difference between the paths, not the absolute microwave amplitude at the junction.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Thermometry and Heating}
        %=========================================================

        The sample space is also equipped with thermometer and heater wiring to monitor and control the local temperature of the base-temperature stage. These lines are routed separately from the transport and microwave paths so that heater or thermometer signals do not leak into the sample circuit. The thermometer provides the local cryostat temperature during cooldown, thermalization, and measurements. The heater allows controlled heating of the stage, for example for thermal cycling, temperature-dependent checks, or recovery after mechanical motion of the break junction has dissipated heat into the cold stage.

        The thermometer and heater lines are filtered and thermalized for the same reason as the transport lines: they connect room-temperature electronics to the millikelvin stage and can therefore act as both thermal and electrical noise channels. Their requirements are less restrictive than those of the transport lines with respect to voltage noise at the sample, but they still have to be sufficiently well anchored to avoid parasitic heating. In practice, the thermometer and heater wiring is therefore treated as part of the same cryogenic wiring system rather than as auxiliary cabling that can be ignored once the electrical transport circuit is defined.
