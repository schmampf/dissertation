\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:methods:setup}
%=========================================================

    The experimental setup combines a dilution refrigerator, a mechanically controlled break junction, and a filtered low-noise wiring environment. Together, these subsystems define how superconducting atomic contacts and tunnel devices are formed, tuned, and measured. The cryostat provides the millikelvin environment required to resolve superconducting energy scales. The break-junction mechanics adjusts the contact conductance in situ from the tunneling regime to few-channel and atomic-scale contacts. The wiring, filtering, microwave lines, and grounding scheme determine the bias condition, noise environment, thermalization, and high-frequency coupling. This section follows the order in which these elements constrain the measurement: cryogenic environment, mechanical contact tuning, and electrical measurement chain.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Cryostat}
    \label{subsec:setup:cryostat}
    %=========================================================

        The measurements were carried out in an LD400 Bluefors dry dilution refrigerator. The cryostat combines several cooling stages, each of which removes heat in a different temperature range and prepares the next stage for operation. In the following, the cooling process is discussed along this sequence: first, the pulse-tube cryocooler provides closed-cycle precooling to the few-kelvin range; second, Joule--Thomson cooling is used to condense and further cool the circulating helium mixture; and third, the dilution refrigerator reaches the millikelvin regime by exploiting the enthalpy of mixing of \textsuperscript{3}He and \textsuperscript{4}He.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Pulse Tube Cryocooler}
        %=========================================================

            The first cooling stage of the dry cryostat is a two-stage Gifford--McMahon-type pulse-tube cryocooler. It is a closed-cycle refrigerator in which helium is periodically compressed and expanded, but the cold head contains no mechanical displacer. The pressure oscillation is generated at room temperature by a compressor package and rotary valve. Regenerators, heat exchangers, pulse-tube volumes, and flow impedances then convert this oscillating gas flow into refrigeration. In the Bluefors LD400 system, the pulse tube precools the radiation shields, thermalizes wiring, and brings the helium mixture to the few-kelvin range.

            The basic pulse-tube principle is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:pulsetube}(a) for a single-stage orifice pulse-tube refrigerator, following Refs.~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011, bar-cohen_low_2019}. From the warm end to the cold end, the stage contains an aftercooler, a regenerator, a cold heat exchanger, the pulse-tube volume, a warm heat exchanger, and a flow impedance connected to a buffer volume. The compressor imposes a periodic pressure variation, so the helium oscillates through the cold head rather than circulating in one direction. In the regenerator, the gas exchanges heat with a porous matrix of high heat capacity, which maintains the temperature gradient between the warm and cold ends.

            The pulse tube replaces the mechanical displacer by a gas column that acts as a virtual piston. Near the warm end, gas enters from the buffer when the pulse-tube pressure is low and leaves again when the pressure is high. Because compression heats the gas and expansion cools it, gas parcels near the warm end reject heat to the warm heat exchanger. At the cold end, the phase timing is such that gas returns to the cold heat exchanger at lower temperature and absorbs heat from the cold stage. The useful cooling is therefore associated with a net enthalpy flow through the pulse-tube gas column.

            The phase relation between pressure and volume flow is the central design parameter. The warm-end impedance, realized by an orifice, an inertance tube, a reservoir, or a combination of these elements, delays gas exchange with the buffer volume and shifts the gas displacement relative to the pressure oscillation. The compressor and rotary valve provide the pressure drive, while the impedance network determines when gas moves. This phase shift replaces the function of the cold displacer in a conventional Gifford--McMahon cooler.

            In a Gifford--McMahon-type pulse tube, the compressor maintains high- and low-pressure helium reservoirs, and a rotary valve alternately connects the cold head to these pressure levels. The drive is therefore similar to that of a Gifford--McMahon cryocooler, while the cold head remains a pulse-tube cold head. This distinction matters experimentally. The absence of a moving displacer at low temperature reduces wear and direct vibration close to the sample, but the rotary valve, pressure oscillations, and gas dynamics still generate vibrations that can couple into sensitive transport measurements.

            To reach the few-kelvin range, two pulse-tube stages are cascaded, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:pulsetube}(b) and described in Ref.~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011}. The first stage removes the dominant heat load at intermediate temperatures and provides a thermal anchor near 40\,K. The second stage is precooled by the first stage and reaches the few-kelvin range. In the idealized U-shaped two-stage geometry, the cold ends are placed conveniently for cryostat integration, while heat from the warm end of the second pulse tube is not imposed on the first stage.

            The pulse-tube system is driven by an oil-lubricated room-temperature helium compressor. Low-pressure helium returning from the cold head is compressed, cooled, purified, and supplied again as high-pressure helium. Oil lubricates the moving parts, improves sealing, and helps remove heat generated during compression. Before the helium returns to the cold head, oil separators, coalescing filters, and adsorbers remove oil droplets, mist, and residual contaminants. This purification chain is essential because impurities can freeze out at cryogenic temperatures and degrade the regenerator or block small flow impedances.\footnote{During this work, several maintenance interventions were required on the pulse-tube compressor package. The compressor adsorber was replaced as part of the oil-management and helium-purification chain, and the compressor heat exchanger required repeated repairs due to leaks. One contributing practical issue is that the compressor cooling water is supplied from Lake Constance. The lake water is very soft and can therefore be corrosive to copper cooling-water circuits if not chemically conditioned. Over time, this can promote corrosion and gradual dissolution of copper piping. Due to insufficient filtering, the water may contain dirt particles with diameters of up to 1\,mm, which can further abrade the piping. In addition, Lake Constance has been affected by the invasive quagga mussel, which can contribute shell and mussel debris to technical water circuits~\cite{haltiner_distribution_2022}. Eventually, a leak between the cooling-water circuit and the helium circuit led to replacement of the compressor package.}

        \subsubsection*{Joule--Thomson Cooling}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-2.0em}
                \import{methods/setup/cryo}{joule-thomson.pgf}
                \vspace{-2.0em}
                \caption{Joule--Thomson coefficient of \textsuperscript{4}He as a function of temperature. The curve was calculated for a pressure of $p = 1\,\mathrm{bar}$ using CoolProp~\cite{bell_pure_2014} and illustrates the change of sign at the inversion temperature.}
                \label{fig:setup:joule-thomson}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            After the pulse tube has cooled the cryostat to the few-kelvin range, the circulating helium mixture still has to be condensed before it can enter the dilution circuit. Condensation cannot be performed efficiently at room temperature, because the incoming gas would carry too much enthalpy into the cold part of the cryostat. The high-pressure mixture is therefore thermalized at the pulse-tube stages and then expanded through a flow impedance. This expansion stage is the Joule--Thomson stage of the refrigerator~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011,pobell_matter_nodate}.

            Joule--Thomson cooling is based on the temperature change of a real gas during expansion at approximately constant enthalpy $H$, given by
            \begin{equation}
                H = U + pV\,,\qquad \Delta H \approx 0\,,
            \end{equation}
            with internal energy $U$, pressure $p$, and volume $V$. During throttling through a narrow restriction, no mechanical work is extracted and ideally no heat is exchanged within the impedance. The gas therefore changes pressure without changing its enthalpy appreciably.

            For a small pressure drop, the corresponding temperature change can be written as
            \begin{equation}
                \Delta T \approx \mu_\mathrm{JT}\, \Delta p\,,
            \end{equation}
            where $\mu_\mathrm{JT}$ is the Joule--Thomson coefficient, which is given by
            \begin{equation}
                \mu_\mathrm{JT} = \left( \tfrac{\partial T}{\partial p} \right)_H = \tfrac{V}{C_\mathrm{p}} (\alpha T -1)\,.
            \end{equation}
            Here $C_\mathrm{p}$ is the heat capacity at constant pressure and $\alpha$ is the thermal expansion coefficient. The Joule--Thomson coefficient is the slope of an isenthalpic path in the temperature-pressure plane, and its sign determines whether a pressure drop cools or heats the gas. For an ideal gas, the enthalpy depends only on temperature, so an isenthalpic expansion would not change the temperature. Joule--Thomson cooling is therefore a real-gas effect. In the expression above, this appears through the factor $\alpha T - 1$, which vanishes for an ideal gas.

            The sign of $\mu_\mathrm{JT}$ changes at the inversion temperature, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:joule-thomson}. On one side of this point, an isenthalpic pressure drop cools the gas. On the other side, it heats it. Helium has an unusually low inversion temperature, so room-temperature helium would not be cooled by a Joule--Thomson expansion. The pulse tube is therefore essential because it brings the gas into the regime where the subsequent expansion cools the mixture.

            In the dry dilution refrigerator, the incoming high-pressure mixture is cooled stepwise before it reaches the Joule--Thomson impedance. It is anchored at the pulse-tube stages and exchanges heat with colder return flows in the lower-temperature part of the dilution unit. After this precooling, the pressure drop across the impedance lowers the temperature further and condenses part of the mixture. The Joule--Thomson stage thus links pulse-tube precooling to dilution refrigeration by converting a precooled, pressurized gas flow into a cold liquid mixture for continuous circulation.

            A practical distinction must be made between initial condensation and regular circulation. During condensation, the mixture line is operated at overpressure to drive gas through the impedance and condense it. Leaks are then critical because mixture can be lost to the surrounding vacuum or laboratory environment. During regular circulation, by contrast, large parts of the return path are below atmospheric pressure. Small leaks are less likely to lose mixture, but they can draw residual gas or contamination into the circulation path.

            This contamination is problematic because the Joule--Thomson impedance is narrow and can become clogged by residual gases or moisture that freeze out at cryogenic temperatures. For this reason, the mixture handling line is passed through a liquid-nitrogen trap before condensation or circulation. During continuous circulation, the same restriction still defines part of the pressure drop and the counterflow heat exchangers recover cooling from the outgoing stream, but the pressure conditions and failure modes are different.

            Once the mixture has condensed and circulation is established, the Joule--Thomson stage becomes part of the continuous circulation path that supplies liquid mixture to the lower-temperature stages. The actual millikelvin cooling is produced in the mixing chamber, as discussed in the following subsection.

            The dilution refrigerator uses the phase separation of liquid \textsuperscript{3}He\,/\,\textsuperscript{4}He mixtures to reach the millikelvin regime. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:phase-diagram}, a liquid mixture below the phase-separation boundary separates into a \textsuperscript{3}He-rich concentrated phase and a \textsuperscript{4}He-rich dilute phase. In the low-temperature limit, the concentrated phase approaches pure \textsuperscript{3}He, while the dilute phase retains a finite \textsuperscript{3}He concentration of about 0.066. This finite solubility is the key property that allows continuous refrigeration: \textsuperscript{3}He can keep entering the dilute phase even as the temperature approaches zero.

            The cooling mechanism is based on the enthalpy difference of \textsuperscript{3}He in the two phases. In the mixing chamber, the phase boundary separates the concentrated phase from the dilute phase. As \textsuperscript{3}He crosses this boundary from the concentrated phase into the dilute phase, energy is required. This energy is absorbed from the mixing chamber and provides the cooling power of the dilution stage. The process is not a one-time mixing event, but is maintained by continuously removing \textsuperscript{3}He from the dilute phase and returning it to the concentrated phase through the circulation loop.

            The circulation path is illustrated schematically in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:dilution-fridge}. The incoming \textsuperscript{3}He-rich stream is first precooled by the outgoing cold stream in the counterflow heat exchangers and then enters the mixing chamber as part of the concentrated phase. At the phase boundary, \textsuperscript{3}He enters the dilute phase and produces cooling. The dilute mixture then flows back toward the still. Since \textsuperscript{3}He has a much higher vapor pressure than \textsuperscript{4}He at the still temperature, heating the still preferentially evaporates \textsuperscript{3}He and maintains the circulation. The evaporated \textsuperscript{3}He-rich gas is pumped away, purified and compressed by the room-temperature gas-handling system, and finally returned to the condensing line. The cooling power of the dilution refrigerator is therefore set primarily by the \textsuperscript{3}He circulation rate and by the efficiency of the heat exchangers, which determine how well the incoming stream is precooled before it reaches the mixing chamber~\cite{de_waele_basic_2011,pobell_matter_nodate}.

            Together, these stages define the thermal environment of the experiment. The pulse tube provides the dominant cooling power at intermediate temperatures, the Joule--Thomson stage condenses the helium mixture and enables continuous circulation, and the dilution unit provides the millikelvin cooling power at the mixing chamber. The sample, filters, thermometers, and wiring are thermally anchored to the corresponding cryostat stages, so that electrical measurements can be performed while maintaining the sample close to the base-temperature stage.

    \subsection{Mechanically Controlled Break Junction}
    \label{subsec:setup:mcbj}
    %=========================================================
        
        \begin{wrapfigure}[32]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{bruchmechanik.pdf_tex}
            \caption{Rendering of the differential screw, stamp, counterrods and sample holder. Project~\texttt{07062016}.}
            \label{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        The mechanically controllable break-junction (MCBJ) setup is used to tune the conductance of the atomic contact in situ at low temperature. The sample is mounted in a three-point bending geometry, where the substrate is supported by two fixed counter supports and bent by a central pushing element, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}. In many MCBJ implementations, the counter supports are moved with respect to a fixed pushing element. In the present design, by contrast, the counter supports remain fixed and the central pushing element is displaced. Because the metallic constriction is patterned as a suspended bridge on an elastic substrate, a comparatively large displacement of the bending mechanism is converted into a much smaller elongation of the atomic contact. This mechanical reduction provides the stability required to form and maintain atomic-scale contacts over extended measurement times.

        The break mechanics used in this work is based on the LD400-compatible design developed by A. Fischer and M. Prestel~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}, which follows the same general MCBJ concept as earlier implementations in the group. The relative displacement between the pushing element and the counter supports is generated by a differential screw, which converts the rotary motion of the drive train into the linear motion of the bending mechanism. The screw combines an M4 and an M5 thread. Since the two threads have slightly different pitches, one full revolution does not move the bending mechanism by either pitch individually, but only by their difference. This results in an effective feed of 0.1\,mm per revolution. The differential screw therefore forms the mechanical interface between the rotary drive train and the three-point bending mechanism.

        The central design constraint of the cryogenic drive train is the transmission of a controlled rotary motion from room temperature to the sample space while keeping the heat load on the lower cryostat stages small and avoiding excessive mechanical stress. For this reason, the drive train is mechanically segmented and thermalized at several cryostat stages. Hollow stainless-steel tubes connect the individual gear stages, while the gearboxes simultaneously transmit the motion and provide thermal interruption of the drive shaft.

        In the original implementation, four commercial gearboxes with equal reduction were used along the drive train. For the measurements presented here, this distribution was modified. The gearhead close to the motor was replaced by a 1:9.7 reduction, and the first two cryogenic gearboxes were replaced by 1:5.4 reductions. The remaining gear stages (1:3.1) were left unchanged. The motivation for this modification was to redistribute the required torque more favorably along the drive train. Instead of generating a large torque already at the uppermost cryogenic gearbox, the reduction is distributed over several stages, so that the mechanical load is transferred more gradually toward the actual break mechanics. The increased number of rotations at the 40\,K and 4\,K stages is acceptable, because these stages provide substantially larger cooling power than the millikelvin stages.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{motorhalterung.pdf_tex}
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \caption{Rendering of mounting plates for vacuum feed through and motor. Project~\texttt{13042026}.}
            \label{fig:setup:motorhalterung}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        Several mechanical components of the room-temperature drive were redesigned during this work. In the previous motor mount, the clamping element consisted of a massive plastic disk with a central bore and a narrow radial slit that was closed by a screw. In practice, most of the applied force was spent on elastically deforming the plastic body instead of reducing the bore diameter. In addition, the thread was cut directly into the plastic and was damaged by the high forces required to close the slit. As a result, the mount did not clamp reliably to the motor or to the vacuum feedthrough. This part was replaced by a two-piece clamp manufactured by the scientific workshop, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:motorhalterung}. Separate clamps are now used for the motor and the vacuum feedthrough and are connected by rods, which provides a much stiffer and more reproducible alignment of the room-temperature drive. The motor and gearhead are also connected by a 3D-printed clamp instead of being glued in place.

        The vacuum feedthrough is another critical component of the room--temperature drive. To keep the rotational friction low and to avoid stick--slip motion or excessive load on the motor, the feedthrough was lubricated regularly with Fomblin vacuum grease.

        The slip clutch between the motor and the vacuum feedthrough was also modified. In the original part, the clamping threads were cut into plastic. During operation these threads were repeatedly compressed and deformed, which shifted the clutch away from the rotation axis. This misalignment increased the load on the motor and led to premature motor failure. The plastic threads were therefore replaced by brass inserts. This change improves the centering of the clutch and makes the coupling mechanically more stable under repeated clamping.

        Careful alignment of the cryogenic drive train is essential. The rods inside the cryostat were fixed while already rotating slowly, so that small angular or lateral offsets could relax during assembly. This procedure reduces the probability that the completed drive train locks at low temperature because of a residual misalignment between adjacent stages. The gearboxes were prepared before cooldown by removing the original lubricant. They were cleaned overnight in an ultrasonic acetone bath, subsequently rinsed with isopropanol, and heated with a hot-air gun immediately before cooldown to remove residual solvent as well as moisture adsorbed after cleaning. This preparation is necessary because frozen lubricant, solvent, or water residues can block the gear motion at cryogenic temperatures.

        With these modifications, the break mechanics provides a slow and reproducible conversion from motor rotation to sample bending while keeping the dissipated heat and the mechanical load on the cold stages manageable. The setup therefore allows the contact to be opened and closed repeatedly and to be positioned in the tunneling, few-channel, or atomic-contact regime during low-temperature transport measurements.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Electrical Measurement Environment}
    \label{subsec:setup:eletrical}
    %=========================================================

        The electrical setup connects a nanoscale device at millikelvin temperature to room-temperature sources, amplifiers, microwave equipment, and control electronics. This makes the wiring more than a passive connection. It defines the bias condition of the sample, sets the noise environment, determines how well the electronic degrees of freedom are thermalized, and limits which microwave amplitudes can be applied without excessive heating. In the following, I first introduce the measurement schematic used to reconstruct the sample voltage and current. I then describe the cryostat wiring, filtering, microwave lines, thermometry, and the grounding strategy of the complete setup.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Measurement Schematic}
        %=========================================================

            The transport measurement is based on a voltage-biased circuit with simultaneous readout of sample voltage and current. A programmed bias voltage $V_\mathrm{bias}$ is applied to the bias line, but the voltage arriving at the device is not assumed to be identical to this source voltage. The finite resistance of filters, leads, reference resistors, and contacts creates a voltage divider together with the sample. Therefore both relevant quantities are measured directly: $V_\mathrm{sample}$ is measured across the device, and the current is inferred from the voltage drop $V_\mathrm{ref}$ across a known reference resistor.

            A useful way to characterize the bias condition is the ratio
            \begin{equation}
                \eta = R_\mathrm{series}\,/\, R_\mathrm{sample}\,,
                \label{eq:setup:bias-eta}
            \end{equation}
            where $R_\mathrm{sample}$ is the instantaneous device resistance and $R_\mathrm{series}$ is the remaining resistance in the bias path,
            \begin{equation}
                R_\mathrm{series}
                % = R_\mathrm{total}-R_\mathrm{sample}
                \simeq 2R_\mathrm{ref} + R_\mathrm{filters} + R_\mathrm{leads} + \ldots\,.
                \label{eq:setup:series-resistance}
            \end{equation}
            In the limit $\eta \ll 1$, the circuit approaches an ideal voltage bias because most of the applied voltage drops across the sample. In the opposite limit, $\eta \gg 1$, the series resistance dominates and the circuit approaches a current-bias condition. The present setup is deliberately flexible, but this also means that the bias condition changes with the state of the MCBJ. During a measurement the sample resistance can range from a few ohms in a closed atomic contact to many megaohms in the tunneling regime, while the series resistance of the filtered bias environment is of order 100\,k\textohm. The experiment therefore moves between voltage-biased, mixed, and more current-biased regimes depending on the junction configuration.

            This is the reason why both $V_\mathrm{sample}$ and $I_\mathrm{sample}$ are reconstructed from measured voltages rather than inferred from the programmed source value. The current is obtained from the reference resistor,
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{sample} = V_\mathrm{ref}\,/\,R_\mathrm{ref}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:sample-current}
            \end{equation}
            In a linearized resistive approximation, the voltage and current would follow
            \begin{align}
                V_\mathrm{sample} &\simeq V_\mathrm{bias}\,R_\mathrm{sample}\,/\,R_\mathrm{total}\,, \\
                I_\mathrm{sample} &\simeq V_\mathrm{bias}\,/\,R_\mathrm{total}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:bias-divider}
            \end{align}
            These expressions are useful for intuition, but the actual analysis uses the simultaneously measured voltage drops. This is essential for nonlinear superconducting transport, where the sample resistance changes with bias and where small deviations from an ideal bias condition can distort the apparent \textit{I--V} curve.

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema}(a) shows the standard configuration used for high-impedance tunnel and few-channel measurements. For very low sample impedances, however, the large cold series resistance limits the voltage that can be applied across the sample. A substantial part of the programmed bias then drops across the reference resistors instead of the device, so that the accessible sample-voltage range can become too small to drive the junction out of the zero-voltage supercurrent branch or to reach spectroscopic features such as the superconducting gap. In this regime I use the modified configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema}(b), which reduces the effective cold series resistance to about 500\,\textohm. The additional warm reference resistor is then used for current readout. Although its resistance is substantially smaller than in the standard configuration, it is located at room temperature and therefore adds Johnson--Nyquist noise from the warm environment. The low-impedance configuration is therefore used only when the increased accessible sample-voltage range outweighs this added noise contribution.

        \subsubsection*{Wiring inside Cryostat}
        %=========================================================
        \begin{figure}[t!]
            \centering
            {\fontsize{7}{9}\selectfont

        The electrical wiring inside the cryostat has to satisfy two competing requirements. First, the wiring must provide a sufficiently well-defined electrical environment for low-noise transport measurements on high-impedance tunnel contacts and few-channel atomic contacts. This requires filtering of external noise, suppression of unwanted high-frequency components, and a symmetric wiring geometry, so the sample is on zero potential \cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,sprenger_interplay_2019,sobral-rey_interplay_2022}. Second, the same wiring must not introduce an excessive heat load from room temperature to the millikelvin stage and must thermalize the electronic degrees of freedom close to the sample temperature. The wiring concept therefore follows the same general strategy as the earlier MCBJ and SET setups in the group~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018, prestel_magnetic_2020}: resistive low-thermal-conductance wiring is used between temperature stages, the lines are thermalized and filtered repeatedly, and the most sensitive components are mounted close to the sample at the base-temperature stage.

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:setup} shows the custom wiring used in this work. The sample is mounted at the base-temperature stage and connected by short copper wires with pin heads. Those pinheads separates the different electrical terminals of the device: the bias contacts across the MCBJ--tunnel-barrier series circuit, the DC gate, the AC gate, the antenna line, thermometer wiring, and heater wiring. Short copper wires are used only in the immediate sample region, where good thermalization and low contact resistance are required. From the pin heads onward, the low-frequency lines are routed as coaxial twisted pairs. This geometry reduces inductive pickup, keeps the two wires of a differential signal close to the same electromagnetic environment, and provides additional shielding through the outer conductor. The high-frequency drive lines are routed separately as coaxial microwave lines.

        \subsubsection*{DC Filtering}
        The DC wiring carries the slowly varying bias and gate voltages as well as the voltage signals used to determine the current and the sample voltage. The bias circuit is operated in a quasi-DC mode. The externally applied voltage is swept slowly, while the actual sample voltage and the current through the device are recorded simultaneously. This is important because the MCBJ conductance can change over several orders of magnitude and because the voltage division between reference resistors, filters, wiring impedance, and sample depends on the instantaneous device resistance. Measuring both voltage and current directly avoids relying only on the programmed source voltage.

        The low-frequency lines are filtered and thermalized in several stages. The custom-built copper-powder filters are mounted at the base-temperature stage and suppress high-frequency radiation that would otherwise couple to the sample through the DC leads. In these filters, the signal wire is embedded in compacted copper powder inside a metallic housing. The wire and the surrounding powder form a lossy distributed transmission line: at high frequencies, currents are increasingly confined to the wire surface by the skin effect, while the electromagnetic field couples dissipatively into the surrounding metallic powder. The resulting attenuation is broadband and does not rely on a single lumped resonance. At the same time, the large metal--metal contact area between wire, powder, and housing provides an efficient thermal anchor for the electronic degrees of freedom in the line. This combination of microwave attenuation and thermalization is the main reason why copper-powder filters are commonly used in cryogenic low-level transport measurements~\cite{thalmann_comparison_2017}.

        The attenuation of such distributed filters depends on the conductor material, wire diameter, packing and geometry of the powder, and the thermalization of the filter body. Measurements on comparable cryogenic filter types show that capillary wiring and powder filters can provide strong attenuation in the GHz range, but also that the detailed transmission depends on the conductor material and can contain frequency windows with reduced attenuation. Using several filtering and thermalization stages therefore makes the filtering more robust than relying on a single component. In addition to the copper-powder filters, commercial MFT25 microwave filter--thermalizers are used on selected lines. According to the manufacturer, these filters embed the signal conductor in a highly conductive silver matrix, which acts as a heat exchanger, and the compact MFT25 package contains 25 such filter lines in a gold-plated non-magnetic copper box. The manufacturer specifies strong microwave attenuation above 130\,MHz together with low capacitance and good thermal contact to the millikelvin stage. In this setup, the MFT25 filters are therefore used as compact commercial microwave filter--thermalizers rather than as the only low-pass element of the DC measurement chain.

        The combination of resistive wiring, twisted-pair geometry, copper-powder filters, commercial MFT25 filter--thermalizers, and cold thermal anchoring reduces externally injected noise and helps to keep the electronic temperature of the sample close to the base-temperature environment.

        The reference resistors are mounted in the shielded base-temperature region close to the sample. Their voltage drop is used to infer the current through the device. Placing these resistors cold and close to the sample reduces the length of the high-impedance part of the circuit and keeps the current measurement in the same filtered environment as the sample. The reference-resistor region is additionally shielded, as indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:setup}, to reduce capacitive pickup from neighboring lines. This is particularly relevant for superconducting subgap measurements, where small current contributions must be resolved on top of a strongly nonlinear current--voltage characteristic.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{AC Cabling}
        %=========================================================

        In addition to the DC bias and gate lines, the setup contains two high-frequency paths for microwave excitation. One path drives the AC gate on the sample chip, while the other drives the on-chip antenna or stripline. Both are routed as coaxial lines, because at GHz frequencies impedance control, shielding, and reproducible attenuation matter more than the low-frequency symmetry that is useful for the DC wiring. The microwave lines are thermalized by attenuators mounted at cryogenic stages. In the present setup, the attenuators indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:setup} are mounted at the 4\,K stage, where they suppress room-temperature microwave noise and define part of the total transmission to the sample.

        The important point is not that these two paths realize fundamentally different excitation schemes. Rather, they exhibit different transmission to the sample, and that transmission depends on frequency. That difference is useful, because it gives access to different effective power windows for different microwave tones without requiring an absolute calibration of the local field. Together with Patrick Raif~\cite{raif_electronic_2024}, we showed that both paths act, in practice, like antennas for the device: part of the microwave power is launched already at the pin head, and from the point of view of the sample the two paths behave more like two different antennas than like two sharply separated drive mechanisms. The AC gate couples capacitively to the island region, while the stripline or antenna couples through the local microwave field in the sample vicinity. What we can state reliably is the relative transmission difference between the paths, not the absolute microwave amplitude at the junction.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Thermometry and Heating}
        %=========================================================

        The sample space is also equipped with thermometer and heater wiring to monitor and control the local temperature of the base-temperature stage. These lines are routed separately from the transport and microwave paths so that heater or thermometer signals do not leak into the sample circuit. The thermometer provides the local cryostat temperature during cooldown, thermalization, and measurements. The heater allows controlled heating of the stage, for example for thermal cycling, temperature-dependent checks, or recovery after mechanical motion of the break junction has dissipated heat into the cold stage.

        The thermometer and heater lines are filtered and thermalized for the same reason as the transport lines: they connect room-temperature electronics to the millikelvin stage and can therefore act as both thermal and electrical noise channels. Their requirements are less restrictive than those of the transport lines with respect to voltage noise at the sample, but they still have to be sufficiently well anchored to avoid parasitic heating. In practice, the thermometer and heater wiring is therefore treated as part of the same cryogenic wiring system rather than as auxiliary cabling that can be ignored once the electrical transport circuit is defined.

        \subsubsection*{Instrumentation and Grounding Schematic}
        %=========================================================
        \begin{figure}[t!]
            \centering
            {\fontsize{6}{9}\selectfont
            \import{methods/setup/electric}{grounding-schema.pdf_tex}
            }
            \caption{Grounding and instrument schematic of the room-temperature setup. Building ground and cryostat ground are kept separate except for a 13\,M\textohm{} connection~(G) through the temperature controller. The motor controller (B) and LAN switch (W) are floating because their plugs do not provide a protective-earth connection. The magnet power supply (Y) was also operated floating, which minimized the observed noise in the measured spectra.}
            \label{fig:setup:grounding}
        \end{figure}

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:grounding} summarizes the room-temperature instruments and their grounding connections. The cryostat was treated as the local ground reference of the experiment. This is practical because the cryostat is a large metallic body with a stable electrical potential, while the setup did not provide a separate measurement ground comparable to the P5 setup~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018}. The main grounding rule was therefore to avoid uncontrolled loops between building ground, computer interfaces, and the cryostat body. The control computer was connected to the laboratory network, while GPIB and RS-232 instruments were interfaced through USB adapters. USB connections were isolated either by USB-over-LAN links or by galvanic USB isolators. The cryostat control unit\footnote{Bluefors LD400, connected by USB} was included in this isolated control chain.

        The voltage signals were first amplified by voltage preamplifiers\footnote{Femto Pre-Amplifier DLPVA, power supply \texttt{WW-98000627}, communication Luci10, connected by USB}. These amplifiers provide selectable gains and a low-pass bandwidth of about 100\,kHz. Their output voltage is referenced to ground and is digitized by the analog inputs of the measurement card\footnote{ADwin Gold II, connected by LAN}. Although the ADwin can sample at rates up to 100\,kHz, much faster than required for the quasi-dc sweeps used here, the data stream was reduced on the ADwin to 137\,Hz.

        The bias voltage was generated by an analog output of the same measurement card. Before entering the cryostat wiring, it passed through a warm symmetric voltage divider and a low-pass filter\footnote{Thorlabs EF120}. The divider reduced the full output range of the card to the voltage window relevant for the sample, thereby improving the effective bias resolution. Its resistances were chosen low enough that their thermal noise and their contribution to the total source impedance remained limited. The divider was kept at room temperature, in contrast to the cold shunt used in Ref.~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. This choice preserved access to filter, wiring, and grounding diagnostics during cooldown and low-temperature operation, following the same practical motivation as in Ref.~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018}. A shunt mounted inside the cryostat would have fixed part of the bias environment locally, but it would also have made several grounding and continuity checks inaccessible during a cooldown.

        Several combinations of available voltage sources, amplifiers, and multimeters were tested before settling on the configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:grounding}. The practical optimization criterion was the low-frequency noise in the recorded sample- and reference-voltage channels under the same filtering and grounding conditions used for transport measurements. Using one measurement card for both voltage channels also ensured that the sample-voltage and reference-voltage readings were acquired as synchronized pairs. During a typical measurement, the bias was applied symmetrically around ground, so that the common-mode voltage of the sample remained close to zero. The voltage was then ramped slowly down and up, with full periods of 5\,min or longer.

        The remaining instruments can be grouped by function. The DC gate voltage was applied with respect to ground by a high-precision voltage source\footnote{Yokogawa GS200, connected by GPIB}, and all DC lines were routed through a break-out box\footnote{Major Tom \texttt{WW-2280090}} into the commercial cryostat cabling. The microwave excitation paths were driven by the two ports of a vector network analyzer\footnote{Rohde \& Schwarz ZNB40, connected by LAN}, which were connected to the AC gate and antenna lines. Temperature monitoring and local heating were handled by an AC resistance bridge\footnote{Lakeshore 372AC \& 3726, connected by USB}, which read out the sample heater, the sample thermometer, and auxiliary thermometers. Mechanical actuation of the break junction was provided by an encoded motor and driver\footnote{Faulhaber Motor 2232R024S, Encoder R IE2-1024 4613, Driver MCDC3002S RS, Gear Head 22/2S, connected by RS-232}. A one-axis 7\,T magnet supply\footnote{American Magnetics Inc. AMI430, connected by LAN} was used when superconductivity had to be suppressed. Additional diagnostic access was provided by a precision source\footnote{Keysight B2962A, connected by LAN} and several digital multimeters\footnote{Keysight 34461A, connected by LAN}.

        For the lowest-noise spectra, devices that were not required during the actual sweep were disconnected from the setup. In particular, the magnet controller and magnet power supply were usually disconnected when not in use. The communication cables of the preamplifiers were removed whenever remote gain control was not needed. This reduced the number of possible ground-loop and pickup paths connected to the sensitive voltage-readout circuit.

        The final grounding configuration was therefore not imposed by the nominal instrument grounds alone, but selected empirically from configurations that preserved a well-defined cryostat reference while minimizing pickup in the measured voltage channels.
