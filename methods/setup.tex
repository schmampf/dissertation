\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:methods:setup}
%=========================================================

    The experiments in this thesis require a mechanically controlled break junction (MCBJ) that can form and tune superconducting atomic contacts at millikelvin temperatures, together with a filtered electrical environment for nonlinear transport measurements and microwave excitation. The dilution refrigerator provides the low-temperature environment and thermal anchors for the sample and wiring. The junction mechanics define the contact-control coordinate, while the bias circuit, filtering, microwave coupling, instrumentation, and grounding determine the experimentally accessible voltages, currents, and drive conditions.

    These parts cannot be treated as independent accessories. A low base temperature is only useful if the electrical lines are filtered and thermalized well enough that the electronic temperature follows the cold stage. A nominal bias voltage is meaningful only if the voltage division between reference resistors, filters, leads, and the sample is measured rather than assumed. A microwave source power is likewise not the same as a calibrated local microwave voltage at the junction. The setup therefore defines both the experimental control parameters and the limits of their interpretation.

    The platform was inherited from earlier MCBJ experiments in the group, but it was modified substantially during this work for superconducting devices, microwave irradiation, local thermometry, and more robust repeated cooldowns. Mechanical parts were replaced, gearbox ratios were redistributed, fragile wiring was removed, filter and reference-resistor layouts were revised, and the grounding configuration was selected empirically from noise measurements. These practical details determine whether the data are reproducible. They also state which assumptions are justified when later chapters interpret \textit{I--V} curves, derived \textit{dI--dV} spectra, and microwave-induced features.

    I first describe the MCBJ mechanics and the conversion from motor motion to the relative contact-control coordinate in Sec.~\ref{subsec:setup:mcbj}. I then define the cold electrical measurement environment, including voltage and current reconstruction, filtering, and microwave coupling in Sec.~\ref{subsec:setup:electrical}. Finally, I describe the instrumentation and grounding, including the practical cryostat ground reference and the isolation choices used for low-noise measurements in Sec.~\ref{subsec:setup:instrumentation-grounding}. The operation and performance of the dilution refrigerator are documented separately in Appendix~\ref{appendix:cryostat}.

    \newpage
    \subsection{Mechanically Controlled Break Junction}
    \label{subsec:setup:mcbj}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[32]{r}{0.35\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.3em}
            \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{bruchmechanik.pdf_tex}
            \vspace{-.3em}
            \caption{Rendering of the differential screw, central pushing element, counter supports, and sample holder used for the cryogenic MCBJ mechanics.}
            \label{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}
        \end{wrapfigure}

        The mechanically controllable break-junction (MCBJ) setup provides the in situ control coordinate of the experiment. Its task is to convert a macroscopic room-temperature motor motion into a reproducible, much smaller elongation of the metallic constriction at millikelvin temperature. The sample is mounted in a three-point bending geometry, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:bruchmechanik}. Two fixed counter supports hold the substrate, while a central pushing element bends it from the opposite side. In this implementation the counter supports remain fixed and the central element is displaced. The suspended metallic bridge on the elastic substrate then elongates by only a small fraction of the macroscopic bending motion. This mechanical reduction allows atomic-scale contacts to be formed, opened, and held over long measurement times.

        The cryogenic mechanics are based on the LD400-compatible design developed by Ansgar Fischer and Martin Prestel~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020} and built by the scientific workshop.\footnote{Scientific workshop, \texttt{07062016}.} At the sample stage, a differential screw converts the rotary motion of the drive train into linear motion of the central pushing element. The screw combines an M4 and an M5 thread, so one full revolution advances the bending mechanism only by the difference between the two thread pitches. The resulting macroscopic feed is \qty{0.1}{\milli\meter} per revolution.

        The motor position is treated operationally rather than as a universal measure of junction elongation. The motor encoder reports integer values up to about 1.8$\,\times\,$10\textsuperscript{9}, which are not useful for describing contact tuning. The laboratory control software therefore maps the motion to a relative coordinate from \qtyrange{0}{18}{}. This range corresponds to 30 revolutions of the differential screw and thus to \qty{3}{\milli\meter} of macroscopic screw feed. For an individual opening trace, the motor coordinate can be converted into an approximate relative electrode displacement by fitting the exponential conductance decay in the tunnel regime, as demonstrated in Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:pincode}. I treat this conversion as sample dependent because the effective mechanical reduction can vary with the realized bridge and mounting geometry, including variations introduced by the resist stack. The operational coordinate is therefore used for reproducible opening and closing, while a displacement scale is calibrated from the tunnel data whenever it is required.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Cryogenic Drive Train}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.35\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1.3em}
                \import{methods/setup/mcbj}{motorhalterung.pdf_tex}
                \caption{Rendering of the redesigned room-temperature mounting plates for the motor and vacuum feedthrough.}
                \label{fig:setup:motorhalterung}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            The cryogenic drive train must transmit controlled rotary motion from room temperature to the sample space without creating excessive heat load or mechanical stress. The established design meets the thermal requirement by dividing the train into several gearbox stages that are thermalized at the corresponding cryostat stages. Hollow stainless-steel tubes connect the gearboxes.\footnote{Faulhaber, \texttt{401.4659.04 ``Ritzel'' 2.0x4.35x4.7 Z12 Weld}, welded to stainless-steel tubes with \qty{4}{\milli\meter} outer diameter and \qty{0.1}{\milli\meter} wall thickness.} The gearboxes transmit the motion and interrupt the thermal path along the shaft.

            The existing drive train used the same 1:3.1 reduction at each of its four commercial spur gearheads.\footnote{Faulhaber, \texttt{22/2K}, \qty{0.1}{\newton\meter}.} Repeated failures during this work showed that this distribution loaded the upper cryogenic gearboxes too strongly when a lower stage became stiff or blocked. The resulting torque could damage the small gears inside the gearboxes or decouple from the following shaft. I redistributed the reduction by replacing the gearhead close to the motor with a 1:9.7 reduction and the first two cryogenic gearboxes with 1:5.4 reductions. The remaining 1:3.1 stages were left unchanged. This configuration reduces the torque applied by the first cryogenic gearbox. It requires more rotations at the \qty{40}{\kelvin} and \qty{4}{\kelvin} stages and therefore increases the heat dissipated there. This additional heat load is acceptable because these stages provide much greater cooling power than the millikelvin stages.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Room-Temperature Drive and Alignment}
        %=========================================================

            The room-temperature drive must hold the motor and rotary vacuum feedthrough on a common axis so that the cryogenic shaft is not loaded by lateral motion. The previous mount used a massive plastic disk with a central bore and a narrow radial slit. Tightening the clamp mainly deformed the plastic body instead of reducing the bore diameter, and the thread cut directly into the plastic was damaged by the required clamping force. The mount therefore did not hold the motor or feedthrough reproducibly. Coaxial alignment of the two components was not possible, which imposed substantial lateral load on the entire drive train. 
            
            I replaced it with the two-piece mount shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:motorhalterung}, designed by Oliver Bruttel and myself.\footnote{Scientific workshop, \texttt{13042026}.} Separate clamps hold the motor and vacuum feedthrough, while rods connect the two plates and maintain their alignment. A 3D-printed clamp holds the separately ordered motor and gearhead in place instead of glue, allowing either component to be replaced independently. Under excessive load, the printed clamp also provides a sacrificial mechanical weak point that can fail before the motor is damaged. The revised mount is stiffer, can be assembled reproducibly, and improves the maintainability of the drive.

            Friction and misalignment at the vacuum feedthrough and slip clutch produced a second source of excessive motor load. The rotary feedthrough\footnote{Pfeiffer Vacuum, \texttt{DD 016 A}, rotary feedthrough, \texttt{DN 16 ISO-KF}.} was therefore lubricated regularly with vacuum grease\footnote{Syensqo Specialty Polymers, Fomblin \texttt{YL VAC 25/6}.} to keep its friction low. In the original slip clutch, repeated compression deformed the clamping threads cut into the plastic body and displaced the clutch from the rotation axis. The resulting misalignment contributed to premature motor failure. I replaced the plastic threads with brass inserts. This improved the centering and kept the coupling stable under repeated clamping.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Assembly and Commissioning}
        %=========================================================

            Reliable operation also depends on assembly and preparation. The rods inside the cryostat were fixed while rotating slowly so that small angular or lateral offsets could relax before the drive train was locked in place. This reduces the risk of residual misalignment that can block motion after cooldown. Each gearbox was stripped of its original lubricant, cleaned overnight in an ultrasonic acetone bath, and rinsed with isopropanol. Immediately before cooldown, the gearboxes were heated with a hot-air gun to remove residual solvent and adsorbed moisture. This preparation prevents lubricant, solvent, or water residues from freezing and blocking the gears at cryogenic temperature.

            The drive train cannot be inspected directly at low temperature, so I used heating at the cryostat stages as an operational diagnostic. Mechanical motion dissipates heat at the stages through which it is transmitted. A temperature increase at successive stages therefore indicates that the corresponding parts of the drive train are moving. If this response stops at one stage or changes abruptly relative to earlier operation, the blockage or failure is likely near the following gearbox or coupling. In some tests, motion of the cold mechanism heated the sample stage to about \qty{1}{\kelvin}. The gas-handling system must be monitored during such tests, because additional heat at the still can increase the return pressure and may cause overpressure if the motion is too aggressive or repeated too quickly.

            The final configuration was reached through iterative commissioning. Each change required a full cryogenic test, and a failed test usually required another warm-up, repair or replacement, pump-down, and cooldown. After the gearbox distribution, motor mount, slip clutch, feedthrough lubrication, and assembly procedure had been revised, the mechanics operated reproducibly during cooldown and at base temperature. The samples could then be broken and tuned without further mechanical failures. The MCBJ position therefore provides a reproducible conductance-control coordinate, while the absolute atomic displacement remains inferred from the measured transport response.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Electrical Measurement Environment}
    \label{subsec:setup:electrical}
    %=========================================================

        The electrical setup must bias the junction and determine its nonlinear \textit{I--V} characteristic while the device resistance changes by orders of magnitude. It must also maintain a low electronic temperature and provide controlled microwave excitation. I first define how the sample voltage and current are obtained from the circuit. I then describe the cryogenic wiring, filtering, microwave coupling, thermometry, and room-temperature instrumentation that establish the measurement conditions.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Measurement Schematic}
        %=========================================================

            Figure~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema} shows the two bias and readout configurations used in this work. In both configurations, the transport circuit is operated in a quasi-dc mode. A programmed voltage $V_\mathrm{bias}$ is applied to the bias line, while the synchronized voltage drops $V_\mathrm{sample}$ and $V_\mathrm{ref}$ are recorded across the sample region and a known reference resistor. The sample current is inferred from $V_\mathrm{ref}$. The physical \textit{I--V} characteristic is therefore reconstructed from measured voltage drops rather than from the programmed source voltage.

            \begin{figure}[t]
                \centering
                \subfigure[
                Standard high-impedance wiring.
                ]{\fontsize{7}{9}\selectfont
                \import{methods/setup/electric}{measurement-schema-high.pdf_tex}
                }
                \hfill
                \subfigure[
                Modified low-impedance wiring.
                ]{\fontsize{7}{9}\selectfont
                \import{methods/setup/electric}{measurement-schema-low.pdf_tex}
                }
                \caption{Bias and readout configurations used to reconstruct sample voltage and current. The programmed quasi-dc bias \textit{V}\textsubscript{bias} is applied through the bias line, while \textit{V}\textsubscript{sample} and \textit{V}\textsubscript{ref} are recorded as synchronized voltage drops. Opposing triangles denote the sample, and gray shading (\legend{seegrau65}) marks components thermalized inside the cryostat. (a) The standard high-impedance configuration places two cold reference resistors symmetrically around the sample, keeping the current readout in the cold filtered environment. (b) For low sample impedances, two additional warm \qty{100}{\ohm} resistors provide a lower-resistance bias and current-readout path. The current is inferred from the voltage drop across one of these resistors.}
                \label{fig:setup:measurement-schema}
            \end{figure}

            The standard configuration in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema}(a) is used for tunnel junctions and few-channel contacts. Two cold reference resistors are placed symmetrically on the two sides of the sample. This keeps the sample potential close to the cryostat ground during a bias sweep and reduces common-mode offsets in the voltage readout. The voltage $V_\mathrm{ref}$ is measured across one of these resistors. Its measured base-temperature resistance is
            \begin{equation}
                R_\mathrm{ref} = \qty{101.473}{\kilo\ohm}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:cold-reference-resistance}
            \end{equation}
            The second cold reference resistor contributes to the total series resistance of the bias path, but it is not part of the measured reference-voltage drop. The sample current is obtained directly from
            \begin{equation}
                I_\mathrm{sample} = V_\mathrm{ref}\,/\,R_\mathrm{ref}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:sample-current}
            \end{equation}

            The programmed voltage $V_\mathrm{bias}$ is not equal to $V_\mathrm{sample}$ because the cold reference resistors, filters, leads, contacts, and device form a bias divider. In a linearized resistive approximation, the voltage and current would follow
            \begin{align}
                V_\mathrm{sample} &\simeq V_\mathrm{bias}\,R_\mathrm{sample}\,/\,R_\mathrm{total}\,, \\
                I_\mathrm{sample} &\simeq V_\mathrm{bias}\,/\,R_\mathrm{total}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:bias-divider}
            \end{align}
            These expressions illustrate the voltage division, but the analysis uses the simultaneously measured values of $V_\mathrm{sample}$ and $V_\mathrm{ref}$. This is essential for nonlinear superconducting transport because the differential resistance changes with bias.

            A useful measure of the instantaneous bias condition is
            \begin{equation}
                \eta = R_\mathrm{series}\,/\, R_\mathrm{sample}\,,
                \label{eq:setup:bias-eta}
            \end{equation}
            where $R_\mathrm{sample}$ is the instantaneous device resistance and $R_\mathrm{series}$ is the remaining resistance in the bias path,
            \begin{equation}
                R_\mathrm{series}
                % = R_\mathrm{total}-R_\mathrm{sample}
                \simeq 2\,R_\mathrm{ref} + R_\mathrm{filters} + R_\mathrm{leads} + \ldots\,.
                \label{eq:setup:series-resistance}
            \end{equation}
            In the limit $\eta \ll 1$, most of the applied voltage drops across the sample and the circuit approaches voltage bias. In the opposite limit, $\eta \gg 1$, the series resistance dominates and the circuit approaches current bias. The MCBJ moves between these limits during operation. A closed junction can have a resistance of order \qty{100}{\ohm}, a single atom contact has a resistance of order \qty{12.9}{\kilo\ohm}, and the tunnel regime is characterized in the \qtyrange{100}{500}{\kilo\ohm} range. Since the standard filtered circuit contains about \qty{200}{\kilo\ohm} from the two cold reference resistors, its effective bias condition changes with the junction state.

            For low sample resistances, the standard configuration directs most of the programmed bias across the cold reference resistors. The accessible sample-voltage range can then become too small to leave the zero-voltage supercurrent branch or reach spectroscopic features such as the superconducting gap. This regime requires a lower series resistance rather than a different reconstruction principle.

            I therefore used the modified configuration in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:measurement-schema}(b) for low-impedance measurements. Two additional warm resistors with nominal resistances of \qty{100}{\ohm} provide the lower-resistance bias path. The voltage drop across one of these resistors provides the current readout. The Johnson--Nyquist voltage-noise density of a resistor is~\cite{johnson_thermal_1928,nyquist_thermal_1928}
            \begin{align}
                S_V^{1/2} &= \sqrt{4k_\mathrm{B}TR}\,.
                \label{eq:setup:johnson-nyquist-reference}
            \end{align}

            Although the additional resistor is warmer, its resistance is about three orders of magnitude smaller than that of the standard reference resistor. Its voltage-noise density therefore remains of the same order. The smaller resistance also produces a correspondingly smaller reference voltage for a given current. For a fixed readout-voltage range, this increases the measurable current range by approximately three orders of magnitude. The tradeoff is reduced current sensitivity. I used this configuration when the higher sample-voltage and current ranges were required.

            Both configurations therefore produce the same physical observables, the measured sample voltage and the current inferred from a reference-voltage drop. The choice between them sets the available sample-voltage range, the source impedance, and the associated noise environment.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Cryogenic Measurement Lines}
        %=========================================================

            \begin{figure}[p]
                \centering
                {\fontsize{7}{9}\selectfont
                \import{methods/setup/electric}{cabling.pdf_tex}
                }
                \caption{Custom cryostat cabling at the sample stage. All components shown are mounted at the base-temperature stage, except for the microwave attenuators at the \qty{4}{\kelvin} stage. The island is coupled to the MCBJ (left), the tunnel barrier (bottom), the dc gate (right), and the ac gate (top). Thin copper wires connect the sample pads to pin headers, which are connected either to low-frequency coaxial twisted-pair cables or to high-frequency coaxial cables. The cold reference resistors \textit{R}\textsubscript{ref} are mounted in an additionally shielded box. Copper powder (CP) filters and the commercial MFT25 filter suppress high-frequency leakage and thermalize the lines.}
                \label{fig:setup:cabling}
            \end{figure}

            The cryostat wiring has to satisfy two requirements at the same time. It must define a low-noise electrical environment for high-impedance tunnel devices and few-channel atomic contacts, and it must not carry excessive heat from room temperature to the millikelvin stage. The first requirement motivates symmetric wiring, cold reference resistors, shielding, and filtering of external high-frequency noise. The second requirement motivates low-thermal-conductance cables and careful thermal anchoring. The wiring concept follows earlier MCBJ and single-electron-transistor setups in the group~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,prestel_magnetic_2020}, but several parts were changed for this experiment.

            Figure~\ref{fig:setup:cabling} shows the custom wiring used near the sample. The cryostat contains separate cable systems for low-frequency signals and microwave excitation. The low-frequency system serves the readout of sample voltage and current, for the application of bias and dc gate voltage, and as well for themperature readout and applied heater power. The ac gate and antenna lines are high-frequency coaxial cables, described below. The dc gate uses two physical lines that are shorted before the thin copper wires connect to the sample. This gives diagnostic and backup access while the sample sees one gate terminal. From room temperature to the base-temperature region, the low-frequency lines use the commercial Bluefors cryostat cabling, in contrast to earlier implementations~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020,raif_electronic_2024}. At the base-temperature stage, they are continued with double-twisted coaxial cable\footnote{GVL Cryoengineering, \texttt{GVLZ 169}, connected with two-pole LEMO, \texttt{FGG.00.302.CLAD35} plugs and \texttt{EGG.00.302.CLL} sockets. Copper tubes with \qty{2}{\milli\meter} outer diameter and \qty{0.45}{\milli\meter} wall thickness are used as bend protection.} with brass inner and outer conductors.

            The base-temperature cables are soldered to pin headers. From there, \qty{90}{\micro\meter} insulated copper wires connect to the sample pads through conductive silver paste. This short copper section gives a low contact resistance and provides a local thermal anchor close to the device. The voltage and current leads define a four-terminal measurement with respect to the cryostat wiring. They do not form an ideal Kelvin measurement of the atomic contact alone, because the local copper wires, silver-paste contacts, and sample pads remain inside the measured sample region. For the high-impedance devices considered here, this residual series resistance is negligible compared with the device resistance.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Low-Frequency Filtering}
        %=========================================================

            The dc wiring carries the slowly varying bias and gate voltages as well as the voltage signals used to determine sample current and voltage. Its task is not only to pass low-frequency signals. It must also suppress high-frequency radiation that would heat the junction or create photon-assisted features unrelated to the intended microwave drive. The filter stack was therefore designed as both an electrical low-noise environment and a thermalization stage.

            The low-frequency lines are filtered and thermalized in several stages. Four group-built copper-powder filters are mounted at the base-temperature stage, one each for the voltage, bias, current, and dc-gate lines. They follow the filter concept described in Ref.~\cite{thalmann_comparison_2017}. In this filter type, a copper signal wire is embedded in compacted copper powder inside a gold-plated copper housing. The filter has low resistance at dc and low frequency, but becomes lossy at high frequency. The relevant mechanism is the skin effect. High-frequency currents are confined closer to the surface of the signal wire, while the surrounding copper powder provides a dissipative electromagnetic environment. The filter therefore attenuates signals over a broad frequency range and along its full length, rather than above one well-defined RC cutoff frequency.

            The copper-powder filters act as a broadband protection stage against high-frequency leakage. At the same time, the large metal--metal contact area between wire, powder, and housing provides an efficient thermal anchor for the electronic degrees of freedom in the line. Their role in the present stack is to add high-frequency loss without adding a large dc resistance to the measurement circuit.

            A commercial microwave filter--thermalizer\footnote{Basel Precision Instruments, \texttt{MFT25-100Ohm SN0219}. The unit is connected through a Glenair, \texttt{25-way MWDM Micro-D} panel-mount receptacle.} (MFT25) is used in series with the copper-powder filters. According to the manufacturer, the MFT25 embeds the signal conductors in a highly conductive silver matrix that acts as a heat exchanger, and the compact package contains 25 filter lines in a gold-plated non-magnetic copper box. The manufacturer specifies strong microwave attenuation above \qty{130}{\mega\hertz} together with low capacitance and good thermal contact to the millikelvin stage~\cite{basel_precision_mft_nodate}. In the present setup, the MFT25 provides the compact commercial filter--thermalizer stage, while the copper-powder filters add additional broadband high-frequency loss.

            The MFT25 also improved the practical reliability of the dc wiring. Earlier versions of the setup used fragile steel-capillary wiring, similar to the configuration described in Ref.~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. These lines could break during assembly or thermal cycling, and inherited copper-powder filters failed intermittently. The MFT25 replaced most of this capillary-filter chain on the transport and dc-gate lines while adding a well-thermalized commercial filtering stage. The result was a mechanically more robust wiring system that was better suited to maintaining a low electronic temperature at the sample.

            The reference resistors are mounted in the shielded base-temperature region close to the sample. In an earlier configuration they were located at the still stage. Moving them to the base-temperature stage reduces their Johnson--Nyquist voltage noise by lowering the resistor temperature, while keeping the current readout in the same cold filtered environment as the sample~\cite{johnson_thermal_1928,nyquist_thermal_1928}. The design was also made mechanically more robust. An earlier through-hole metal-oxide resistor developed intermittent contacts, while the revised design uses an SMD resistor\footnote{Scientific workshop, \texttt{2481023}. Vishay/Draloric, \texttt{TNPU1206100KBZEN00}.} on a small chip. This provides better thermal contact and a less fragile electrical connection. The reference-resistor region is additionally shielded, as indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:cabling}, to reduce capacitive pickup from neighboring lines. Before cooldown, the wiring was checked by measuring the resistance matrix between the relevant ports and by verifying the ground connections of the lines.

            The filtering stack was therefore optimized for low electronic temperature and robust wiring. The MFT25 and the revised reference-resistor design reduced electronic heating and measurement noise. This was essential for resolving sharp superconducting features in the \textit{dI--dV} spectra.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Microwave Cabling}
        %=========================================================

            In addition to the dc bias and gate lines, the setup contains two microwave paths for excitation. I refer to them as the ac-gate line and the antenna line. The ac-gate line terminates on the on-chip ac gate, which is capacitively coupled to the sample. The antenna line is a stripped coaxial cable placed close to the sample. These names describe the hardware geometry, not a calibrated lumped circuit. At GHz frequencies, both paths can launch local electromagnetic fields that couple to the device region.

            Both microwave paths are routed as coaxial lines. The commercial microwave cabling inside the cryostat uses superconducting cables to keep the transmission high. At each temperature stage, the microwave line passes through a connector that also provides a thermalization point. The cryostat was initially delivered with attenuators installed at these positions. I replaced them by pass-through connectors\footnote{Amphenol SV Microwave, \texttt{SF1597-6003}.} except at the \qty{4}{\kelvin} flange. The remaining \qty{-10}{\deci\bel} attenuators at \qty{4}{\kelvin} provide a defined thermalization point while placing the dissipated power at a stage with comparatively large cooling power. For future measurements, moving attenuation closer to the mixing-chamber stage would likely be preferable, because the electronic temperature at the device is more important than maximizing cooling power at base temperature.

            The base-temperature part of the microwave cabling\footnote{Elspec, \texttt{DA50047-821}, \qty{50}{\ohm}, CuAg/PTFE/Cu, \qty{1.19}{\milli\meter}. Rosenberger, \texttt{02S141-270L5}.} was installed together with Patrick Raif~\cite{raif_electronic_2024}. Frequencies up to about \qty{20}{\giga\hertz} were transmitted reliably in the installed configuration, as inferred from microwave-induced changes in the \textit{dI--dV} spectra. The network analyzer specifies its output as a power $P_\mathrm{out,dBm}$. For the amplitude sweeps discussed later, I convert this setting to the nominal source amplitude $A_\mathrm{out}$ using the matched-load convention
            \begin{align}
                P_\mathrm{out, W}
                = 10^{P_\mathrm{out, dBm}/10-3},
                &&
                A_\mathrm{out}
                = \sqrt{2 Z_0 P_\mathrm{out}},
                &&
                Z_0=\qty{50}{\ohm}.
                \label{eq:methods:setup:microwave-source-amplitude}
            \end{align}
            
            Here $A_\mathrm{out}$ is the nominal peak voltage amplitude\footnote{The corresponding root-mean-square voltage is $V_\mathrm{out,rms}=A_\mathrm{out}/\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{Z_0P_\mathrm{out}}$.}. The maximum applied setting of \qty{10}{\dBm} therefore corresponds to \qty{10}{\milli\watt} and \qty{1}{\volt}. Equation~\eqref{eq:methods:setup:microwave-source-amplitude} assumes a matched \qty{50}{\ohm} load and defines only a source-side coordinate. It does not account for attenuation, reflections, standing waves, or antenna coupling and therefore does not give the microwave amplitude at the junction~\cite{pozar_microwave_2011}. The two paths, connecting ac gate and antenna, have different relative transmission as a function of frequency, which provides access to different effective power windows for different microwave tones.

            Together with Patrick Raif, we showed that both paths act in practice like antennas for the device~\cite{raif_electronic_2024}. Part of the microwave power is launched already near the pin headers. From the point of view of the sample, the two paths therefore behave more like two different antennas than like two sharply separated drive mechanisms. The coupling is not impedance matched, and the break-junction architecture was not designed as a calibrated microwave transmission structure. Reflections, standing waves, and antenna coupling consequently produce an unknown transfer function between the source setting and the local voltage at the junction. This transfer function depends on frequency, coupling path, and device configuration and cannot be determined once as a fixed setup calibration. A matched calibration would require a substantially different architecture, closer to the dedicated microwave design used by Bretheau~\cite{bretheau_localized_2013}.

            I therefore use the applied source power only as a reproducible control coordinate. Whenever the local amplitude is required quantitatively, I determine an effective microwave voltage from an internal reference in the transport data, in particular from photon assisted tunneling as described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}. This calibration is performed separately for the corresponding device, frequency, and coupling path in the results sections. It incorporates the complete unknown microwave coupling chain into a data-derived effective amplitude rather than assuming a universal conversion from source power to junction voltage.

        %=========================================================
        \subsubsection*{Thermometry and Heating}
        %=========================================================

            The sample space is equipped with dedicated thermometer and heater wiring to monitor and control the local sample-holder temperature. These lines are filtered by copper-powder filters, but they are routed through a separate cable tree and a separate break-out box. This keeps heater and thermometer signals away from the sensitive transport circuit. The separation is important because thermometer readout and heater operation can otherwise interfere with low-current measurements. The thermometer arrangement follows the earlier MCBJ setup used by Martin Prestel~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. In the present setup, a ruthenium-oxide resistor thermometer\footnote{Lake Shore Cryotronics Inc., \texttt{RX102B-CB}, serial number \texttt{U06030}, curve name \texttt{MCBJ Sample ORI}.} was mounted on the sample holder next to the sample.

            The heater is a local resistive heater made from thin insulated manganin wire with a resistance of about \qty{100}{\ohm}. It is wrapped around one of the posts that connects the sample holder to the mechanical break-junction assembly. This geometry heats the sample holder locally while remaining mechanically simple and robust. I used the heater for controlled temperature-dependent measurements.

            The resistance thermometer measures its own temperature at its mounting position on the sample holder. Because it is thermally anchored to the holder, I use its calibrated reading as an estimate of the local bath temperature. It does not directly determine the effective electronic temperature relevant for transport through the junction, which is a model-dependent parameter inferred from the measured transport spectrum. The junction electrons can be warmer than the thermometer because of imperfect line thermalization, residual high-frequency radiation, dissipated bias power, or microwave-induced heating. This distinction is particularly important under microwave irradiation. A change in the thermometer reading can indicate heating of the sample holder, but it can also include a direct microwave-induced readout artifact.

            The thermometer and heater lines are filtered and thermalized for the same reason as the transport lines. They connect room-temperature electronics to the millikelvin stage and can therefore act as thermal and electrical noise channels. Their voltage-noise requirements are less restrictive than those of the transport lines, but they still have to be well anchored to avoid parasitic heating. The heater line is connected separately to the Bluefors cabling\footnote{LEMO, \texttt{FGG.0S.302.ZLAT}.}.

            I calibrated the heating protocol empirically by recording the steady sample-holder temperature $T_\mathrm{bath}$ for a series of applied heater powers $P_\mathrm{heater}$. Here, $T_\mathrm{bath}$ denotes the temperature obtained from the factory-calibrated resistance curve of the ruthenium-oxide thermometer. It is therefore the local thermometer reading used by the control software and by the PID loop. The heater power denotes the electrical power dissipated in the manganin heater. Figure~\ref{fig:methods:setup:thermometry:heater-calibration} shows the resulting relation for the temperature sweep used in this thesis.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{1.5in}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                % \vspace{-.5em}
                \thesisplot{tunnelbarrier/highres/temperature}{heater}
                % \vspace{-.5em}
                \caption{
                        Applied heater power $P_\mathrm{heater}$ as a function of the sample-holder temperature $T_\mathrm{bath}$. The line shows the empirical power law used to choose the heater setpoints.
                    }
                \label{fig:methods:setup:thermometry:heater-calibration}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            Because the thermal conductance between the sample holder and the mixing chamber increases with temperature, equally spaced power setpoints do not produce equally spaced temperatures. I therefore selected the heater setpoints from the empirical relation
            \begin{equation}
                P_\mathrm{heater} \approx \qty{0.11}{\micro\watt\per\kelvin\tothe{5/2}} \cdot T_\mathrm{sample}^{2.5}\,.
                \label{eq:methods:setup:heater-power-scaling}
            \end{equation}
            This choice produced an approximately uniform temperature axis for repeated \textit{I--V} sweeps. Equation~\eqref{eq:methods:setup:heater-power-scaling} is a calibration of the applied setpoints for this assembly, not a microscopic model of its thermal conductance. It can change after remounting the sample holder or modifying the thermal links and must then be measured again.

            The heater, thermometer, sample holder, and junction are not perfectly thermally equivalent. Finite thermal resistance along the pushing rod, stamp, and counter rods can produce temperature gradients. From the setup and control perspective, I denote the calibrated thermometer reading by $T_\mathrm{sample}$. In the later transport analysis, the same recorded quantity is denoted by $T_\mathrm{bath}$ to distinguish it from $T_\mathrm{fit}$. Thus, $T_\mathrm{sample}$ and $T_\mathrm{bath}$ refer to the same thermometer channel, while $T_\mathrm{fit}$ is inferred from the junction transport.

            The calibrated thermometer reading estimates the local bath temperature, while controlled heating and temperature-dependent \textit{I--V} curves provide consistency checks on the electronic response. Together, the cold reference resistors, thermalized wiring, and microwave lines define the current readout, limit noise and heating, and provide reproducible but not absolutely calibrated irradiation.

    \newpage
    \subsection{Instrumentation and Grounding}
    \label{subsec:setup:instrumentation-grounding}
    %=========================================================
        

        Figure~\ref{fig:setup:grounding} summarizes the room-temperature instruments and their grounding connections. This part of the setup had two tasks. It had to provide synchronized voltage readout and bias control, and it had to avoid additional ground paths that bypassed the filtered cryostat wiring. The cryostat was treated as the local ground reference because its large metallic body provides a stable electrical potential. The working rule was to connect each instrument to this reference only through its intended signal path or through a deliberately isolated control link. Uncontrolled parallel connections between building ground, computer interfaces, instrument shields, and the cryostat body were avoided.

        Instrument control was part of the grounding task because control cables can introduce additional shield and ground paths. The control computer was connected to the laboratory network, while GPIB and RS-232 instruments were interfaced through USB adapters\footnote{National Instruments, \texttt{GPIB-USB-HS}, used for GPIB access.}. USB connections can introduce additional shield and ground paths, so they were isolated either by USB-over-LAN links\footnote{Icron, \texttt{USB 2.0 Ranger 2304}.} or by galvanic USB isolators\footnote{CESYS, \texttt{C028149}.}. The cryostat control unit\footnote{Bluefors, \texttt{LD400}, serial number \texttt{BF2015-05}, connected by USB.} was included in this isolated control chain. LAN-connected instruments were used where available, because they allowed remote control without direct USB connections to the measurement computer. The motor controller and LAN switch\footnote{D-Link, \texttt{DGS-108}.} were powered without a protective-earth connection and therefore floated relative to building ground, as indicated in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:grounding}.

        The voltage signals were first amplified by voltage preamplifiers\footnote{Femto, \texttt{DLPVA}, preamplifier. Scientific workshop, \texttt{WW-98000627}, power supply. Femto, \texttt{Luci10}, USB control interface.}. These amplifiers provide selectable gains and a low-pass bandwidth of about \qty{100}{\kilo\hertz}. Their ground-referenced output voltages are digitized by the analog inputs of the measurement card\footnote{Jäger Computergesteuerte Messtechnik GmbH, \texttt{ADwin Gold II}, connected by LAN.}. Although the card can sample at rates up to \qty{100}{\kilo\hertz}, the quasi-dc sweeps used here did not require this bandwidth. The data stream was therefore reduced on the card to \qty{137}{\hertz}.

        The acquisition strategy was to record synchronized voltage traces as directly as practical and to postpone non-essential filtering, smoothing, and gridding to the evaluation stage. This keeps irreversible processing out of the measurement chain and allows processing errors to be corrected from the stored raw data. Once digitized by the measurement card, the unprocessed voltage traces were written to the measurement file and evaluated offline. No intermediate instrument performed additional conversion or filtering before storage.

        \begin{figure}[t!]
            \centering
            {\fontsize{6}{9}\selectfont
            \import{methods/setup/electric}{grounding-schema.pdf_tex}
            }
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \caption{Grounding and instrument schematic. Building ground and cryostat ground are separated except for a \qty{13}{\mega\ohm} connection~(G) through the temperature controller. The motor controller (B) and LAN switch (W) lack a protective-earth connection and therefore float. Operating the magnet power supply (Y) floating minimized noise in the \textit{I--V} traces.}
            \label{fig:setup:grounding}
        \end{figure}

        The bias voltage was generated by an analog output of the same measurement card. Before entering the cryostat wiring, it passed through a warm symmetric voltage divider and a low-pass filter\footnote{Thorlabs Inc., \texttt{EF120}.}. The divider reduced the full output range of the card to the voltage window relevant for the sample and improved the effective bias resolution. Its resistances were chosen low enough that their thermal noise and their contribution to the total source impedance remained limited.

        The divider was kept at room temperature, in contrast to the cold shunt used in Ref.~\cite{prestel_magnetic_2020}. This preserved access to filter, wiring, and grounding diagnostics during cooldown and low-temperature operation, following the practical motivation of Ref.~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018}. A shunt mounted inside the cryostat would have fixed part of the bias environment locally, but it would also have made several grounding and continuity checks inaccessible during a cooldown.

        Several combinations of available voltage sources, amplifiers, and multimeters were tested before settling on the configuration shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:setup:grounding}. The practical optimization criterion was the low-frequency noise in the sample voltage and reference voltage channels under the same filtering and grounding conditions used for transport measurements. The relevant observable was not only electrical continuity, but the stability and cleanliness of the measured \textit{I--V} traces.

        Using one measurement card for both voltage channels ensured that the sample voltage and reference voltage readings were acquired as synchronized pairs. During a typical measurement, the bias was applied symmetrically around ground so that the common mode voltage of the sample remained close to zero. The voltage was ramped slowly down and up, with full periods of \qty{5}{\minute} or longer.

        The remaining instruments provided gate control, microwave excitation, temperature control, mechanical actuation, magnetic field control, and diagnostics. The dc gate voltage was applied with respect to ground by a high-precision voltage source\footnote{Yokogawa, \texttt{GS200}, connected by GPIB.}. All dc lines were routed through a break-out box\footnote{Scientific workshop, \texttt{WW-2280090}, ``Major Tom''.} into the commercial cryostat cabling. The microwave excitation paths were driven by the two ports of a vector network analyzer\footnote{Rohde \& Schwarz, \texttt{ZNB40}, connected by LAN.}, which were connected to the ac gate and antenna lines.

        Temperature monitoring and local heating were handled by an ac resistance bridge\footnote{Lake Shore Cryotronics Inc., \texttt{372AC} and \texttt{3726}, connected by USB.}. One channel continuously read the sample thermometer, while the second channel and scanner read the auxiliary thermometers through the separate thermometry cable tree. The bridge also controlled the local heater. I replaced the previous bridge with this two-channel unit so that the sample thermometer remained available continuously during auxiliary readout. This required an additional break-out box\footnote{Scientific workshop, \texttt{WW-2480259}, thermometry break-out box.} to separate the dedicated sample thermometer line from the auxiliary thermometry lines. The continuous reading enabled proportional--integral--derivative (PID) control of the local bath temperature at a chosen setpoint.

        Mechanical actuation of the break junction was provided by an encoded motor and driver\footnote{Faulhaber, Motor \texttt{2232R024S}, Encoder \texttt{R IE2-1024 4613}, Driver \texttt{MCDC3002S RS}, Gear Head \texttt{22/2S}, connected by RS-232.}. A one-axis \qty{7}{\tesla} magnet system, comprising a controller and a separate high-current power supply, was used when superconductivity had to be suppressed.\footnote{American Magnetics Inc., \texttt{AMI430}, connected by LAN.} Additional diagnostic access was provided by a precision source and several digital multimeters\footnote{Keysight, \texttt{B2962A}, connected by LAN. Keysight, \texttt{34461A}, connected by LAN.}. These diagnostic instruments were connected only when their information was needed, because each additional instrument could introduce another pickup or ground path.

        For the lowest-noise \textit{I--V} traces, devices that were not required during the actual sweep were disconnected from the setup. In particular, both the magnet controller and its high-current power supply were usually disconnected when not in use. The power supply can couple low-frequency pickup or ground offsets into the cryostat environment even when the magnetic field is constant. The communication cables of the preamplifiers were removed whenever remote gain control was not needed. This reduced the number of possible ground loop and pickup paths connected to the sensitive voltage readout circuit.

        The final grounding configuration was therefore developed empirically. It preserved a well-defined cryostat reference while minimizing pickup in the measured voltage channels. It should be understood as the working grounding solution for this specific cryostat, instrument rack, and wiring configuration. It is not a universal grounding model, but it documents which wiring and isolation choices produced stable \textit{I--V} traces in this experiment.

        Together, acquisition synchronization, bias range and resolution, and low-frequency pickup determine the noise floor of the reconstructed \textit{I--V} traces.
