\chapter{Practical Notes on Sample Fabrication}
\label{appendix:sample-fabrication}
%=========================================================

    This appendix collects practical notes from the laboratory that complement the conceptual description in Sec.~\ref{section:sample-preparation}. It is not intended as a fully standardized protocol. Instead, it documents the small but critical steps, common pitfalls, and empirically useful practices that improve the reliability of sample preparation.


    %=========================================================
    \subsection*{Wafer Preparation}
    %=========================================================

        I started with a bronze wafer with a thickness of \qty{300}{\micro\meter}, which was covered by a protective foil. To remove this foil without damaging the wafer, I cooled the wafer in liquid nitrogen and gently peeled off the brittle fragments. This method proved effective and largely nondestructive. I then polished the wafer using a polishing head made of sewn cotton cloth attached to a drill and aluminum oxide from a white polishing compound block as the abrasive. I soaked the polishing head with ethanol or IPA and fixed the wafer to a smooth milled steel block using double-sided adhesive tape\footnote{Tesa, Universal.}, taking care that no tape protruded beyond the wafer edge. During polishing, I moved the polishing head from top to bottom so that debris generated in the process was less likely to settle back onto the wafer surface. I rotated the steel block periodically because the lower part of the wafer was generally easier to polish uniformly. I continued until the entire surface had a homogeneous mirror-like finish. To detach the wafer from the steel block, I cooled it again in liquid nitrogen. I then rinsed the wafer first with acetone and subsequently with IPA, ensuring that the acetone was removed completely before switching solvents. Finally, I dried the wafer with nitrogen gas while the IPA was still wet to avoid residue formation. \cite{oberg_machinerys_2000}
        
        An alternative polishing method uses sandpaper with progressively finer grit on a glass slide, as described by Patrick Raif \cite{raif_fabrication_2023}. It is better suited to reducing long-range surface variations, but it is also considerably more time-consuming and was therefore not used routinely.

        Next, I prepared the spin coater, the polyamic acid (PA), and the vacuum oven. The PA was preportioned into small crimp seal vials to minimize moisture exposure and reduce the time required for warming. I removed one vial from the freezer and allowed it to reach room temperature while preheating the convection oven to \qty{135}{\degreeCelsius}. I lined the inside of the spin coater with aluminum foil and verified that the vacuum oven was operational and open.

        To remove adsorbed water, I placed the wafer on a hot plate at \qty{100}{\degreeCelsius} for at least one minute. For reliable adhesion to the \qty{1}{\inch} chuck, I applied a layer of parafilm to the chuck and bent it securely around the chuck edge. I then made five small holes in the parafilm using a syringe, one in the center and one near the edge in each cardinal direction. I allowed the wafer to cool to room temperature before centering it on the chuck and only then switched on the vacuum pump. Before dispensing the PA, I ran the spin coating program once to confirm that the wafer was held securely. This check was particularly important for larger wafers, for which insufficient adhesion was not always immediately apparent.
        
        I blew the wafer dry with nitrogen to remove residual dust and poured the PA onto the wafer until roughly \qty{90}{\percent} of the surface was covered, taking care to avoid trapped air bubbles. If bubbles remained, I moved or burst them carefully with a syringe without scratching the wafer. The spin coating program consisted of an initial spread step at \qty{300}{\rpm} for \qty{30}{\second}, followed by a final spin step at \qty{5000}{\rpm} for \qty{90}{\second}. Immediately afterward, I transferred the wafer to the convection oven for a \qty{5}{\minute} soft bake at \qty{135}{\degreeCelsius}. I then performed the hard bake in the vacuum oven using a programmed cycle that ramped to \qty{400}{\degreeCelsius}, held this temperature for \qty{30}{\minute}, and cooled back to room temperature over several hours. Detailed instructions and the corresponding temperature profile are given in Ref.~\cite{raif_fabrication_2023}. In practice, starting the bake immediately after spin coating improved reproducibility.
        
        I applied the EL11 and A4 resist layers using the same general workflow, but with different spin and bake parameters. Aluminum foil inside the spin coater was unnecessary because these resists could be removed easily with acetone. I checked the age of the resist before use because prolonged storage can noticeably degrade its performance. I applied EL11 first and performed the corresponding soft bake. After applying the A4 layer, the subsequent A4 bake also hard-baked the underlying EL11 layer. The relevant process parameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:methods:sample_parameter}.

        I then cut the wafer into pieces with dimensions of 3$\,\times\,$\qty{8}{\milli\meter} using a custom sheet shear equipped with \qty{3}{\milli\meter} and \qty{18}{\milli\meter} spacers. Before cutting, I cleaned the cutting edges thoroughly with IPA to reduce contamination and maintain clean cuts. Samples taken from the outer wafer region were more likely to show thickness variations in the PI and resist layers. I therefore preferred pieces from the central region of the wafer for reproducible fabrication. Edge related thickness variations can affect the dose required during electron beam lithography. After cutting, I stored the samples in a chip tray\footnote{Entegris, custom configuration.} for safe handling.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection*{Electron Beam Lithography}
    %=========================================================

        I performed electron beam lithography (EBL) using the Crossbeam system. I began by measuring the beam current with the jig containing the Faraday cup. A position list in the Zeiss software helped me locate the Faraday cup quickly. I kept the working distance as close as possible to \qty{5.00}{\milli\meter}. Because the beam current required some time to stabilize, I either waited before measuring or returned later and repeated the check.

        Accurate horizontal alignment of the sample was essential for exposure. I used the alignment tools in the focused ion beam (FIB) toolbox to simplify this step. I zoomed out to the maximum field of view and used the upper-right corner together with the leftmost sample edge for the initial alignment. This was usually sufficient to correct the dominant angular misalignment. Additional refinement within Elphy Plus was possible, but in practice it rarely improved the final angular alignment once the sample was mounted in the cryostat.

        Next, I focused the beam in the upper-right corner of the sample. I used the wobble function to center the beam in the aperture and minimized astigmatism with the \textit{x}- and \textit{y}-stigmators. Once the beam was well focused, I checked the current again at the Faraday cup. For the \qty{30}{\micro\meter} aperture in high-current mode, the beam current was about \qty{0.5}{\nano\ampere}. I used this setting for the small high-resolution structures. For the \qty{120}{\micro\meter} aperture, which I used for the \qty{1000}{\micro\meter} writing fields, the exact current was less critical because the larger leads tolerated moderate underexposure. In practice, slight underexposure in these larger fields reduced the writing time without compromising the result. The beam current for the \qty{120}{\micro\meter} aperture in high-current mode was typically around \qty{5}{\nano\ampere}. I performed a final focusing step at a central position along the upper sample edge, using a particle on the resist as a focusing aid. At this stage, I confirmed once more that the working distance was \qty{5.00(1)}{\milli\meter} and that the beam shift was set to zero.

        Before starting the exposure, I noted the current time. Because the design layout included a timestamp, this provided a convenient way to match laboratory notes to the physical sample. If the measured beam current differed from earlier sessions, I recalculated the settling time for the \qty{100}{\micro\meter} field accordingly. The position list executed in Elphy Plus then located the writing fields automatically, applied the corresponding settings, and wrote the design. I enforced stitching between adjacent writing fields by overlapping their nominal positions by \qty{100}{\micro\meter}.

        In practice, it is advantageous to keep the written design as simple as possible and avoid an unnecessarily large number of polygons. This reduces writing overhead and makes the exposure procedure more robust. I also avoided contamination dots as focusing markers. Instead, the combination of high-current mode and the \qty{30}{\micro\meter} aperture proved sufficient for reliable focusing because it provides a comparatively large depth of focus. The drawback is that very low exposure doses are no longer readily accessible, but this was not a practical limitation for the structures used here.
        
        I repeated the full alignment, focusing, and writing sequence for the remaining samples until I had completed a batch of roughly four to five samples.

        If several weeks had passed since the previous EBL session, I verified the alignment between the \qty{100}{\micro\meter} and \qty{1000}{\micro\meter} writing fields before exposing the first sample. I exposed one \qty{100}{\micro\meter} field together with a neighboring \qty{1000}{\micro\meter} field in the upper-right corner of a sample. The exposed resist appeared visibly different from the unexposed surroundings and could therefore be used to judge the relative alignment. I avoided prolonged observation because it could unintentionally expose the resist and reduce its contrast. If the fields were misaligned, I adjusted the settings accordingly. For example, if the \qty{100}{\micro\meter} field was shifted to the left, I corrected \textit{U} by $-\Delta X$. If it was shifted upward, I corrected \textit{V} by $-\Delta Y$. I repeated the test until the alignment was satisfactory.

        After exposure, I developed the samples. I prepared a crimp seal vial containing a mixture of one part MIBK and three parts IPA and one additional vial of pure IPA for each sample. I immersed the sample in the MIBK/IPA developer for \qty{25}{\second} while gently swirling the vial to maintain uniform development. I then transferred the sample immediately into pure IPA and continued swirling. I reused the developer for all samples in the same batch but used fresh IPA for each sample. I left the sample in IPA for \qty{60}{\second}, removed it, and carefully dried it with a stream of nitrogen. Throughout the process, I held the sample securely with tweezers to avoid dropping it while waiting or transferring it between vials. Finally, I inspected the developed structures under a light microscope to verify that the pattern was complete and free of obvious defects.

    \subsection*{Shadow Evaporation}
    %=========================================================

        To begin shadow evaporation, I placed the sample holder on a hot plate at \qty{100}{\degreeCelsius} for a few minutes to remove adsorbed water. This step noticeably improved the vacuum and adhesion. I carefully checked the sample orientation to ensure that the shadow evaporation proceeded in the intended direction. I then loaded the sample into the load lock and pumped it overnight, or longer if possible, before starting the deposition sequence.

        Once the vacuum was satisfactory, I transferred the sample holder into the main chamber for the first evaporation. Using electron beam evaporation, I deposited \qty{60}{\nano\meter} of aluminum at an angle of \qty{4}{\degree}. I heated the aluminum gradually over approximately \qtyrange{5}{10}{\minute} to avoid stressing the crucible and promote stable evaporation. I used one to three aluminum pellets per evaporation to ensure that the crucible contained sufficient material.

        After the first evaporation, I transferred the sample back into the load lock and closed the pumping line. I introduced pure oxygen until the pressure reached \qty{3.0(2)}{\milli\barunit} and oxidized the sample for \qty{3}{\minute}. To terminate the oxidation, I reopened the pumping line. I thoroughly evacuated the oxygen feed line to recover a vacuum on the order of \qty{1e-6}{\milli\barunit} afterward.

        Once the load lock vacuum had recovered, I transferred the sample back into the main chamber for the second evaporation. I deposited \qty{100}{\nano\meter} of aluminum at an angle of \qty{34}{\degree} to complete the shadow evaporation sequence.

        A smooth aluminum surface is desirable for forming a well-defined AlO\textsubscript{x} layer. In practice, however, the deposited aluminum often showed a large number of dimples, about \qty{100}{nm} in diameter. Several measures were tested in an attempt to reduce them, but no decisive remedy was identified. There was some indication that higher evaporation rates, particularly values above \qty{4}{\angstromunit\per\second}, may reduce the number of dimples, although the effect was not statistically convincing. Contrary to earlier expectations, a higher pressure or a shorter time between the last vacuum break and evaporation occasionally appeared to improve the surface. One possible interpretation is that a very clean sample mainly suppresses the desorption of water or other contaminants during deposition, whereas less favorable chamber conditions may influence nucleation in a way that incidentally smooths the film. Because the observed differences remained small, the issue was not pursued further.

        I performed lift-off by placing the sample in a crimp seal vial filled with pure acetone and heating it on a hot plate at about \qty{60}{\degreeCelsius} for \qtyrange{1}{3}{\hour}. Afterward, I used a pipette to agitate the acetone carefully until all visible metal flakes had detached. I rinsed the sample with IPA and dried it with nitrogen.

        For the penultimate fabrication step, I used the PlasmaPro 80 ICP RIE system from Oxford Instruments. Although this tool was not designed specifically for highly isotropic etching, I operated it in a regime that gave sufficiently isotropic results by combining low table RF power with elevated pressure. I first used an ignition step to stabilize the plasma before switching to the less stable regime that favored isotropic etching. The sample temperature was particularly important for obtaining the desired undercut. A table temperature of \qty{100}{\degreeCelsius} proved to be a good compromise because it produced a sufficient undercut while keeping the etch slow enough to control the depth reliably. The process parameters that gave the best results are listed in Table~\ref{tab:methods:rie_parameter}.

        I used a laser interferometer to monitor the etched depth. For this purpose, I chose a spot on the sample without metal structures where the reflected intensity was as large as possible without reaching saturation.
        
        During the etch, the signal follows an approximately cosine-like oscillation that can be used to estimate the removed thickness. The required number of periods $m$ depends on the laser wavelength $\lambda=\qty{632.8}{\nano\meter}$, the target depth $d=\qty{500}{\nano\meter}$, and the refractive index\footnote{Fujifilm Electronic Materials U.S.A., Inc., \texttt{Polyamic Acid Durimide 100}} $n=1.81$. For the present process, the target number of periods is
        \begin{align}
            m = \frac{2 \cdot n \cdot d}{\lambda} = \frac{2 \cdot 1.81 \cdot \qty{500}{\nano\meter}}{\qty{632.8}{\nano\meter}} = 2.86\,.
        \end{align}
        An example of the recorded laser intensity during etching is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:rie}.
        
        Because the etch rate varied noticeably from sample to sample, I etched each sample individually.

    \subsection*{Electrical Contacting}
    %=========================================================

        Electrical contacting was one of the most delicate steps in the entire fabrication sequence. Many otherwise successful samples were lost at this stage, so patience and a calm working environment were more important than speed. In practice, I obtained better results by contacting fewer samples carefully rather than rushing several samples in one session.

        I used a setup with stable hand positioning, appropriate lighting, and a comfortable bench height. Sitting close to the sample while keeping both hands supported made the process significantly easier. I also minimized avoidable sources of hand tremor before starting.

        To fix the sample, I used adhesive strips\footnote{Tesa, tesafilm standard.} on a copper plate. This arrangement kept the sample in place while still allowing me to reposition the plate. I prepared \qty{90}{\micro\meter} insulated copper wires soldered to post connectors in advance. I stripped the wire ends and twisted the bias line wires. I kept the wires as short as practical, typically \qtyrange{4}{5}{\centi\meter}, and insulated the rear part of each post connector with varnish\footnote{Oxford Instruments, \texttt{C5-101}, GE Low Temperature Varnish.}.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[15]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \includegraphics[width=.4\textwidth]{methods/sample/contacting/contacting.jpg}
            \caption{Example of a contacted sample, before adding strain relief.}
            \label{fig:methods:contacting}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        I positioned the wire ends on the sample pads with the help of modeling clay, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:contacting}. Then, using a sharpened toothpick, I applied a small amount of silver paste to connect each wire to its pad. The viscosity of the silver paste was critical. If it was too fluid, shorts could form. If it was too viscous, it tended to remain on the toothpick instead of wetting the pad properly. In practice, paste collected from the lid after shaking the bottle worked well. I transferred only enough paste to wet the pad. Afterward, I applied two-component epoxy as strain relief for the wires. Again, the viscosity was important. If I used the epoxy too soon after mixing, it continued to flow and could spread into unwanted areas. Because the interaction between epoxy and silver paste was not fully understood, I kept the two materials separate as much as possible. I tested the epoxy on small trial dots and waited until it reached a suitable consistency before applying it to the sample. I glued the two sides of the sample separately because the useful working window was fairly short.
        
        After mounting the sample in the cryostat, I scratched the shorting bridge with an engraving tool. I ensured proper grounding during this step and while closing the radiation shields by wearing a grounding bracelet. In dry conditions, I also increased the ambient humidity to reduce the risk of electrostatic discharge.
