    Single-electron transistors (SETs) consist of two junctions that define a small island capacitively coupled to a dc gate electrode. In the present approach, one of these junctions is realized as a mechanically controllable break junction (MCBJ), while the second junction is an oxide tunnel barrier. Aluminum is used as the device material, and an ac gate is integrated to couple microwave fields to the device. Figure~\ref{fig:methods:sem} shows such a sample.

    This section describes work carried out together with Patrick Raif as part of his project practicum\footnote{An optional component of the master's curriculum completed within four weeks.} \cite{raif_fabrication_2023}.

    The following subsections present the sample preparation process from complementary perspectives. First, I introduce the conceptual framework. Next, I discuss the main design choices and their rationale. The fabrication procedure, including practical considerations and typical challenges encountered in the laboratory, is given in Appendix~\ref{appendix:sample-fabrication}.

    \subsection{Device Concept} 
    \label{subsection:concept}
    %=========================================================

        In this subsection, I introduce the general concept of the sample fabrication process and its connection to the underlying device physics.

        The sample design must satisfy several requirements simultaneously. Mechanically, it must provide a freestanding bridge on a bendable substrate so that an atomic contact can be formed in a controlled manner. Lithographically, it must enable suspended shadow mask structures for double angle evaporation. Electrically, it must define an island between two weak links and provide gate coupling. Finally, the design should allow efficient coupling of microwave fields.

        The MCBJ is realized as a freestanding bridge-like structure on a bendable substrate, as illustrated schematically in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:bruchmechanik}. The substrate can be bent either by pushing a stamp from below or by pressing counter supports from above. This bending elongates the bridge and allows the junction to be tuned into the atomic contact and tunnel regimes. The mechanical setup of the MCBJ is described in more detail in Subsection~\ref{subsec:setup:mcbj}.

        A polyimide (PI) layer insulates the substrate from the structures produced in the subsequent steps. More importantly, the PI layer serves as a sacrificial layer. During later processing, it is partially etched off, allowing the structure to become freestanding.

        Next, a bilayer of positive electron sensitive resist is applied. Electron beam lithography (EBL) is then used to expose the desired pattern with high precision. After development, the exposed resist is removed, leaving behind the lithographically defined structure.

        In addition to the MCBJ, the SET requires a second weak link, which is realized as an oxide barrier. A convenient method for fabricating this barrier is shadow evaporation \cite{niemeyer_observation_1976,dolan_offset_1977}. In this technique, two evaporation steps are carried out at different incident angles, producing a lateral offset between the deposited structures. Between the two depositions, a controlled oxidation step is performed without breaking vacuum. This yields overlapping regions with a metal--oxide--metal cross section, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:flow}.

        Since the aim is to fabricate a superconducting single-electron transistor (SSET), aluminum (Al) is used in both evaporation steps. This results in an Al break junction (BJ) and an Al--Al$_\text{2}$O$_\text{3}$--Al oxide barrier, as described by Thomas Lorenz \cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018} and Susanne Sprenger \cite{sprenger_interplay_2019}. Alternatively, the second electrode material can be changed. For example, Al--Al$_\text{2}$O$_\text{3}$--Cu structures were successfully realized by Laura Sobral Rey \cite{sobral_rey_interplay_2022}.

        In the final fabrication step, approximately \qty{500}{\nano\meter} of the PI layer is removed by oxygen plasma etching. Because the etch proceeds essentially isotropically, it creates a pronounced undercut, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:sem}. This step releases the bridge and renders the break junction freestanding.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection{Design Choices} 
    \label{subsection:design}
    %=========================================================

        This section discusses the fabrication process from the perspective of the underlying design decisions. While there is no single unique route to a working sample, individual choices can have important practical consequences. The following paragraphs summarize the reasoning behind the most relevant steps.

        Bronze has long been used as a substrate material for MCBJ samples. A key reason is that it bends with comparatively little force while retaining excellent elastic behavior. This is crucial for MCBJs, where the junction configuration must be adjusted repeatedly and reproducibly. Other materials, such as copper, bend less uniformly and tend to deform mainly in the center beneath the stamp. This bending behavior leads to larger angular changes and poorer elastic reproducibility. In addition, copper often shows nonmonotonic bending behavior, which makes fine adjustment in the atomic contact regime impractical. Kapton, by contrast, bends homogeneously along its length but does not allow sufficiently strong bending for the present MCBJ geometry. Copper--beryllium would be mechanically suitable, but its toxicity makes it undesirable for routine use. Finally, the substrate must be nonmagnetic and contain as few magnetic impurities as possible. Considering all these requirements, bronze has proven to be the most suitable substrate material for this type of MCBJ \cite{schirm_einfluss_2009}.
        
        A polyamic acid (PA) layer is spin-coated onto the entire bronze wafer and converted into polyimide (PI) during a subsequent hard-bake step. Two resist layers are then applied. The thin top layer is optimized for high-resolution lithography and defines sharp feature edges. The thicker bottom layer serves two purposes. It lifts the top layer away from the substrate and promotes undercut formation during development. Two effects contribute to this undercut. First, electrons spread as they pass through the resist stack, so the exposed volume widens with depth. Backscattered electrons from the substrate can also reenter the resist from below and increase the effective exposure in the lower resist region. As a result, the bottom layer develops more rapidly and forms an undercut. Second, prolonged rinsing in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) after development in diluted methyl isobutyl ketone (MIBK) further enhances the undercut.

        EBL is carried out using a Zeiss Crossbeam system that was modified for significantly faster beam blanking. The beam blanker operates synchronously with the beam deflection, allowing arbitrary structures to be exposed precisely within the selected field of view. For small high-resolution structures, a field size of \qty{100}{\micro\meter} is typically used, whereas coarser and larger features are written in a \qty{1000}{\micro\meter} field. Structures exceeding these dimensions are exposed over multiple adjacent fields by moving the sample stage. To ensure smooth transitions between neighboring fields, a deliberate overlap is included for stitching.

        At the beginning of my doctoral work, only one of the two previously used EBL software packages, \texttt{Elphy Plus} by Raith, was functional and properly supported. The sample design therefore had to be reimplemented from scratch because my predecessors had used a different software environment. The dimensions of the break junction and the gate follow the established geometry and are consistent with earlier implementations in Refs.~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,sprenger_interplay_2019,sobral_rey_interplay_2022}.

        The tip of the bottom electrode connects to the island during the second evaporation at an oblique angle. The angle and height of the mask define the lateral offset of this deposition. I encountered difficulties with the mechanical stability of the evaporation mask. During evaporation, the mask sagged, and the shadow electrode did not connect electrically to the island. I modified the finger geometry to reduce the freestanding length of the evaporation mask. A new resist further improved the stability of the mask, and the finger then reliably reached the island during the second evaporation. The complete electron beam lithography design is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:methods:ebeam}.

        The larger leads and contact pads are arranged to simplify electrical contacting. In addition, an ac gate or stripline is placed at a distance of \qty{8}{\micro\meter} from the junction to maximize microwave coupling while keeping the electron beam writing time within reasonable limits. A shorting structure is also included to protect the sensitive tunnel barrier against electrostatic discharge. Finally, the central vertical lead is made wider to improve its visibility, which helps to align the break junction precisely with the stamp during mounting.

        The evaporator used in this work has a comparatively long source-to-sample distance. As a result, the aluminum deposition is highly directional. For the same reason, however, the effective deposition cross section is reduced. The system offers two methods for aluminum evaporation: thermal evaporation and electron beam evaporation. In thermal evaporation, a large current heats a small crucible until the aluminum evaporates. The crucible used for this method is fragile and must be loaded and completely emptied during each run to avoid crack formation.
        
        This procedure is laborious, prone to error, and offers no clear advantage over electron beam evaporation. In electron beam evaporation, a focused electron beam locally heats the aluminum charge, resulting in a more efficient and reliable deposition process. For this reason, and in contrast to Refs.~\cite{lorenz_wechselspiel_2018,sprenger_interplay_2019,sobral_rey_interplay_2022}, I used electron beam evaporation throughout this work.

        The oxidation step between the two evaporations is performed in the load lock of the evaporator at an oxygen pressure of approximately \qty{3.0(2)}{\milli\barunit} for \qty{3}{\minute} \cite{sobral_rey_interplay_2022}. In the past, this step was found to be insufficiently reproducible. It is therefore monitored carefully in the present process \cite{sprenger_interplay_2019,conzelmann_optimierung_2016}.

        Reactive ion etching (RIE) is carried out using the \textit{PlasmaPro 80 ICP RIE system} from Oxford Instruments. I established the break junction etching process on this newly introduced system. Although this tool is not primarily designed for highly isotropic etching, acceptable uniformity can be achieved by combining high inductively coupled plasma (ICP) radio-frequency (RF) power, low RF power at the sample table, and high chamber pressure. The ICP source generates a high-density plasma by coupling energy into the chamber through an electromagnetic field, whereas the RF power applied to the table controls the ion energy at the sample surface. A heated sample table increases the chemical etching component and further improves etch isotropy. The etched depth is monitored in real time by laser interferometry, which allows precise process control.

        The final step is electrical contacting of the sample. Wedge bonding is unsuitable because the bonding wire can press through the pad and penetrate the soft PI layer, thereby causing a short to ground. Instead, thin copper wires are attached to the pads using conductive silver paste. This approach provides reliable electrical contact without damaging the underlying structure.

        Overall, the modified finger geometry and resist process stabilized the suspended evaporation mask and enabled the shadow electrode to connect reliably to the island. Together with the isotropic etching, protected contact layout, and nondestructive electrical contacting, these changes produced freestanding MCBJ devices that combined a tunable atomic contact, an oxide tunnel barrier, and integrated gate coupling. The resulting geometry provided the mechanical stability and electrical access required for the measurements described in the following sections.
