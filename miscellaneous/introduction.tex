%=========================================================
\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
%=========================================================

    Information processing has become a foundation of modern science, industry, and everyday life. Its continued expansion increases both the demand for computational capability and the associated energy consumption, motivating the investigation of new device concepts and physical principles \cite{iea_energy_ai_2025}.

    For decades, increasing component density and reducing transistor dimensions improved computational capability, as summarized by Moore's law and Dennard scaling \cite{moore_cramming_1965,dennard_design_1974}. This strategy no longer guarantees proportional improvements in performance and energy efficiency because power dissipation, fabrication complexity, material properties, and device physics increasingly constrain further scaling \cite{esmaeilzadeh_dark_2011,markov_limits_2014}. As device dimensions approach microscopic scales, quantum effects change from small corrections into central design constraints. The same transition creates opportunities for architectures that use quantum mechanics as a functional resource. Quantum computers do not replace classical computers, but they may provide advantages for selected problems, particularly the simulation of quantum systems originally envisioned by Feynman \cite{feynman_simulating_1982}. Such calculations could extend the accessible electronic structure and dynamics of molecules, with potential applications in pharmaceuticals, catalysts, and materials development \cite{cao_quantum_2019}.

    Superconducting circuits are an important platform for quantum information processing because they combine low-loss electrical dynamics, nonlinear circuit elements, and microwave control at the level of individual quantum states \cite{devoret_superconducting_2013,kjaergaard_superconducting_2020}. Their operation relies on nanoscale weak links whose useful nonlinearity, dissipation, and response to microwave fields originate from microscopic charge transport. This thesis investigates these underlying transport processes and the conditions under which a superconducting junction can be represented by an effective circuit element.

    At the circuit level, a superconducting weak link is often represented as an ideal nonlinear element characterized by a small set of effective parameters. Its measured response, however, emerges from microscopic transport processes whose relative importance depends on the junction transmission, temperature, applied voltage, and electromagnetic environment. Quasiparticles, Cooper pairs, and multiple Andreev reflection contribute distinct charge transfer mechanisms, while highly transmitting atomic contacts are described by a small set of quantum transmission channels rather than by a bulk conductivity \cite{agrait_quantum_2003,scheer_conduction_1997}. These processes determine not only the useful nonlinear response of a junction, but also its dissipation, spectral structure, and sensitivity to fluctuations. Establishing how they appear in experimentally accessible transport observables is therefore necessary to connect the ideal circuit description of a superconducting device to its physical realization.

    This connection becomes particularly important under microwave irradiation. A periodic drive can create photon assisted replicas that encode the energy and effective charge of the underlying transport process, but the observed response also depends on the frequency dependent coupling between the source, the junction, and its environment \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. The same spectrum can therefore contain information about microscopic transport, microwave calibration, and environmental dynamics. Separating these contributions requires reference systems with controlled transmission, direct comparison between static and driven transport, and models whose assumptions and limits are tested against the measured \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} responses. This thesis addresses that need by following superconducting transport from weakly transmitting tunnel barriers to atomic contacts with finite and high transmission.

    Experimentally, I studied aluminum superconducting junctions at millikelvin temperatures under dc bias and continuous microwave irradiation. Oxide tunnel barriers provided the weak transmission reference, while a mechanically controllable break junction allowed atomic contacts with different channel compositions to be formed and adjusted. For each transport regime, I compared the static and irradiated \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} responses. I used the static spectra to determine the superconducting energy scales and, for atomic contacts, the individual channel transmissions. The driven spectra then revealed how quasiparticle, multiple Andreev reflection, and Cooper pair contributions evolved with microwave amplitude and junction transmission.

    \subsubsection*{State of the Art and Open Questions}

    The limiting regimes of superconducting transport are well established. At low transmission, quasiparticle tunneling between superconducting electrodes probes the BCS density of states through the gap edge and coherence peaks. Under a harmonic voltage drive, the Tien-Gordon Model describes photon assisted replicas of the static characteristic that are shifted by integer multiples of the photon energy \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_study_1961,tien_multiphoton_1963}. At finite transmission, this independent tunneling picture no longer suffices. An atomic contact instead contains a small number of conduction channels whose transmission probabilities determine its nonlinear superconducting response. Multiple Andreev reflection transfers charge through repeated electron--hole conversion, produces the subharmonic gap structure, and allows the channel set to be inferred from the static \textit{I--V} characteristic \cite{cuevas_hamiltonian_1996,scheer_conduction_1997,agrait_quantum_2003}.

    Microwave irradiation extends these measurements from static spectroscopy to driven transport. Coherent Josephson oscillations can synchronize with an external drive and produce Shapiro steps at voltages fixed by the Josephson relation \cite{shapiro_josephson_1963}. Incoherent transport processes also exchange energy with the drive. Under irradiation, these mechanisms produce photon assisted quasiparticle tunneling, photon assisted multiple Andreev reflection (PAMAR), and photon assisted incoherent Cooper pair tunneling (PAICPT). Their voltage spacing and amplitude dependence can constrain the energy and effective charge involved in transport \cite{cuevas_subharmonic_2002,roychowdhury_microwave_2015,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. The resulting spectra provide information that is not available from a static trace alone, but their interpretation becomes more difficult when several processes contribute in the same voltage range.

    A junction cannot generally be separated from its electromagnetic environment. The junction capacitance and surrounding impedance generate voltage fluctuations and permit energy exchange during charge transfer. In the weak tunneling regime, these effects are described by \textit{P(E)} Theory, which connects the environmental spectrum to measurable changes in the tunneling current \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992}. At finite transmission, environmental corrections depend on the individual channel transmissions and on the multiple charges transferred by Andreev processes, so a description based only on a resistance and capacitance becomes insufficient \cite{levy_yeyati_dynamical_2005}. This defines an important boundary in the field: macroscopic circuit parameters may describe an opaque junction, while a microscopic account of conduction channels, charge transfer, and nonequilibrium occupations becomes necessary as the transmission and drive increase.

    A particularly close precedent for the present atomic contact measurements is the work of Chauvin \textit{et al.}~on voltage biased aluminum break junctions \cite{chauvin_superconducting_2006}. They determined the individual channel transmissions from the static MAR response and observed coherent integer and fractional Shapiro resonances on a background modified by PAMAR. At finite bias, a microscopic calculation of driven MAR reproduced representative differential conductance traces using the measured channel transmissions and calibrated microwave drive without adjustable parameters. Their low-impedance environment approached the voltage bias assumed by the microscopic theory and supported coherent phase locking near zero voltage. This work established quantitative PAMAR in aluminum atomic contacts with independently characterized channels, but it did not address the simultaneous amplitude resolved separation of PAMAR and incoherent Cooper pair transport.

    Complementary experiments established the remaining ingredients. In small Josephson STM junctions, the replica spacing and squared-Bessel amplitude dependence identified PAICPT and distinguished it from coherent phase locking \cite{roychowdhury_microwave_2015}. Kot \textit{et al.}~investigated quasiparticle tunneling, PAICPT, and driven MAR across different conductance regimes of a superconducting STM junction \cite{kot_microwave-assisted_2020}. They also showed that direct charge dependent dressing of the static full counting statistics (FCS) components does not reproduce the irradiated MAR spectrum, while a microscopic driven MAR calculation performs substantially better. Related STM measurements of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov states showed that simple effective-charge dressing also fails for resonant Andreev reflection because the microwave field addresses the electron and hole thresholds separately, so the visible sideband spacing need not reflect only the net transferred charge \cite{peters_resonant_2020}.

    Carrad \textit{et al.}~recorded microwave amplitude and frequency dependent maps of nanowire Josephson junctions. They identified the MAR thresholds from minima in the static differential resistance and resolved their photon assisted sidebands using its second derivative. For a phenomenological comparison, they applied charge dependent dressing to the measured static current under the piecewise assumption that one transferred charge dominates in each voltage region around a MAR threshold \cite{carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. Taken together, these studies did not provide a common description of coexisting PAMAR and PAICPT in the same few-channel atomic contact with independently characterized channels. It therefore remained unclear whether both contributions could be separated using independently constrained static parameters and whether one construction would remain useful across substantially different channel compositions.

    The available approaches leave a practical modeling gap. Microscopic calculations of driven MAR account for the coherent microwave dynamics, but require computationally demanding Floquet treatments. Published comparisons demonstrate an improvement over direct charge dependent dressing of the static FCS components, but remain restricted to selected spectra and retain substantial deviations from the measurements \cite{kot_microwave-assisted_2020}. Their computational cost also makes systematic fitting across complete microwave amplitude maps and multiple channel compositions difficult. Phenomenological constructions avoid this cost, but their reliance on the measured line shape and piecewise regime assignments limits their predictive independence \cite{carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. A deterministic intermediate description is therefore needed to connect independently characterized static transport to complete driven transport maps without requiring a full microscopic calculation at every parameter point.

    Despite the mature descriptions of the individual limiting cases, driven measurements combine intrinsic transport, microwave coupling, heating, nonequilibrium occupations, and environmental dynamics in the same observable. Similar voltage spacings can arise from coherent phase locking and incoherent photon assisted transfer, and a microwave amplitude extracted from a stationary model remains physically meaningful only while the assumptions of that model are satisfied. The open task is therefore not merely to identify additional spectral features, but to determine which information can be extracted from them reliably.

    \subsubsection*{Contributions}

    The deterministic threshold decomposition developed in this thesis fills this methodological role. I first tested direct charge dependent dressing of the static FCS components and resolved its failure across the full transport maps. I then used the static MAR response calculated from independently extracted channel transmissions and introduced normalized weights that partition its differential conductance smoothly on the inverse-voltage coordinate, where the MAR thresholds occur at integer charge orders. I reconstructed an effective current for each threshold before applying the corresponding charge dependent photon assisted transformation. Combined with an independently constrained PAICPT contribution, this construction reproduces the dominant trajectories and spectral weight redistribution observed under microwave irradiation without selecting structures from the driven measurement.

    The static environmental analysis and the amplitude dependence of the replicas originating from zero bias support their identification with incoherent Cooper pair transport rather than the coherent Shapiro response observed in voltage biased atomic contacts. I tested the combined description on contacts with substantially different channel compositions, ranging from moderate transmissions to a nearly ballistic leading channel. The agreement shows that the same framework captures the principal microwave response throughout the experimentally studied few-channel regime. This construction does not replace microscopic Floquet theory. Instead, it provides a transparent and computationally tractable model for testing charge assignments, separating overlapping mechanisms, and identifying where a microscopic treatment becomes necessary. At high conductance, where many channels contribute and a unique channel decomposition is no longer possible, the controlled description breaks down. In this regime, I observed an additional subharmonic photon assisted response that is not explained microscopically by the models considered here.

    In a complementary tunnel barrier experiment, I observed microwave induced rectification whose magnitude and sign depend systematically on frequency and drive amplitude. This response violates the bias symmetry assumed by the stationary Tien-Gordon Model and therefore marks a second limit of transport based microwave calibration. When a unique microscopic mechanism could not be assigned, I separated the experimentally established observation from the interpretations that remain open.

    \subsubsection*{Thesis Outline}

    Chapter~\ref{ch:superconducting-transport} develops the theoretical framework used throughout the thesis. It begins with BCS quasiparticle tunneling in the weak transmission regime, introduces Josephson phase dynamics, extends the description to channel resolved mesoscopic transport and multiple Andreev reflection, and finally includes charge fluctuations and energy exchange with the electromagnetic environment. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} then describes how the samples were fabricated and measured. It follows the experimental chain from the mechanically controllable break junction and tunnel barriers through the cryogenic wiring and microwave coupling to the digital acquisition, processing, and fitting infrastructure.

    Chapter~\ref{chapter:experimental-results} presents the measurements in order of increasing transmission and model complexity. Section~\ref{sec:tunnelbarrier} establishes the tunnel regime using two aluminum tunnel barriers. It first examines high-resolution, bias symmetric photon assisted quasiparticle transport and then investigates the frequency dependent rectification observed in a nominally similar device. Section~\ref{sec:atomic-contacts} turns to mechanically controlled atomic contacts. It first uses multiple Andreev reflection to determine their transmission channels and then develops the combined PAMAR and PAICPT description of their microwave response. The comparison across substantially different channel compositions establishes the useful range of this framework, while the final high-conductance contact exposes its breakdown and an additional subharmonic response. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes the results across these regimes, distinguishes established observations from unresolved mechanisms, and identifies the measurements needed to test the remaining interpretations.

    The appendices collect supporting material that would interrupt the progression of the main text. They document the research software and supplementary digital resources in Appendix~\ref{appendix:software-supplementary}, provide additional theoretical and experimental methods in Appendices~\ref{app:theory:diffusive-transport}, \ref{appendix:sample-fabrication}, and \ref{appendix:cryostat}, and present supplementary analyses of the tunnel barrier and atomic contact measurements in Appendices~\ref{appendix:tb2} and \ref{appendix:ac}. Appendix~\ref{ch:ssn-set} places the main junction measurements in the wider context of related hybrid superconducting single-electron devices.

    Taken together, this thesis treats a superconducting junction not as a fixed circuit symbol, but as a physical boundary whose meaning changes with transmission and drive. In the tunnel regime, the superconducting gap provides a precise energy ruler. In an atomic contact, discrete conduction channels expose the microscopic structure hidden behind a single conductance value. Under microwave irradiation, the trajectories and spectral weights of replicated features reveal the energy and charge associated with transport, while deviations from these patterns expose environmental coupling and the limits of the models themselves. The progression from tunnel barriers to atomic contacts therefore represents more than a sequence of increasingly complicated devices. It follows the point at which a macroscopic circuit description gives way to a microscopic quantum conductor. Establishing where that transition occurs, what can be learned from it, and where present descriptions fail is essential for turning quantum transport from an unavoidable consequence of miniaturization into a controllable property of superconducting devices.
