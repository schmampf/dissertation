\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}
%=========================================================

    Information processing has become a foundation of modern science, industry, and everyday life. Its continued expansion increases both the demand for computational capability and the associated energy consumption, motivating the investigation of new device concepts and physical principles \cite{iea_energy_ai_2025}.

    For decades, increasing component density and reducing transistor dimensions improved computational capability, as summarized by Moore's law and Dennard scaling \cite{moore_cramming_1965,dennard_design_1974}. This strategy no longer guarantees proportional improvements in performance and energy efficiency because power dissipation, fabrication complexity, material properties, and device physics increasingly constrain further scaling \cite{esmaeilzadeh_dark_2011,markov_limits_2014}. At microscopic dimensions, quantum effects become central design constraints and potential functional resources. Quantum computers may consequently provide advantages for selected problems, particularly the simulation of quantum systems envisioned by Feynman \cite{feynman_simulating_1982}. Sufficiently capable quantum computers could address electronic structure and dynamics beyond the practical reach of established classical methods. This capability could open access to unresolved problems in strongly correlated materials, catalysis, and biochemical reactions, and ultimately support the predictive design of new compounds and materials \cite{mcardle_quantum_2020}.

    Superconducting circuits are an important platform for quantum information processing because they combine low-loss electrical dynamics, nonlinear circuit elements, and microwave control of individual quantum states \cite{devoret_superconducting_2013,kjaergaard_superconducting_2020}. Their operation relies on nanoscale weak links whose nonlinearity, dissipation, and microwave response originate from microscopic charge transport. Understanding these processes is therefore relevant both to mesoscopic physics and to the control of superconducting devices.

    At the circuit level, a superconducting weak link is often represented as an ideal nonlinear element with a few effective parameters. Its measured response, however, emerges from quasiparticle, Cooper pair, and multiple Andreev reflection processes whose relative importance depends on transmission, temperature, voltage, and the electromagnetic environment. Highly transmitting atomic contacts are further described by discrete quantum transmission channels rather than a bulk conductivity \cite{scheer_conduction_1997,agrait_quantum_2003}. Connecting these microscopic processes to transport observables is necessary to understand the nonlinearity, dissipation, and spectral structure hidden behind the circuit element.

    Under microwave irradiation, photon assisted tunneling produces replicas of quasiparticle transport features, while Josephson oscillations can synchronize with the applied drive and generate Shapiro steps \cite{tien_multiphoton_1963,shapiro_josephson_1963}. These responses encode characteristic energies and transferred charges, but also depend on the frequency dependent coupling between the source, junction, and environment. Microwave spectroscopy can therefore probe microscopic transport, calibrate the local drive, and expose environmental dynamics. Separating these contributions requires controlled transmission regimes and models tested against static and driven \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} responses. This thesis follows that progression from weakly transmitting tunnel barriers to finite- and high-transmission atomic contacts.

    I studied aluminum junctions at millikelvin temperatures under dc bias and continuous microwave irradiation. Oxide tunnel barriers provided the weak transmission reference, while a mechanically controllable break junction was used to form atomic contacts with different channel compositions. Static spectra determined the superconducting energy scales and channel transmissions. Driven spectra then revealed how quasiparticle, multiple Andreev reflection, and Cooper pair contributions evolved with microwave amplitude and transmission.

    \subsubsection*{State of the Art and Open Questions}

    The limiting transport regimes are well established. At low transmission, quasiparticle tunneling probes the BCS density of states. At finite transmission, multiple Andreev reflection produces the subharmonic gap structure and allows the transmission channels of an atomic contact to be inferred from its static \textit{I--V} characteristic \cite{bardeen_tunnelling_1961,giaever_study_1961,cuevas_hamiltonian_1996,scheer_conduction_1997,agrait_quantum_2003}.

    Microwave driving adds coherent phase locking, photon assisted quasiparticle tunneling, photon assisted multiple Andreev reflection (PAMAR), and photon assisted incoherent Cooper pair tunneling (PAICPT), which may overlap in the same spectrum \cite{cuevas_subharmonic_2002}. The junction environment further reshapes this response through voltage fluctuations and energy exchange. Its interpretation must therefore connect the transport regime to the surrounding circuit \cite{falci_quasiparticle_1991,ingold_charge_1992,levy_yeyati_dynamical_2005}.

    A particularly close precedent is the work of Chauvin \textit{et al.}~on voltage biased aluminum atomic contacts \cite{chauvin_superconducting_2006}. They extracted channel transmissions from static MAR and observed coherent integer and fractional Shapiro resonances on a PAMAR background. Their low-impedance environment supported phase locking, while microscopic driven MAR theory reproduced representative finite-bias conductance traces using the measured transmissions and calibrated drive without adjustable parameters. This work established quantitative PAMAR, but did not address its amplitude resolved separation from incoherent Cooper pair transport.

    Josephson STM experiments identified PAICPT through its replica spacing and squared-Bessel amplitude dependence \cite{roychowdhury_microwave_2015}. Kot \textit{et al.}~then studied quasiparticle tunneling, PAICPT, and driven MAR across different STM conductance regimes. Direct charge dependent dressing of the static full counting statistics (FCS) components failed to reproduce the irradiated MAR spectrum, while a microscopic calculation performed better but retained substantial deviations \cite{kot_microwave-assisted_2020}. Related measurements of Yu-Shiba-Rusinov states further showed that separate electron and hole thresholds can make the visible sideband spacing differ from the net transferred charge \cite{peters_resonant_2020}.

    Carrad \textit{et al.}~resolved charge dependent MAR sidebands in microwave amplitude and frequency maps of nanowire Josephson junctions. Their phenomenological comparison dressed the measured static current under the piecewise assumption that one charge dominates near each MAR threshold \cite{carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. Taken together, these studies did not provide a common description of coexisting PAMAR and PAICPT in the same few-channel contact with independently characterized channels or test such a construction across substantially different channel compositions.

    These approaches leave a practical modeling gap. Microscopic Floquet treatments include coherent microwave dynamics but are computationally demanding and difficult to apply systematically across complete amplitude maps. Phenomenological constructions are tractable, but their reliance on measured line shapes and piecewise assignments limits predictive independence \cite{kot_microwave-assisted_2020,carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. A deterministic intermediate description is therefore needed to connect independently characterized static transport to complete driven maps.

    A microwave transport map does not reflect the intrinsic junction response alone. The measured structures also depend on the microwave voltage reaching the junction, heating, nonequilibrium occupations, and the electromagnetic environment. Moreover, similar voltage spacings can result from coherent phase locking and incoherent photon assisted transfer. Identifying a spectral feature is therefore insufficient. Its interpretation requires testing which quantities, such as transferred charge or local microwave amplitude, remain robust under the assumptions of the applied model.

    \subsubsection*{Contributions}

    The deterministic threshold decomposition developed in this thesis fills this role. After finding that direct FCS dressing failed across the full maps, I partitioned the simulated static MAR conductance around its known thresholds and reconstructed an effective current for each charge order. Charge dependent photon dressing and an independently constrained PAICPT contribution then reproduce the dominant trajectories and spectral weight redistribution without selecting structures from the driven measurement.

    The static environmental analysis and the zero-bias replicas support incoherent Cooper pair transport rather than coherent Shapiro response. Tests from moderate transmissions to a nearly ballistic leading channel show that the combined framework captures the principal few-channel microwave response. It does not replace microscopic Floquet theory, but provides a tractable model for testing charge assignments, separating overlapping mechanisms, and locating where a microscopic treatment becomes necessary. In the many-channel regime, the controlled description breaks down and an additional subharmonic photon assisted response remains without a unique microscopic explanation.

    In a complementary tunnel barrier experiment, I observed microwave induced rectification whose magnitude and sign depend systematically on frequency and drive amplitude. The response violates the bias symmetry of the stationary Tien-Gordon Model and marks a second limit of transport based microwave calibration. Where no unique mechanism could be assigned, I separated the established observation from the interpretations that remain open.

    \subsubsection*{Thesis Outline}

    Chapter~\ref{ch:superconducting-transport} develops the theory from BCS quasiparticle tunneling and Josephson phase dynamics to channel resolved MAR and environmental energy exchange. Chapter~\ref{ch:methods} follows the experiment from sample fabrication and cryogenic microwave coupling to acquisition, processing, and fitting.

    Chapter~\ref{chapter:experimental-results} orders the measurements by increasing transmission and model complexity. Section~\ref{sec:tunnelbarrier} examines high-resolution photon assisted quasiparticle transport and frequency dependent rectification in two aluminum tunnel barriers. Section~\ref{sec:atomic-contacts} extracts atomic-contact transmissions, develops the combined PAMAR and PAICPT description, tests it across different channel compositions, and concludes with its breakdown and the subharmonic response at high conductance. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarizes the established observations, model limits, and unresolved mechanisms.

    The appendices document the research software, additional theory and methods, and supplementary tunnel barrier and atomic contact analyses in Appendices~\ref{appendix:software-supplementary}, \ref{app:theory:diffusive-transport}, \ref{appendix:sample-fabrication}, \ref{appendix:cryostat}, \ref{appendix:tb2}, and \ref{appendix:ac}. Appendix~\ref{ch:ssn-set} places the junction measurements in the wider context of hybrid superconducting single-electron devices.

    Taken together, this thesis treats a superconducting junction not as a fixed circuit symbol, but as a physical boundary whose meaning changes with transmission and drive. In the tunnel regime, the superconducting gap provides a precise energy ruler. In an atomic contact, discrete conduction channels expose the microscopic structure hidden behind a single conductance value. Under microwave irradiation, the trajectories and spectral weights of replicated features reveal the energy and charge associated with transport, while deviations from these patterns expose environmental coupling and the limits of the models themselves. The progression from tunnel barriers to atomic contacts therefore represents more than a sequence of increasingly complicated devices. It follows the point at which a macroscopic circuit description gives way to a microscopic quantum conductor. Establishing where that transition occurs, what can be learned from it, and where present descriptions fail is essential for turning quantum transport from an unavoidable consequence of miniaturization into a controllable property of superconducting devices.
