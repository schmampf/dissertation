%=========================================================
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}
%=========================================================

    In this thesis, I systematically mapped microwave driven superconducting transport from the tunnel limit to atomic contacts with nearly perfect channel transmission. Across this range, the same experimental strategy connected static junction characterization with complete microwave amplitude maps. This progression resolved conventional photon assisted tunneling with unprecedented detail, revealed two previously unreported transport phenomena, and established a unified phenomenological description of driven charge transfer in few-channel contacts.

    Static transport first determined the superconducting energy scales and the structure of each junction. In atomic contacts, channel resolved MAR spectroscopy additionally provided the mesoscopic pincode. Microwave irradiation then introduced a second spectroscopic dimension. The voltage positions of the resulting structures constrained their energy thresholds and transferred charges, while their evolution with drive amplitude distinguished processes that occupied the same voltage range. The combination transformed complex transport maps into a systematic comparison of quasiparticle, Cooper pair, and multiple Andreev reflection transport.

    This approach relied on stable mechanically controllable break junctions, filtered low-temperature measurements, synchronized acquisition, and a common numerical analysis chain. In situ calibration converted the hardware dependent microwave output into the local ac voltage experienced by each junction. The resulting maps could therefore be compared on the physical drive coordinate relevant to the transport models rather than on an instrument setting.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{From Tunnel Barriers to Atomic Contacts}
    %=========================================================

        TB1 established the high-resolution tunnel reference. The SIS BCS model described its static transport, and the Tien-Gordon Model reproduced the complete microwave driven evolution with exceptional agreement. Individual photon assisted replicas remained visually resolved and could be followed continuously to at least order 25. The measured ladder extended substantially beyond the superconducting gap while retaining the predicted redistribution of spectral weight across the full amplitude range. To my knowledge, no previous SIS amplitude map has continuously resolved quasiparticle replicas to a higher order. This result demonstrated quantitative Tien-Gordon transport deep into the multiphoton regime and set the reference against which the more complex junctions could be understood.

        TB2 revealed the first new phenomenon. I observed microwave induced rectification with a local current asymmetry reaching up to \qty{25}{\percent}. Dc bias, microwave amplitude, and frequency provided three independent controls over its magnitude and polarity. The response switched on with increasing drive, reacted sensitively to frequency, and reversed direction across the investigated interval. It was nonhysteretic under the investigated conditions and turned the junction into a microwave controlled diode-like element. Comparison with the bias symmetric Tien-Gordon response isolated the additional asymmetric contribution and established a regime beyond uniform ac driving. A resonantly driven Josephson weak link provides a common, experimentally testable explanation for the drive threshold, frequency sensitivity, and polarity reversal.

        The atomic contacts extended the experiment from weak tunneling to discrete channels of finite and nearly perfect transmission. Along a complete opening trace, the extracted pincodes evolved continuously within conductance plateaus and reorganized at abrupt jumps, revealing contact changes that remained hidden in the normal-state conductance. Under microwave irradiation, the same channels supported overlapping quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair processes. I connected these structures through a deterministic decomposition of the static MAR response around its known energy thresholds, followed by charge dependent photon dressing. Combined with photon assisted incoherent Cooper pair tunneling, this framework reproduced the dominant trajectories and their spectral weight evolution across complete amplitude maps without adjustment to individual driven features.

        Contacts with substantially different pincodes tested this description from moderate transmission to a nearly ballistic leading channel. Threshold weighted PAMAR captured the dominant finite-bias response throughout this range. The squared-Bessel amplitude evolution of the replicas originating from zero bias identified an additional PAICPT contribution and distinguished it from a coherent Shapiro response. The model therefore did more than reproduce a collection of lines. It provided a common assignment of overlapping structures to their energy thresholds, effective charge orders, and static transport origins.

        At high conductance, a second new phenomenon emerged. The microwave maps of C3 contained trajectories halfway between the conventional Cooper pair replicas. Their positions and squared-Bessel-like amplitude evolution consistently identified an additional subharmonic \(p=2\) component. Its effective \(4e\) form does not yet determine the microscopic transfer process, but the replica pattern itself is an experimental result. To my knowledge, such a subharmonic PAICPT response has not been reported previously. The transition from the controlled few-channel regime to this response marks a new region in which higher charge correlations, phase dynamics, and the electromagnetic environment can be investigated together.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Charge Resolved Microwave Spectroscopy}
    %=========================================================

        The broader contribution of these measurements is a practical route from transport maps to charge resolved spectroscopy. Feature spacing alone is generally insufficient because quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair structures can overlap or share characteristic voltages. Static transport supplies the channel composition and energy thresholds. Microwave spacing constrains the effective transferred charge. Amplitude evolution then tests how spectral weight moves between replica orders. Using all three pieces of information made it possible to separate coexisting processes without selecting isolated features from the driven data.

        This perspective also clarifies when a macroscopic junction description is sufficient and when microscopic charge processes must be resolved. In the tunnel reference, a single driven voltage and the static \textit{I--V} characteristic captured the response over an exceptionally wide range. At finite transmission, the individual channels and voltage dependent MAR charge orders became essential. The electromagnetic environment added another active degree of freedom by setting the local drive, enabling incoherent Cooper pair transfer, and coupling phase dynamics back into quasiparticle transport. The rectification in TB2 and the subharmonic response in C3 appeared precisely where these additional degrees of freedom became visible. They therefore extend the systematic progression rather than standing as unrelated anomalies.

        These results also have implications for superconducting quantum circuits. Josephson junctions are commonly represented by a small set of circuit parameters, although their dissipation and microwave response originate from microscopic charge transport within a local electromagnetic environment. The measurements here show that this environment can set the effective drive at the junction, redistribute quasiparticle and Cooper pair transport, and generate strongly frequency dependent responses that are absent from static characterization. The same spectroscopy could therefore be used in dedicated test structures to probe microwave delivery, package resonances, subgap transport, and unintended high-transmission channels. Such diagnostics would not directly measure qubit coherence, but they could connect junction fabrication and microwave packaging to microscopic loss channels that limit the reproducibility of superconducting quantum circuits.

        The underlying approach is not restricted to superconducting qubits or metallic atomic contacts. Hybrid nanowires, quantum dots, molecular junctions, superconducting detectors, and driven single-charge devices all support regimes in which several charge transfer processes coexist under periodic drive. Across these systems, static energy thresholds, replica spacing, and amplitude evolution provide three complementary coordinates for identifying nonequilibrium transport. Charge resolved microwave spectroscopy can therefore serve as a general strategy for organizing complex driven responses rather than as a model tied to one device class.

        The resulting framework is intentionally phenomenological, but it is constrained by independently characterized static transport and a common physical assignment of the driven structures. It bridges the experimentally transparent Tien-Gordon limit and a future microscopic nonequilibrium treatment. In this role, it provides both a compact description of the present data and a benchmark for determining which features require coherence, occupation dynamics, or environmental backaction.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Future Research}
    %=========================================================

        The next experimental step is to embed the junction in a deliberately designed electromagnetic environment. A calibrated impedance or resonator would turn microwave amplitude, phase, and environmental mode structure into controlled variables. Phase control through a superconducting loop would further separate incoherent Cooper pair transfer from Josephson phase locking. Applied to TB2, such a device could test whether a resonance and the driven weak link generate the observed rectification. Applied to atomic contacts, it would reveal how selected environmental modes redistribute weight between quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair processes. The environment would then become a tunable part of the junction rather than an uncontrolled boundary condition.

        A longer-term direction is to integrate charge resolved transport spectroscopy with superconducting quantum circuits. A junction or tunable weak link embedded in a resonator could simultaneously probe transferred charge, phase coherence, and photon exchange with a designed mode. In this setting, the environment would no longer appear only as a source of broadening or loss, but as a controlled quantum degree of freedom. Correlating the transport signatures with resonator loss, quasiparticle dynamics, or qubit relaxation could establish a direct experimental link between microscopic junction processes and circuit level coherence.

        On the theory side, microscopic Floquet or Keldysh calculations can build directly on the extracted pincodes, calibrated drive amplitudes, and charge-order assignments established here. The central question is not only whether such a theory improves the agreement. It is which parts of the response follow from charge quantization and static thresholds, and which require coherent interference, nonequilibrium occupations, or backaction from the environment. Finite-frequency current noise would add an independent probe of transferred charge and photon emission or absorption, particularly in the subharmonic regime.

        A superconducting single-electron transistor provides the natural next platform. It adds charging energy and gate induced charge to the transmission, bias, amplitude, frequency, phase, and impedance controls established for the two-terminal junctions. The connection between MAR charge order and energy threshold developed in this thesis offers a way to organize the otherwise dense SSET stability diagrams. Gate--bias--amplitude maps could follow how a process transferring an effective charge \(me\) crosses a Coulomb threshold, absorbs or emits photons, and changes the charge state of the island. Phase control could then distinguish transport cycles containing coherent Josephson transfer from incoherent quasiparticle and MAR sequences. This extends the present charge resolved spectroscopy toward the original goal of studying superconducting coherence, nonequilibrium, and charge fluctuations within the same tunable device.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Final Remarks}
    %=========================================================

        The central result of this thesis is that systematic microwave amplitude spectroscopy provides access to superconducting charge transport across widely different transmission regimes. In the tunnel limit, it resolved the conventional Tien-Gordon response with exceptional dynamic range. At finite transmission, it connected static energy thresholds and channel composition to the simultaneous transfer of different effective charges. At the boundaries of these descriptions, it revealed microwave controlled rectification and a previously unreported subharmonic response. Together, these results establish a basis for extending charge resolved spectroscopy to junctions with controlled phase, impedance, and island charge, where superconducting coherence, nonequilibrium, and charge fluctuations can be explored within the same experiment.

        Superconducting quantum technologies ultimately depend on controlling how charge and phase respond to electromagnetic fields at individual junctions. By turning these microscopic responses into experimentally distinguishable signatures, the measurements presented here contribute to the understanding needed to make superconducting circuits more controllable, reproducible, and scalable.
