\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}
%=========================================================

    In this thesis, I systematically mapped microwave driven superconducting transport from the tunnel limit to atomic contacts with nearly perfect channel transmission. Across this range, the same experimental strategy connected static junction characterization with complete microwave amplitude maps. This progression resolved conventional photon assisted tunneling with unusually high resolution and established a unified phenomenological framework for photon assisted few-channel transport. Along the way, it revealed two previously unreported responses, a tunable microwave induced rectification and a subharmonic photon assisted incoherent Cooper pair tunneling (PAICPT) response at half the conventional replica spacing.

    Static transport determined the superconducting energy scales and, for atomic contacts, the mesoscopic pincode. Microwave irradiation then introduced a second spectroscopic dimension. The voltage positions of the resulting structures constrained their energy thresholds and transferred charges, while their evolution with drive amplitude distinguished processes that occupied the same voltage range. This combination transformed complex transport maps into a systematic comparison of quasiparticle, Cooper pair, and multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) transport.

    Stable mechanically controllable break junctions and a common measurement and analysis chain made the comparison possible across all regimes. In situ calibration converted the hardware dependent microwave output into the local ac voltage experienced by each junction, so the maps could be compared on the physical drive coordinate of the transport models.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{From Tunnel Barriers to Atomic Contacts}
    %=========================================================

        The first tunnel junction established the high-resolution tunnel reference. The SIS BCS model described its static transport, and the Tien-Gordon model reproduced the complete microwave driven evolution with exceptional agreement. Individual photon assisted replicas remained visually resolved and could be followed continuously to at least order 25. The measured ladder extended substantially beyond the superconducting gap while retaining the predicted redistribution of spectral weight across the full amplitude range. To my knowledge, no previous SIS amplitude map has continuously resolved quasiparticle replicas to a higher order. This result demonstrated quantitative Tien-Gordon transport deep into the multiphoton regime and set the reference against which the more complex junctions could be understood.

        A second, nominally similar tunnel junction revealed the first new phenomenon. I observed microwave induced rectification with a local current asymmetry reaching up to \qty{25}{\percent}. Dc bias, microwave amplitude, and frequency provided three controls over its magnitude and polarity. The response switched on with increasing drive, reacted sensitively to frequency, and reversed direction across the investigated interval. It was nonhysteretic under the investigated conditions and turned the junction into a microwave controlled diode-like element. Comparison with the bias symmetric Tien-Gordon response isolated the additional asymmetric contribution and established a regime beyond uniform ac driving. Static magnetic field maps showed no unexpected features and the transport stayed unchanged across a wide range of gate voltages, supporting a microwave induced origin over a static flux sensitive or Coulomb blockaded state. The narrow drive frequency at which the effect appears also matched an electromagnetic mode of the sample environment identified independently in an earlier transmission measurement of the same cryostat. A resonantly driven Josephson weak link, consistent with the conventional switching current of a comparable junction, provides a plausible explanation for the drive threshold, frequency sensitivity, and polarity reversal.

        Moving from the tunnel regime to atomic contacts introduced discrete channels of finite and nearly perfect transmission, and with them a new question. Static characterization along a complete opening trace showed that the extracted pincodes evolved continuously within each conductance plateau and reorganized at abrupt jumps, revealing contact changes that stayed hidden in the normal-state conductance alone. Once microwave irradiation was added, the same channels supported quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair processes that overlapped in the same voltage range, so the static pincode alone could no longer explain the driven maps. To separate these processes, I decomposed the static MAR response deterministically around its known energy thresholds and applied charge dependent photon dressing to each resulting order. Combined with PAICPT, this framework reproduced the dominant trajectories and their spectral weight evolution across complete amplitude maps, without adjusting parameters to fit individual driven features.

        This description then had to hold up away from the single contact where it was developed. Testing it across contacts with substantially different pincodes, from moderate transmission to a nearly ballistic leading channel, showed that threshold weighted PAMAR captured the dominant finite-bias response throughout this range, while the squared-Bessel amplitude evolution of the replicas originating from zero bias supported their assignment to PAICPT rather than to a coherent Shapiro response. The model therefore did more than reproduce a collection of lines. It assigned overlapping structures consistently to their energy thresholds, effective charge orders, and static transport origins, across contacts that shared little beyond the same measurement and analysis chain.

        At the highest conductance investigated, a second new phenomenon emerged, a subharmonic PAICPT response that, to my knowledge, has not been reported previously. In this contact, the microwave maps contained trajectories running halfway between the conventional Cooper pair replicas, at half their spacing in voltage, positions that no combination of the established single-pair replicas can reach. A second, distinct replica family at half the conventional spacing is therefore required to describe them. Within the phenomenological construction, this spacing matches the effective transfer of two Cooper pairs, placing the response one step beyond the incoherent single-pair transport that describes the other contacts. Their squared-Bessel-like amplitude evolution is consistent with this assignment, but does not by itself establish a simultaneous, correlated two-pair transfer or identify the microscopic process behind it. The replica pattern itself is the experimental result. It marks the point where the controlled few-channel description reaches its limit, opening a new region in which higher charge correlations, phase dynamics, and the electromagnetic environment can be investigated together.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{From Microwave Response to Transport Processes}
    %=========================================================

        The broader contribution of these measurements is a practical route from microwave maps to the underlying transport processes. Feature spacing alone is insufficient when quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair structures overlap. Static transport supplies the channel composition and energy thresholds, microwave spacing determines the transferred charge, and amplitude evolution distinguishes incoherent tunneling from coherent phase locking. Together, these coordinates separate coexisting processes by their static origin, charge, and coherence without selecting isolated features from the driven data.

        This perspective also clarifies when a macroscopic junction description is sufficient and when microscopic charge processes must be resolved. In the tunnel reference, a single driven voltage and the static \textit{I--V} characteristic captured the response over an exceptionally wide range. At finite transmission, the individual channels and voltage dependent MAR charge orders became essential. The electromagnetic environment added another active degree of freedom by setting the local drive, enabling incoherent Cooper pair transfer, and coupling phase dynamics back into quasiparticle transport. The rectification in the second tunnel junction and the subharmonic response in the highest-conductance atomic contact appeared precisely where these additional degrees of freedom became visible. They therefore extend the systematic progression rather than standing as unrelated anomalies.

        The distinction between circuit parameters and microscopic transport has direct implications for superconducting quantum circuits. Their Josephson junctions are commonly represented by a few circuit quantities, although dissipation and microwave response originate from charge transport within a local electromagnetic environment. The measurements here show that this environment can set the junction drive, redistribute quasiparticle and Cooper pair transport, and generate frequency dependent responses absent from static characterization. Dedicated test structures based on the same spectroscopy could probe microwave delivery, resonances of the sample holder and wiring, subgap transport, and unintended high-transmission channels. They would not measure qubit coherence directly, but could reveal how junction fabrication and the surrounding microwave circuit influence microscopic dissipative processes that limit circuit reproducibility.

        The underlying approach is not restricted to superconducting qubits or metallic atomic contacts. Hybrid nanowires, quantum dots, molecular junctions, superconducting detectors, and driven single-charge devices all support regimes in which several charge transfer processes coexist under periodic drive. Applying the same strategy there requires only that static transport independently fix the relevant energy thresholds, as the pincode does here, so that the microwave spacing and amplitude evolution can be compared against a prediction rather than fitted freely. Where that condition holds, static energy thresholds, replica spacing, and amplitude evolution provide complementary information about process origin, transferred charge, and coherence, and their combination can serve as a general strategy for organizing complex driven responses rather than as a model tied to one device class. Where it does not, for example when the relevant thresholds are not independently accessible or several channels remain unresolved, the same three coordinates cannot separate coexisting processes, and a different characterization step would have to substitute for the pincode used here.

        The framework remains phenomenological, but independently characterized static transport constrains its physical assignments. It bridges the transparent Tien-Gordon limit and a microscopic nonequilibrium treatment, providing a benchmark for determining which features require coherence, occupation dynamics, or environmental backaction.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Future Research}
    %=========================================================

        The rectification found in the second tunnel junction motivates a dedicated follow-up rather than a side test. A deliberately fabricated series of tunnel junctions with systematically varied asymmetry, for example in barrier thickness, oxidation, or electrode geometry, would turn the accidental asymmetry of this sample into a controlled parameter and map how the drive threshold, frequency sensitivity, and polarity reversal scale with it. Combined with the frequency and amplitude sweeps already established here, this would test whether the effect scales as expected for a resonantly driven Josephson weak link, and could turn a favorable asymmetry into a designed microwave rectifier rather than an observed one. This step needs no new measurement technique and only variations on the existing sample fabrication, making it a natural first project on its own.

        The next step is to embed the junction in a deliberately designed electromagnetic environment. A calibrated impedance, resonator, or superconducting loop would turn microwave amplitude, phase, and mode structure into controlled variables, extending the asymmetry series above with an independent check of the resonantly driven weak link picture. The same control addresses a broader question in the atomic contacts, separating incoherent Cooper pair transfer from Josephson phase locking by making the environment an experimental variable rather than a fixed backdrop. A tunable weak link embedded in a resonator could additionally correlate charge transfer and photon exchange with resonator loss, quasiparticle dynamics, or qubit relaxation, so that the environment becomes a controlled quantum degree of freedom rather than an uncontrolled boundary condition.

        The subharmonic response deserves the same systematic treatment as the rest of the few-channel regime, and reaches further into open theoretical territory. A series of highly transmissive contacts, tuned deliberately into and across the conductance range where the subharmonic replicas appear, would establish how the effect depends on transmission, channel number, and drive amplitude, rather than resting on the single contact where it was observed here. Finite-frequency current noise would add an independent, charge-sensitive probe alongside the amplitude maps, since a genuinely correlated two-pair transfer and two uncorrelated single-pair events at the same average current would leave distinct signatures in the noise. Microscopic Floquet or Keldysh calculations can build directly on the extracted pincodes, calibrated drives, and charge order assignments to determine which structures follow from charge quantization and static thresholds and which require coherent interference, nonequilibrium occupations, or environmental backaction, settling in particular whether the subharmonic response reflects higher charge correlations or a more conventional process appearing at an unexpected spacing.

        A superconducting single-electron transistor provides the natural next platform beyond this. It adds charging energy and gate induced charge to the controls established here. The connection between MAR charge order and energy threshold offers a way to organize its dense stability diagrams. Gate--bias--amplitude maps could follow how a process transferring multiple charges crosses a Coulomb threshold, exchanges photons, and changes the island charge, while phase control distinguishes coherent Josephson cycles from incoherent quasiparticle and MAR sequences. This extends the present framework toward the joint study of superconducting coherence, nonequilibrium, and charge fluctuations.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Final Remarks}
    %=========================================================

        Systematic microwave amplitude spectroscopy connects superconducting transport across widely different transmission regimes. It resolves the Tien-Gordon response with exceptional dynamic range, relates finite-transmission structures to their energy thresholds and effective charges, and reveals microwave controlled rectification and a subharmonic response at the boundaries of these descriptions. These results establish a basis for experiments with controlled phase, impedance, and island charge.

        Superconducting quantum technologies ultimately depend on controlling how charge and phase respond to electromagnetic fields at individual junctions. By turning these microscopic responses into experimentally distinguishable signatures, the measurements presented here contribute to the understanding needed to make superconducting circuits more controllable, reproducible, and scalable.
