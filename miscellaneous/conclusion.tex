%=========================================================
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}
%=========================================================

    In this thesis, I systematically mapped microwave driven superconducting transport from the tunnel limit to atomic contacts with nearly perfect channel transmission. Across this range, the same experimental strategy connected static junction characterization with complete microwave amplitude maps. This progression resolved conventional photon assisted tunneling with unusually high resolution, revealed two previously unreported responses, and established a unified phenomenological framework for photon assisted few-channel transport.

    Static transport determined the superconducting energy scales and, for atomic contacts, the mesoscopic pincode. Microwave irradiation then introduced a second spectroscopic dimension. The voltage positions of the resulting structures constrained their energy thresholds and transferred charges, while their evolution with drive amplitude distinguished processes that occupied the same voltage range. This combination transformed complex transport maps into a systematic comparison of quasiparticle, Cooper pair, and multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) transport.

    Stable mechanically controllable break junctions and a common measurement and analysis chain made the comparison possible across all regimes. In situ calibration converted the hardware dependent microwave output into the local ac voltage experienced by each junction, so the maps could be compared on the physical drive coordinate of the transport models.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{From Tunnel Barriers to Atomic Contacts}
    %=========================================================

        TB1 established the high-resolution tunnel reference. The SIS BCS model described its static transport, and the Tien-Gordon model reproduced the complete microwave driven evolution with exceptional agreement. Individual photon assisted replicas remained visually resolved and could be followed continuously to at least order 25. The measured ladder extended substantially beyond the superconducting gap while retaining the predicted redistribution of spectral weight across the full amplitude range. To my knowledge, no previous SIS amplitude map has continuously resolved quasiparticle replicas to a higher order. This result demonstrated quantitative Tien-Gordon transport deep into the multiphoton regime and set the reference against which the more complex junctions could be understood.

        TB2 revealed the first new phenomenon. I observed microwave induced rectification with a local current asymmetry reaching up to \qty{25}{\percent}. Dc bias, microwave amplitude, and frequency provided three controls over its magnitude and polarity. The response switched on with increasing drive, reacted sensitively to frequency, and reversed direction across the investigated interval. It was nonhysteretic under the investigated conditions and turned the junction into a microwave controlled diode-like element. Comparison with the bias symmetric Tien-Gordon response isolated the additional asymmetric contribution and established a regime beyond uniform ac driving. A resonantly driven Josephson weak link provides a plausible and experimentally testable explanation for the drive threshold, frequency sensitivity, and polarity reversal.

        The atomic contacts extended the experiment from the tunnel regime to discrete channels of finite and nearly perfect transmission. Along a complete opening trace, the extracted pincodes evolved continuously within conductance plateaus and reorganized at abrupt jumps, revealing contact changes that remained hidden in the normal-state conductance. Under microwave irradiation, the same channels supported overlapping quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair processes. I connected these structures through a deterministic decomposition of the static MAR response around its known energy thresholds, followed by charge dependent photon dressing. Combined with photon assisted incoherent Cooper pair tunneling, this framework reproduced the dominant trajectories and their spectral weight evolution across complete amplitude maps without adjustment to individual driven features.

        Contacts with substantially different pincodes tested this description from moderate transmission to a nearly ballistic leading channel. Threshold weighted PAMAR captured the dominant finite-bias response throughout this range. The squared-Bessel amplitude evolution of the replicas originating from zero bias supported their assignment to PAICPT rather than a coherent Shapiro response. The model therefore did more than reproduce a collection of lines. It assigned overlapping structures consistently to their energy thresholds, effective charge orders, and static transport origins.

        At high conductance, a second new phenomenon emerged. The microwave maps of C3 contained trajectories halfway between the conventional Cooper pair replicas. Their positions and squared-Bessel-like amplitude evolution were consistent with an additional subharmonic component at half the conventional replica spacing. Within the phenomenological construction, this spacing has the same voltage scaling as the effective transfer of two Cooper pairs. It does not, however, establish their simultaneous correlated transfer or determine the microscopic process. The replica pattern itself is an experimental result. To my knowledge, such a subharmonic PAICPT response has not been reported previously. The transition from the controlled few-channel regime to this response marks a new region in which higher charge correlations, phase dynamics, and the electromagnetic environment can be investigated together.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{From Microwave Response to Transport Processes}
    %=========================================================

        The broader contribution of these measurements is a practical route from microwave maps to the underlying transport processes. Feature spacing alone is insufficient when quasiparticle, MAR, and Cooper pair structures overlap. Static transport supplies the channel composition and energy thresholds, microwave spacing determines the transferred charge, and amplitude evolution distinguishes incoherent tunneling from coherent phase locking. Together, these coordinates separate coexisting processes by their static origin, charge, and coherence without selecting isolated features from the driven data.

        This perspective also clarifies when a macroscopic junction description is sufficient and when microscopic charge processes must be resolved. In the tunnel reference, a single driven voltage and the static \textit{I--V} characteristic captured the response over an exceptionally wide range. At finite transmission, the individual channels and voltage dependent MAR charge orders became essential. The electromagnetic environment added another active degree of freedom by setting the local drive, enabling incoherent Cooper pair transfer, and coupling phase dynamics back into quasiparticle transport. The rectification in TB2 and the subharmonic response in C3 appeared precisely where these additional degrees of freedom became visible. They therefore extend the systematic progression rather than standing as unrelated anomalies.

        The distinction between circuit parameters and microscopic transport has direct implications for superconducting quantum circuits. Their Josephson junctions are commonly represented by a few circuit quantities, although dissipation and microwave response originate from charge transport within a local electromagnetic environment. The measurements here show that this environment can set the junction drive, redistribute quasiparticle and Cooper pair transport, and generate frequency dependent responses absent from static characterization. Dedicated test structures based on the same spectroscopy could probe microwave delivery, resonances of the sample holder and wiring, subgap transport, and unintended high-transmission channels. They would not measure qubit coherence directly, but could reveal how junction fabrication and the surrounding microwave circuit influence microscopic dissipative processes that limit circuit reproducibility.

        The underlying approach is not restricted to superconducting qubits or metallic atomic contacts. Hybrid nanowires, quantum dots, molecular junctions, superconducting detectors, and driven single-charge devices all support regimes in which several charge transfer processes coexist under periodic drive. Across these systems, static energy thresholds, replica spacing, and amplitude evolution provide complementary information about process origin, transferred charge, and coherence. Their combination can therefore serve as a general strategy for organizing complex driven responses rather than as a model tied to one device class.

        The framework remains phenomenological, but independently characterized static transport constrains its physical assignments. It bridges the transparent Tien-Gordon limit and a microscopic nonequilibrium treatment, providing a benchmark for determining which features require coherence, occupation dynamics, or environmental backaction.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Future Research}
    %=========================================================

        The next experimental step is to embed the junction in a deliberately designed electromagnetic environment. A calibrated impedance, resonator, or superconducting loop would turn microwave amplitude, phase, and mode structure into controlled variables. This could test the proposed origin of the TB2 rectification and separate incoherent Cooper pair transfer from Josephson phase locking in atomic contacts. A tunable weak link embedded in a resonator could additionally correlate charge transfer and photon exchange with resonator loss, quasiparticle dynamics, or qubit relaxation. The environment would then become a controlled quantum degree of freedom rather than an uncontrolled boundary condition.

        Microscopic Floquet or Keldysh calculations can build directly on the extracted pincodes, calibrated drives, and charge order assignments. They could determine which structures follow from charge quantization and static thresholds, and which require coherent interference, nonequilibrium occupations, or environmental backaction. Finite-frequency current noise would add an independent probe of transferred charge and photon exchange, particularly in the subharmonic regime.

        A superconducting single-electron transistor provides the natural next platform. It adds charging energy and gate induced charge to the controls established here. The connection between MAR charge order and energy threshold offers a way to organize its dense stability diagrams. Gate--bias--amplitude maps could follow how a process transferring multiple charges crosses a Coulomb threshold, exchanges photons, and changes the island charge, while phase control distinguishes coherent Josephson cycles from incoherent quasiparticle and MAR sequences. This extends the present framework toward the joint study of superconducting coherence, nonequilibrium, and charge fluctuations.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Final Remarks}
    %=========================================================

        Systematic microwave amplitude spectroscopy connected superconducting transport across widely different transmission regimes. It resolved the Tien-Gordon response with exceptional dynamic range, related finite-transmission structures to their energy thresholds and effective charges, and revealed microwave controlled rectification and a subharmonic response at the boundaries of these descriptions. These results establish a basis for experiments with controlled phase, impedance, and island charge.

        Superconducting quantum technologies ultimately depend on controlling how charge and phase respond to electromagnetic fields at individual junctions. By turning these microscopic responses into experimentally distinguishable signatures, the measurements presented here contribute to the understanding needed to make superconducting circuits more controllable, reproducible, and scalable.
