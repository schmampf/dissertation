    The measurements in this section are an earlier attempt to test whether microwave driven quasiparticle transport can be reproduced in an Al TB. I measure the similar device TB2 with an earlier version of the setup, before performing the optimized measurements on TB1 discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:tb:highres}. Although the spectra of TB2 are less sharply resolved, they show reproducible features that depart from the expected bias symmetric SIS response and motivate the analysis presented here.

    TB2 also provides a useful comparison because its fabrication differs from TB1 in several identifiable ways. The junction finger of TB2 is shifted to the left compared to TB1. This shift reduces the unsupported length of the evaporation mask after repeated mask collapse during evaporation. The aluminum layers are slightly thinner, and the oxidation pressure and duration differ moderately. Most importantly, the shadow electrode near the break junction remains connected because the evaporation mask has more undercut. I nevertheless expect the junction itself to remain in a similar tunnel regime.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Static Transport and Reference Parameters}
    \label{subsec:tb:asym:static}
    %=========================================================

        The static transport response initially supports this expectation. TB2 shows a clear quasiparticle onset and no resolved supercurrent, and its \textit{I--V} curve can be described by the same SIS model used for TB1. Under microwave irradiation, however, the transport becomes strongly bias asymmetric within a narrow frequency range. The purpose of this section is therefore to characterize this unexpected rectification, establish its dependence on microwave amplitude and frequency, and test whether it correlates with microwave coupling or sample heating. The available measurements limit possible explanations but do not identify a unique microscopic mechanism.

        I use the representative \textit{I--V} curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:asym:irradiation:single-iv} to determine the static junction parameters $G_\mathrm{N}$, $\Delta_0$, $\gamma$, and $\sigma_V$. The fit also returns the trace dependent fitted temperature $T_\mathrm{fit}$ and effective microwave amplitude $A_\mathrm{fit}$ for this curve. The corresponding values and measurement conditions are listed in Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:asym}. I do not apply the additional offset-correction algorithm discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis} to these data. This algorithm assumes a symmetric \textit{I--V} response, which is not the case for this measurement.

        Compared with TB1, TB2 has approximately half the normal-state conductance, while its fitted superconducting gap remains similar. The representative trace has a higher $T_\mathrm{fit}$ and shows stronger spectral rounding, reflected in the larger fitted values of $\gamma$ and $\sigma_V$. I attribute the reduced spectral resolution primarily to the non-optimized measurement setup and its less effective filtering. The fit alone, however, cannot distinguish instrumental broadening from intrinsic broadening of the junction.

        I perform these measurements with the setup described by Patrick Raif and Martin Prestel, with several changes to the low-temperature wiring \cite{raif_electronic_2024,prestel_magnetic_2020}. Instead of the commercial thermalizer and filter, I use a sequence of copper-powder filters and stainless steel capillaries. The reference resistor is already located at low temperature, in contrast to the configuration used by Martin Prestel. Microwave irradiation is again applied through the antenna.

        For the microwave amplitude sweeps, I hold the static junction parameters $G_\mathrm{N}$, $\Delta_0$, $\gamma$, and $\sigma_V$ fixed at the values obtained from the representative fit. I hold $\nu$ at the nominal applied frequency. I then allow $T_\mathrm{fit}$ and $A_\mathrm{fit}$ to vary for each irradiated trace. This procedure follows the analysis of TB1 and provides a common reference for comparing the different drive frequencies. Unlike TB1, however, TB2 develops a pronounced bias asymmetry under microwave irradiation. The symmetric SIS plus Tien-Gordon model cannot reproduce this response, so the extracted $T_\mathrm{fit}$ and $A_\mathrm{fit}$ should be interpreted as effective comparison parameters rather than as a complete physical description of the asymmetric curves.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Motivating Asymmetric Response}
    \label{subsec:tb:asym:representative}
    %=========================================================

        The deviation from the symmetric SIS response becomes pronounced at selected frequencies and sufficiently large microwave amplitudes. Figure~\ref{fig:tb:asym:irradiation:single-asym} shows a representative trace at $\nu=\qty{7.97}{\giga\hertz}$. To compare the two bias polarities, I reflect the negative-bias branch about the origin and plot it together with the positive-bias branch. The two branches differ substantially in the quasiparticle onset region and remain separated at larger bias. The corresponding \textit{dI--dV} and \textit{dV--dI} curves show that the asymmetry is associated with a redistribution and displacement of the spectral features rather than with a constant current or voltage offset.

        I record the same trace with increasing and decreasing bias. The two sweep directions overlap within the experimental resolution. The asymmetry is therefore reproducible and does not originate from sweep direction hysteresis on the measurement timescale of \qty{3}{\minute}.

        To quantify the effect locally, I define normalized bias asymmetry parameters for the three transport representations,
        \begin{align}
            \chi_I(V) &= \frac{I(V)+I(-V)}{\left|I(V)-I(-V)\right|}\,,\\
            \chi_{\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V}(V) &= \frac{\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(V) - \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(-V)}{\left|\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(V) + \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(-V) \right|}\,,\\
            \chi_{\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}I}(I) &= \frac{\tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(I) - \tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(-I)}{\left|\tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(I) + \tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(-I) \right|}\,.
            \label{eq:tb:asym:rect-chi}
        \end{align}
        All three quantities vanish for an ideally bias symmetric transport response. Their signs indicate which bias branch has the larger response, while their magnitudes quantify the relative difference between the branches. Because the normalization can become small, particularly in the derivative representations, I use $\chi$ primarily to identify the bias regions in which the symmetry breaking occurs other than as a globally averaged measure.

        For the representative trace in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:asym:irradiation:single-asym}, the local current asymmetry reaches $\chi_I=0.254$. The largest values occur in the voltage range where the positive- and negative-bias quasiparticle onsets evolve differently. This observation shows that the microwave response cannot be described by a single bias-symmetric SIS plus Tien-Gordon curve. The full amplitude sweep at this frequency is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:asym:nu_7.97GHz:cal}, in the Appendix.

        This trace creates a calibration problem. For TB1, each irradiated curve can be fitted with the symmetric SIS plus Tien-Gordon model and remapped using its individual $A_\mathrm{fit}$. When the rectification in TB2 becomes large, however, the measured response violates the bias symmetry assumed by this model. The resulting $A_\mathrm{fit}$ is only then a compromise between two differently evolving bias branches and no longer represents the physical microwave amplitude. A pointwise remapping based on these values would therefore imprint the failure of the symmetric model onto the calibrated amplitude axis.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Effective Microwave Amplitude Calibration}
    \label{subsec:tb:asym:amplitude-calibration}
    %=========================================================

        I nevertheless fit every complete \textit{I--V} curve with the symmetric SIS plus Tien-Gordon model and extract $A_\mathrm{fit}$ and $T_\mathrm{fit}$. For asymmetric curves, these values are effective fit parameters because the model cannot reproduce both bias branches simultaneously. I therefore do not use the individual $A_\mathrm{fit}$ values as the remapped amplitude coordinate.

        Instead, I determine one effective coupling coefficient for each frequency. For every nonzero source amplitude in a frequency sweep, I evaluate $A_\mathrm{fit}/A_\mathrm{out}$. All ratios are included without rejection based on fit quality or asymmetry. I define the frequency dependent coupling and its relation as
        \begin{align}
            \eta(\nu)=\operatorname*{median}_{A_\mathrm{out}>0}\!\left(\frac{A_\mathrm{fit}}{A_\mathrm{out}}\right)\,, &&
            A_\mathrm{cal}=\eta(\nu)A_\mathrm{out}\,.
            \label{eq:tb:asym:amplitude-coupling}
        \end{align}
        The median reduces the influence of scattered fits in the asymmetric region, while the symmetric parts of each sweep provide the amplitude scale. This procedure yields a common effective comparison axis but does not constitute an independent measurement of the ac voltage across the junction.

        Frequencies with weaker coupling do not reach the full displayed $A_\mathrm{cal}$ range. With this effective amplitude coordinate established, I now compare how the measured transport evolves across the investigated frequency interval.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Frequency Dependent Transport Response}
    \label{subsec:tb:asym:transport}
    %=========================================================

        The representative trace establishes the presence of microwave induced rectification at one operating point. To determine whether the effect is isolated or evolves systematically, I measure microwave amplitude sweeps from $7.75$ to $\qty{8.05}{\giga\hertz}$ in steps of $\qty{50}{\mega\hertz}$. Figure~\ref{fig:tb:asym:comparison-maps} compares the resulting \textit{dI--dV} and \textit{dV--dI} landscapes at six selected frequencies. The corresponding branch resolved line cuts and the complete \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} frequency comparisons are provided in Appendix~\ref{appendix:tb2}.

        The \textit{dI--dV} maps provide the clearest view of the spectral evolution. At the lowest and highest frequencies, the two quasiparticle onset ridges remain approximately symmetric. At intermediate frequencies, one ridge is suppressed and displaced relative to the other, and the direction of this deformation reverses across the investigated frequency interval. Near the reversal, an additional conductance feature appears between the two main ridges. The \textit{I--V} and \textit{dV--dI} representations show the same systematic amplitude and frequency dependence, although the narrow resistance features make the latter more sensitive to noise.

        The calibrated landscapes establish that the symmetry breaking evolves systematically with both microwave amplitude and frequency. The symmetric calculation reproduces the overall photon assisted redistribution of spectral weight but not the unequal evolution of the two bias branches. I next quantify this difference over the complete data set.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Rectification Analysis}
    \label{subsec:tb:asym:rectification}
    %=========================================================

        The local quantity $\chi$ identifies the bias regions in which the representative trace becomes asymmetric. To compare all measured curves without selecting a particular bias point, I assign each transport curve a signed integrated asymmetry $\mathcal{A}$,
        \begin{align}
            \mathcal{A}_I &= \frac{\int \left[I(V)+I(-V)\right]\mathrm{d}V}{\int \left|I(V)-I(-V)\right|\mathrm{d}V}\,, \\
            \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V} &= \frac{\int \left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(V) - \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(-V) \right)\mathrm{d}V}{\int \left|\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(V) + \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(-V) \right|\mathrm{d}V} \,,\\
            \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}I} &= \frac{\int \left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(I) - \tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(-I) \right)\mathrm{d}I}{\int \left|\tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(I) + \tfrac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}I}(-I) \right|\mathrm{d}I}\,.
            \label{eq:tb:asym:rect-A}
        \end{align}
        The integrations use the common symmetric bias interval available for the two reflected branches. In contrast to the local quantity $\chi$, $\mathcal{A}$ summarizes the signed asymmetry of an entire curve. Positive and negative contributions can therefore cancel.

        Figure~\ref{fig:tb:asym:rectification-map} shows $\mathcal{A}$ as a function of frequency and calibrated amplitude. The magnitude of the \textit{I--V} asymmetry generally increases with amplitude and changes sign across the investigated frequency interval. The \textit{dI--dV} asymmetry follows the same broad frequency dependence, while the \textit{dV--dI} asymmetry has the opposite sign. The derivative measures are noisy at low amplitude because their normalization becomes small, but the large-amplitude structure is consistent across the three transport representations.

        To compare the frequency dependence on a single axis, I average each asymmetry measure over the same calibrated-amplitude interval. I evaluate the average by integration rather than by an arithmetic mean to account for the uneven spacing of the $A_\mathrm{cal}$ points,
        \begin{equation}
            \left\langle\mathcal{A}_X\right\rangle_{A_\mathrm{cal}}=\frac{1}{A_\mathrm{cal,max}-A_\mathrm{cal,min}}\int_{A_\mathrm{cal,min}}^{A_\mathrm{cal,max}}\mathcal{A}_X(A_\mathrm{cal})\,\mathrm{d}A_\mathrm{cal}\,,
            \label{eq:tb:asym:amplitude-averaged-rectification}
        \end{equation}
        where $X$ denotes $I$, $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}V$, or $\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}I$. Panels (a)\,-\,(c) of Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:asym:frequency} show the resulting averages.

        This frequency and amplitude dependent map is the central experimental characterization of the rectification effect. It shows a drive threshold, an increase in magnitude with amplitude, and a sign reversal within a narrow frequency interval, consistently across all three transport representations. The next section tests whether this frequency dependence traces back to microwave coupling or to sample heating.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Interpretation and Model Limits}
    \label{subsec:tb:asym:interpretation}
    %=========================================================

        The measurements establish a reproducible microwave induced breaking of bias symmetry in TB2. At the representative operating point, increasing and decreasing bias sweeps overlap within the experimental resolution. The diode like response is therefore single valued and nonhysteretic on the measurement timescale. Its defining features are an onset at elevated drive, confinement to a narrow frequency interval, and a reversal of direction within that interval.

        The static control measurements show conventional superconducting behavior. Magnetic field dependent maps contain no unexpected features, and the transport remains unchanged over a wide range of dc gate voltages. Together with the low-impedance connection through the intact break junction, these observations support treating the asymmetry as a microwave induced response rather than a property of a static flux sensitive or Coulomb blockaded state.

        The narrow frequency interval points to resonant coupling through the microwave environment. In an earlier transmission experiment in the same cryostat, I observed a feature-rich transfer function and associated prominent features with different parts of the setup. The dominant resonance was consistent with the radius of the semicylindrical shielding cup used at the time \cite{irtenkauf_broadband_2021}. The present cylindrical cup has a diameter of $\qty{38}{\milli\meter}$, close to the free-space wavelength $\lambda_0=c/\nu\approx\qty{37.8}{\milli\meter}$ at $\qty{7.93}{\giga\hertz}$. This correspondence supports an electromagnetic mode of the sample environment as the origin of the frequency selectivity.

        Such a mode couples to the local circuit formed by the island, shadow electrode, TB counterelectrode, and BJ lead. The sample lies near the center of the cup, where a field node or another symmetry point can produce a strong spatial variation of the field. A displacement from this point, together with unequal lead impedances and electrical path lengths, can generate different microwave amplitudes and phases at the electrodes. Resonant enhancement then provides a natural origin for the drive threshold, while the phase evolution across the resonance can account for the reversal of the rectification direction.

        The intact BJ provides a phase sensitive element in this circuit. A similar constriction in sample AC exhibits a conventional Josephson response in its pristine state, including a finite switching current (Sec.~\ref{sec:appendix:ac:unbroken}). I therefore treat the BJ in TB2 as a Josephson weak link. Under irradiation, phase locking can generate an inverse ac Josephson response and Shapiro structure \cite{shapiro_josephson_1963}. Near $\qty{7.8}{\giga\hertz}$, the integer Shapiro spacing is $h\nu/(2e)\approx\qty{16}{\micro\volt}$, below the fitted voltage broadening $\sigma_V\approx\qty{41}{\micro\volt}$ of TB2. The absence of resolved steps is therefore consistent with the experimental resolution.

        In this interpretation, the TB detects the influence of the driven weak link on photon assisted quasiparticle transport. A phase locked voltage contribution across the BJ changes the island potential and redistributes the PAT features of the combined device. This resembles Giaever's detection of the ac Josephson effect with a second tunnel junction, although the topology and coupling differ here \cite{giaever_detection_1965}. Unequal relaxation paths in the island and counterelectrode may additionally produce different quasiparticle distributions, while heating can broaden these distributions and enhance the available spectral weight. The observed heating correlation is consistent with this supporting role.

        The measurements thus support a working sequence in which a resonance redistributes the local microwave amplitude and phase, the BJ contributes a phase dependent island potential, and the TB converts this response into bias asymmetric quasiparticle transport. This sequence accounts qualitatively for the threshold, frequency selectivity, and sign reversal. Its microscopic details remain open because the experiment does not independently resolve the microwave fields, the voltage across the BJ, or the electrode distributions.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Significance and Outlook}
    %=========================================================

        The central result is a microwave controlled rectification effect whose magnitude and polarity are tuned jointly by dc bias, microwave amplitude, and frequency. The response reaches a local current asymmetry of \qty{25}{\percent}, appears above a drive threshold, and reverses direction within a narrow frequency interval. It therefore represents a distinct transport regime beyond the bias symmetric Tien-Gordon model. To my knowledge, this combination of amplitude controlled onset and frequency controlled polarity reversal has not previously been reported for an SIS tunnel junction.

        The response also demonstrates that the electromagnetic environment can become an active part of quasiparticle transport. The measurements are consistent with resonant microwave coupling to a local circuit containing the intact Josephson weak link and the tunnel barrier. A deliberately designed resonator or impedance environment would provide the most direct test of this interpretation. Such an experiment could determine whether the observed rectification can be reproduced and controlled as a junction property rather than as a sample specific response.

        If reproducible, the frequency sensitive sign reversal could provide a basis for a microwave controlled superconducting diode or a local probe of resonant microwave coupling. Establishing such functionality would require calibrated delivered power, zero-bias current or open-circuit voltage measurements, noise characterization, and measurements of nominally identical devices. The present experiment establishes the tunable rectification and identifies a testable physical route toward these applications.
