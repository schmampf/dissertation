        This section starts with the simplest microwave-driven superconducting junction available in this thesis. The goal is to understand one device as completely as possible before moving to junctions where additional mechanisms complicate the interpretation. TB1 is an SIS tunnel barrier without an observable supercurrent. Its static spectra are therefore expected to be described by quasiparticle tunneling in BCS electrodes, and its microwave response should follow photon-assisted tunneling as introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}.

        I used this device as a reference system for three connected questions. First, I determined how well the unirradiated \textit{I--V} curve constrains the tunnel-junction parameters. Second, I used controlled heating to calibrate the temperature axis and to introduce the transport-landscape visualization used throughout the chapter. Third, I drove the same junction with microwaves and used the calibrated amplitude axis to test how far photon-assisted sidebands can be resolved. The high-amplitude data show sidebands up to very high photon order and therefore provide a direct benchmark for the spectral resolution and microwave coupling of the setup.

        The sample TB1\footnote{In the lab book TB1 is called `OI-25c-09'.} was fabricated as described in Sec.~\ref{section:sample-preparation}. Irradiation experiments were performed with the antenna in the setup described in Sec.~\ref{sec:methods:setup}. An SEM image of the sample is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:image-TB1}. All measurements discussed here were taken with the break junction unbroken. 
        
        A typical \textit{I--V} curve is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:single-iv}. The quasiparticle onset appears at approximately $2\Delta/e$, as expected for an SIS junction. I do not observe a supercurrent, even though both electrodes are superconducting. This does not imply that the Josephson coupling is exactly zero. Using the Ambegaokar--Baratoff estimate with $\Delta=195\,\mu\mathrm{eV}$ and $R_\mathrm{N}=68.1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega$ gives
        \begin{align}
            I_\mathrm{c}R_\mathrm{N} &\approx \frac{\pi\Delta}{2e} \approx 306\,\mu\mathrm{V}\,, &
            I_\mathrm{c} &\approx 4.5\,\mathrm{nA}\,, &
            \frac{E_\mathrm{J}}{k_\mathrm{B}} &= \frac{\hbar I_\mathrm{c}}{2e k_\mathrm{B}} \approx 0.11\,\mathrm{K}\,.
            \label{eq:tb:highres:josephson-estimate}
        \end{align}

        This Josephson energy is comparable to or smaller than the effective electronic temperature extracted below. It is also small compared with plausible charging-energy scales of the barrier region. The capacitance of TB1 was not measured independently, but the lithographic overlap is of order $50\times100\,\mathrm{nm^2}$. A parallel-plate estimate with an aluminum-oxide thickness of $1{-}2\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\epsilon_\mathrm{r}\approx8{-}10$ gives $C_\mathrm{TB}\sim0.2{-}0.4\,\mathrm{fF}$, corresponding to $E_\mathrm{c}/k_\mathrm{B}=e^2/(2Ck_\mathrm{B})\sim2{-}5\,\mathrm{K}$. Related MCBJ-SET devices give comparable scales: Sprenger extracted individual junction capacitances of order $100\,\mathrm{aF}$, while Sobral Rey found total island capacitances of order $1\,\mathrm{fF}$ from Coulomb-diamond analyses. The Lorenz--Sprenger--Scheer MCBJ-SET geometry used an oxide-barrier overlap of roughly $50\times50\,\mathrm{nm^2}$ and provides the same order-of-magnitude reference for the tunnel-barrier area \cite{sprenger_herstellung_2016,lorenz_sset_mar_2018,sobral-rey_interplay_2022,sobral_rey_hybrid_sset_2024}. Even allowing for stray capacitance that increases the effective capacitance to several femtofarads, the charging scale remains comparable to or larger than $E_\mathrm{J}$. The unengineered break-junction microwave environment can therefore wash out the dc phase relation through thermal, charging, voltage-noise, and environmental phase fluctuations. The electrodes remain superconducting, but the phase difference across the barrier is not stiff enough to produce a resolvable zero-voltage branch \cite{ambegaokar_tunneling_1963,tinkham_introduction_2004,grabert_single_2013}.

        This absence is important for the interpretation below because it removes Shapiro steps and coherent Cooper-pair tunneling from the expected microwave response. The microwave data can therefore be treated primarily as photon-assisted quasiparticle tunneling.

        The lack of a supercurrent does not prevent us from learning about the junction. The quasiparticle branch still gives access to the superconducting gap, the effective electronic temperature, the normal-state conductance, and the lifetime broadening. Together with the temperature and field studies discussed in the appendix, these measurements establish the basic superconducting properties of the electrodes.
        
        Assuming symmetric electrodes and negligible magnetic field, I obtained the parameters listed in Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres} for the datasets used in this section.

        The BCS/Dynes fit captures the gap position and the overall line shape, but it does not reproduce the full height of the coherence peaks. The \textit{dI--dV} curve also shows small systematic deviations near the gap edge.
        
        Fits without voltage noise produced artifacts around $\Delta/e$. In those fits the Dynes parameter had to account for too much gap-edge rounding, which increased the modeled subgap current around $\Delta/e$ beyond the measured value.

        I therefore use both finite quasiparticle lifetime and voltage broadening as phenomenological broadening channels. The finite lifetime rounds the coherence peaks, while the voltage broadening accounts for residual bias noise and averaging during the voltage sweep.

        The electronic temperature is difficult to estimate from an SIS junction far below the critical temperature because the spectrum changes only weakly in that regime. Although the base-temperature fit gives values as low as $214\,\mathrm{mK}$, I treat this number as an effective transport temperature rather than an absolute thermometer reading.

        The next subsection therefore uses a controlled temperature sweep to test this effective-temperature interpretation, determine the critical temperature, and establish the visualization format used for the later microwave-amplitude maps.

        \subsection{Temperature Calibration}
        \label{subsec:tb:highres:temperature-calibration}
        %=========================================================

            \singlefitfigure{tunnelbarrier/highres/temperature/single-fit}{
                Tbath = 0.697499659340659 K
            }{fig:tb:highres:temperature:single-iv}

            The sample holder was heated locally while a thermometer mounted next to the sample recorded the bath temperature $T_\mathrm{bath}$. For each temperature point, I fitted the measured \textit{I--V} curve with the SIS BCS/Dynes model. I distinguish this thermometer temperature from the temperature parameter $T_\mathrm{fit}$ obtained from the transport fit. The former is measured at the sample holder, while the latter describes the thermal broadening required by the model. I therefore interpret $T_\mathrm{fit}$ as an effective electronic transport temperature, not as an independent measurement of the lattice temperature of the sample.
            All parameters but the temperature are held fixed during this calibration.

            Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:fit} compares $T_\mathrm{fit}$ with the measured $T_\mathrm{bath}$. The data shows two plateaus, and in between a linear dependency. The low-temperature plateau gives the effective base-temperature $T_\mathrm{base}$ floor of the transport measurement. The high-temperature saturation marks the loss of reliable superconducting fit sensitivity close to the critical temperature $T_\mathrm{c}$. I therefore use the median of the saturation as an estimate for the critical temperature. The mean value of the lower plateau is assumed to be the base temperature. The data in between is fitted by a linear fit.
            \begin{equation}
                T_\mathrm{fit}(T_\mathrm{bath}) = \alpha_T\,T_\mathrm{bath} + T_\mathrm{off}\,.
                \label{eq:tb:highres:temperature:calibration}
            \end{equation}
            Here $\alpha_T$ is the slope relating thermometer and fitted temperature in the linear regime, and $T_\mathrm{off}$ is the extrapolated temperature offset between both axes.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{1.6in}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-0.5em}
                \import{tunnelbarrier/highres/temperature}{fit.pgf}
                \caption{
                        Temperature calibration of TB1, showing sample over bath temperature.
                    }
                \label{fig:tb:highres:temperature:fit}
            \end{wrapfigure}
            I obtain
            \begin{align*}
                T_\mathrm{c} &= 1.267(17)\,\mathrm{K}\\
                T_\mathrm{base} &= 0.295(15)\,\mathrm{K}\\
                T_\mathrm{off} &= -0.0778(20)\,\mathrm{K}\\
                \alpha_T &= 0.9910(20)
            \end{align*}

            the critical temperature deducted from the zero temperature gap is $1.274$K, so the obtained critical temperature is well within the uncertainty. 

            The lower plateau might be due to uncertainties of the fit. especially for low temperatures the shape of the IV changes just so slightly, so i expect high errors in that region. However, we can also expect a finite electronic temperature seen by the electrodes, that differ from the bath temperature. This is a well known effect. For now i assume that this base temperature, corresopond to the lowest electronic temperature. 

            alpha beeing close to 1 is the expected behaviour, since we assume direct coupling between bath and sample.

            I think the offset temperature is due to the fact, that the iv is not measured in thermal equilibrium. Meaning, upon reaching the aimed temperature, the iv curve is measured, and for the next temparture is aimed already. so most likely not enough equilibration time for the sample reaching the same temperature as the bath.

            With this calibration, the temperature series can be mapped on a linear effective sample-temperature axis. Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:cal} shows the measured maps up to $T_\mathrm{c}$, where the superconducting gap closes. The calibrated maps shows the collapse of the gap at the transition, the reducing of the peak height at two delta and an enhanced zero-bias conductance near the high-temperature end of the sweep. 
            
            The figure represents three different representations of the very same dataset. On the x axis i usually show the voltage or current in reduced units. on the y axis i show axes like temperature or microwave amplitude. here, i also use reduced units in most cases. On the z axis i didstinguish between current, differential conductance or differential resistance. Again mostly in reduced units.

            The first column shows the IV characteristic, the second one the dI--dV caracteristic. this data is obtained by taking the numerical derivative. the third panel is a little different. For that we remap the voltage data on a linear current axis and take the derivative. Usually in literature one can find either dI--dV over voltage or dV--dI over current. Mostly depending on the bias. However in this thesis we show both for maximum tranparency. 

            Row wise, we first show about five curves, so one can really see the line shape. Since the I--V are usually symmetrical, we usually skip the negative voltages branch. 
            
            below is a surface plot. here I show the many curves measured, between the five shown above. Here i also introduce a color mapping. the highest values are attributed to white, the lowest to black and in between different shades of blue. The plot limits in z correspond to black and white. With that I can give a good feeling of the 3dimensionsal landscape.

            In the lowest row, we have a false color plot. This map is especially good to see features change position. e.g. the closing of the gap. however the shrinking of the coherence peak is not covered well.

            The surface is hard to interpret totally, so we show cuts in the yplane and a top view as false color map. with the cuts we can see the lineshape in great detail and in the false colromap we can see the position of features evolving.

            So in the end, i will now frequently use, the same nine panels to show a single dataset.            

            in the surface plot of the iv you can see one darker stripe, this is due to averaging multiple IVs for that corresponding temperature during the mapping.
            
            The corresponding SIS BCS/Dynes simulation in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:sim} provides the model comparison for the same temperature evolution. Keep in mind, that we now can simulate the whole measured temperature series with just 4 fixed parameters. 

            The simulation and the calibrated map are in great accordance. Leaving me to believe that we did a decent job with calibrating the temperature axis. This also holds true for the low temperature end. This isn't shown to highlight the evolving features for higher temperatures.

            I next used the same tunnel barrier as a microwave test structure. Because the static measurements show no measurable supercurrent, I do not expect Shapiro steps or incoherent Cooper-pair tunneling to dominate the response. The relevant process is instead photon-assisted quasiparticle tunneling. This makes TB1 a clean reference case because the microwave response can be compared directly to the Tien--Gordon model without first disentangling it from coherent Josephson transport.

            Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:exp} shows a measurement at $18.3\,\mathrm{GHz}$ in laboratory units. The vertical axis is the nominal output amplitude of the network analyzer, assuming a $50\,\Omega$ termination.

            In differential conductance, the main $2\Delta$ coherence peaks are accompanied by replicas shifted by integer multiples of $h\nu/e$. This is the expected photon-assisted tunneling pattern from the Tien--Gordon model discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}.

            \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{0.4\textwidth}
                \captionsetup{format=plain}%
                \centering
                \vspace{-1.5em}
                \import{tunnelbarrier/highres/amp_18.3GHz}{fit.pgf}
                \caption{
                        eta = 0.0008387229272526747
                    }
                \label{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:fit}
            \end{wrapfigure}

            To calibrate the microwave axis, I kept the static junction parameters fixed and fitted only the effective microwave amplitude at the junction. Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:fit} shows the resulting effective amplitude as a function of the nominal VNA output amplitude. The relation is approximately linear over most of the sweep, but the slope increases at larger output amplitudes. Around $300\,\mathrm{mV}$ nominal output amplitude, the fitted amplitude changes abruptly. I attribute this discontinuity to an internal source-amplification switch in the VNA.

            The slope is equivalent to the coupling parameter $\eta$. The median slope is
            \begin{equation}
                \eta = 0.008(12)\,.
            \end{equation}

            This calibration converts the nominal source setting into the reduced drive amplitude $eA/h\nu$. On this axis, the vertical coordinate is directly connected to the photon order that can contribute to one tunneling event. The calibrated maps therefore turn a hardware-dependent amplitude sweep into a model-level comparison between measured and simulated photon-assisted spectra.

            Figures~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:exp} and \ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:cal} show the same $18.3\,\mathrm{GHz}$ dataset before and after amplitude calibration, while Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:sim} shows the corresponding simulation in reduced units. The calibrated measurement and simulation reproduce the sideband positions and the redistribution of spectral weight with increasing drive amplitude.

            Figures~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp13.6GHz:cal} and \ref{fig:tb:highres:amp13.6GHz:sim} show the calibrated measurement and simulation for $13.6\,\mathrm{GHz}$. At this frequency the microwave path couples more strongly to the junction, so the effective amplitude is substantially larger for the same nominal output setting. The larger reduced amplitude reveals replicas corresponding to absorption or emission of at least 25 photons during a single quasiparticle tunneling event.

            This high photon order is the main resolution benchmark of the section. The sidebands remain visible far beyond the order commonly reported for superconducting tunnel junctions. Kot et al. resolved photon-assisted tunneling up to 12 photons, while the present SIS data reach at least the 25-photon range \cite{kot_microwave-assisted_2020}. To my knowledge, this is the highest photon order resolved in photon-assisted quasiparticle tunneling through an SIS junction.

            The high-resolution tunnel-barrier data therefore serve two purposes. They validate the calibration and visualization workflow on the easiest superconducting microwave system in this thesis, and they demonstrate that the setup resolves photon-assisted structure deep into the multiphoton regime. With this reference established, the next step is to study a related tunnel-barrier experiment where the plain BCS plus Tien--Gordon description is no longer sufficient.
