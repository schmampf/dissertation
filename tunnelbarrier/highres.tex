\subsection{High-Resolution Characterization}
\label{subsec:tb:highres}
%=========================================================

    This section starts with the simplest microwave driven superconducting junction investigated in this thesis. The goal is to understand one device as completely as possible before moving to junctions where additional mechanisms complicate the interpretation. TB1 is an SIS tunnel barrier (TB) without an observable supercurrent. Its static spectra are therefore expected to be described by quasiparticle tunneling between two BCS electrodes, and its microwave response should follow photon assisted tunneling as introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec:micro}.

    I used this device as a reference system for three connected questions. First, I determined how well the unirradiated \textit{I--V} curve is described by the TB parameters. Second, I used controlled heating to calibrate the temperature axis and to introduce the transport landscape visualization used throughout the chapter. Third, I drove the same junction with microwaves and used the remapped amplitude axis to test how far photon assisted sidebands can be resolved. The high-amplitude data show sidebands up to very high photon order and therefore provide a direct benchmark for the spectral resolution and microwave coupling of the setup.

    The sample TB1 was fabricated as described in Sec.~\ref{section:sample-preparation}. Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:image-TB1} shows an SEM image of the junction. Its caption gives the layer thicknesses, barrier overlap area, and in situ oxidation conditions that define the sample geometry and barrier fabrication. Irradiation experiments were performed with the antenna in the setup described in Sec.~\ref{sec:methods:setup}. All measurements discussed here were taken with the BJ in its virgin state as fabricated.
    
    A typical \textit{I--V} curve is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:single-iv}. The quasiparticle onset appears at approximately $2\Delta/e$, as expected for an SIS junction (Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:tunnel-current}). I do not observe a supercurrent, even though both electrodes are superconducting. A finite Josephson coupling may nevertheless remain below the experimental resolution. Using the Ambegaokar-Baratoff estimate (Eqs.~\ref{eq:macro:critical-current} and \ref{eq:macro:josephson-energy}) with parameters from the first column of Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres} gives
    \begin{align}
        I_\mathrm{c} &\approx \qty{4.5}{\nano\ampere}\,, &
        E_\mathrm{J} &\approx \qty{9.3}{\micro\electronvolt}\,, &
        E_\mathrm{T} &\approx \qty{18.5}{\micro\electronvolt}\,.
        \label{eq:tb:highres:josephson-estimate}
    \end{align}
    The estimated critical current is large enough to be observable in principle, but the thermal energy $E_\mathrm{T}$ is of the same order and already about twice as large as $E_\mathrm{J}$.
    This relation places the junction in a thermally fluctuating regime where the Josephson coupling cannot maintain a stable phase difference. This provides a natural explanation for the absence of a resolved zero-voltage branch.

    The capacitance of TB1 was not measured independently. Using the same geometric parallel-plate approximation as Lorenz \textit{et al.}~gives an estimated range of $C_\mathrm{TB}\approx\qtyrange{0.2}{0.4}{\femto\farad}$ \cite{lorenz_sset_mar_2018,lorenz_wechselspiel_2018}. This range is consistent with related MCBJ SET estimates by Susanne Sprenger, who found junction capacitances of about $\qty{0.1}{\femto\farad}$ \cite{sprenger_interplay_2019}, and Laura Sobral Rey, who found total island capacitances of about $\qty{1.2}{\femto\farad}$ \cite{sobral_rey_interplay_2022,sobral_rey_hybrid_sset_2024}.

    The corresponding charging energy for $C_\mathrm{TB}\sim\qtyrange{0.2}{0.4}{\femto\farad}$ is $E_\mathrm{c}=e^2/2C_\mathrm{TB}\sim\qtyrange{200}{400}{\micro\electronvolt}$. Even allowing for stray capacitance that increases the effective capacitance to several fF, this scale remains comparable to or larger than the Josephson energy. I therefore interpret the missing zero-voltage branch as consistent with a junction whose dc phase relation is strongly affected by thermal fluctuations, charge fluctuations, voltage noise, and the unengineered microwave environment. The electrodes remain superconducting, but the phase difference across the barrier is not stiff enough to produce a resolvable zero-voltage branch \cite{ambegaokar_tunneling_1963,tinkham_introduction_2004,grabert_single_2013}.

    Because no dc Josephson branch is resolved, I attribute the expected microwave response primarily to quasiparticle photon assisted tunneling rather than to Shapiro steps from coherent Cooper pair transport. The sharp superconducting gap edge provides a well-defined spectral reference, and microwave irradiation should generate replicas displaced from it by integer multiples of the single-photon energy.

    The lack of a supercurrent does not prevent me from characterizing the junction. The quasiparticle branch gives access to the superconducting gap $\Delta_0$, the normal-state conductance $G_\mathrm{N}$, the fitted electronic temperature $T_\mathrm{fit}$, the Dynes broadening $\gamma$, and the effective voltage broadening $\sigma_V$. Assuming symmetric electrodes and negligible magnetic field, I obtained the values listed in Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres} for the measurement blocks used in this section.

    In principle, one static \textit{I--V} fit would define the junction parameters for all later analysis. In practice, the extracted static parameters varied slightly between measurement blocks acquired up to several days apart. Small changes in electronic temperature, thermalization, residual voltage noise, or the electromagnetic environment can alter the measured line shape and shift correlated fit parameters. Because $G_\mathrm{N}$ and $\Delta_0$ remain similar across the measurement blocks, I attribute the variation primarily to changing measurement conditions rather than to a modified junction. I therefore used a representative static reference fit for each measurement block (M1\,-\,M4) listed in Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres}. Within each measurement block, I held the corresponding static parameters fixed while I extracted the intended calibration parameter, namely $T_\mathrm{fit}$ for the temperature sweep and the effective microwave amplitude $A_\mathrm{fit}$ for the microwave amplitude sweeps. Unless stated otherwise, the parenthetical uncertainties of fitted quantities correspond to one standard deviation obtained from the covariance matrix.

    The fitted gaps cluster near $\Delta_0\approx\qty{195}{\micro\electronvolt}$, slightly larger than the literature value for bulk aluminum of about $\qty{180}{\micro\electronvolt}$ used in Sec.~\ref{subsubsec:micro:bdg-eigenproblem}. This difference is typical for thin-film aluminum. The weak-coupling estimate from $\Delta_0$ gives $T_\mathrm{c}\approx\qty{1.274}{\kelvin}$, consistent with the temperature calibration in Sec.~\ref{subsec:tb:highres:temperature-calibration} \cite{giaever_study_1961,tinkham_introduction_2004}. The normal-state conductance remains near $G_\mathrm{N}\simeq0.19\,G_0$ in all reference fits, which places TB1 in the tunnel limit. BCS quasiparticle tunneling and Tien-Gordon photon assisted tunneling are therefore the appropriate minimal models for the measurement blocks in this section.

    The fitted electronic temperature $T_\mathrm{fit}$ is difficult to estimate from an SIS junction far below the critical temperature because the spectrum changes only weakly in that temperature range. Although the static fit gives $T_\mathrm{fit}=\qty{215(13)}{\milli\kelvin}$, the controlled temperature sweep in Sec.~\ref{subsec:tb:highres:temperature-calibration} shows a low-temperature fit plateau near $T_\mathrm{base}=\qty{282(2)}{\milli\kelvin}$. I therefore treat the single-trace value as a weakly constrained model parameter rather than as a direct temperature measurement.

    The remaining two fit parameters describe phenomenological broadening. The Dynes parameter $\gamma$ accounts for a finite quasiparticle lifetime and other mechanisms that broaden the singularity in the DOS itself, while $\sigma_V$ describes effective voltage broadening from residual bias noise and averaging during the voltage sweep. I keep both parameters because they affect the fit differently. Increasing $\gamma$ alone can round the curve at the gap edge, but it also increases the modeled subgap conductance. The additional voltage broadening allows the gap edge width to be reproduced without producing an overall rounding of the DOS.

    The BCS fit captures the gap position and the overall line shape, but it does not reproduce the full height of the quasiparticle peaks. The \textit{dI--dV} curve also shows small systematic deviations near the gap edge. I attribute these deviations primarily to effective voltage broadening and DOS broadening. As discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:tunnel-current} and Fig.~\ref{fig:micro:tunnel-iv-didv}, temperature in an SIS spectrum mainly changes the gap, subgap conductance, and the $2\Delta$ peak height in differential conductance near $T_\mathrm{c}$. In the low-temperature reference fits, $\sigma_V$ accounts for the residual gap edge width without requiring $\gamma$ to absorb too much of the rounding.

    The reference fits therefore identify TB1 as a well-behaved SIS tunnel junction across the measurement blocks. The least direct parameter is $T_\mathrm{fit}$, so I next test its meaning with a controlled temperature sweep and use the same data to introduce the transport landscape visualization used below.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Temperature Calibration}
    \label{subsec:tb:highres:temperature-calibration}
    %=========================================================

        \singlefitfigure{tunnelbarrier/highres/temperature/single-fit}{
            Representative \textit{I--V} fit from the temperature sweep at $T_\mathrm{bath}=\qty{697}{\milli\kelvin}$. The SIS BCS fit is used to extract the static parameters, shown in Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres}.
        }{fig:tb:highres:temperature:single-iv}

        The sample holder was heated locally while a thermometer next to the device recorded the bath temperature $T_\mathrm{bath}$. For each temperature point, I fitted the SIS BCS model to the acquired \textit{I--V} curve and extracted the fitted temperature $T_\mathrm{fit}$. I use $T_\mathrm{bath}$ for the thermometer reading and $T_\mathrm{fit}$ for the effective electronic transport temperature required by the tunnel model. For this sweep (M2), I used the static reference parameters in the temperature column of Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres}. Only $T_\mathrm{fit}$ varied point by point. The calibration therefore tests how the fitted SIS response relates the externally controlled bath temperature axis to an effective transport temperature axis.

        Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:fit} compares $T_\mathrm{fit}$ with $T_\mathrm{bath}$. The calibration curve has three regimes: a base plateau, an almost linear middle region, and saturation at high bath temperature. I describe all three regimes with a clipped affine calibration,
        \begin{equation}
            T_\mathrm{cal}(T_\mathrm{bath}) = \min\!\left[T_\mathrm{c},\,\max\!\left(T_\mathrm{base},\,\eta_\mathrm{bath}^{(\mathrm{fit})} T_\mathrm{bath}+T_\mathrm{off}\right)\right]\,.
            \label{eq:tb:highres:temperature:calibration}
        \end{equation}

        \begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{1.6in}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-0.5em}
            \thesisplot{tunnelbarrier/highres/temperature}{fit}
            \caption{
                    Temperature calibration of TB1. Three guides to the eye (GTE) indicate the low-temperature plateau, the linear intermediate regime, and the high-temperature saturation.
                }
            \label{fig:tb:highres:temperature:fit}
        \end{wrapfigure}

        Here $T_\mathrm{base}$ is the effective temperature floor, $T_\mathrm{c}$ is the upper saturation temperature, $\eta_\mathrm{bath}^{(\mathrm{fit})}$ is the slope relating the thermometer reading and fitted temperature in the intermediate regime, and $T_\mathrm{off}$ is the extrapolated temperature offset between the two axes. This procedure avoids manually excluding the plateau and saturation points. The full sweep is described with the same mapping, while the lower and upper bounds encode the weak temperature sensitivity far below and near $T_\mathrm{c}$. This procedure gives
        \begin{equation}
            \begin{aligned}
                T_\mathrm{c} &= \qty{1.256(26)}{\kelvin}\,,\\
                T_\mathrm{base} &= \qty{0.2819(17)}{\kelvin}\,,\\
                T_\mathrm{off} &= \qty{-0.0929(36)}{\kelvin}\,,\\
                \eta_\mathrm{bath}^{(\mathrm{fit})} &= 1.0023(40)\,.
            \end{aligned}
            \label{eq:tb:highres:temperature:parameters}
        \end{equation}
        The critical temperature inferred from the zero-temperature gap is about $\qty{1.274}{\kelvin}$, so the value obtained from the temperature sweep agrees within the uncertainty. The slope $\eta_\mathrm{bath}^{(\mathrm{fit})}$ is close to unity, as expected when the thermometer and electronic transport temperature follow each other in the intermediate region. Limited equilibration may contribute to the negative offset because I waited only a few seconds after each heater setpoint change before recording the next \textit{I--V} curve. The present data do not isolate this contribution from other systematic temperature offsets.

        The low-temperature plateau should not be read as a direct thermometer measurement. Far below $T_\mathrm{c}$, an SIS spectrum changes only weakly with temperature, so the fit has limited sensitivity to small changes in $T_\mathrm{fit}$. In addition, residual radiation, imperfect line thermalization, and bias dissipation can produce a real electronic temperature floor above the thermometer reading. I therefore interpret $T_\mathrm{base}$ as the lowest effective transport temperature resolved by this calibration. The high-temperature plateau corresponds to the critical temperature $T_\mathrm{c}$. At this temperature, superconductivity is completely suppressed, and the \textit{I--V} curve becomes linear.

        I remap the measured temperature sweep from the recorded $T_\mathrm{bath}$ coordinate to the corresponding effective $T_\mathrm{fit}$ coordinate. The transport data themselves are unchanged, but I placed each trace at the pointwise fitted transport temperature shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:fit}. The linear relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tb:highres:temperature:calibration} summarizes the fitted-temperature data, but the remapped landscape uses the fitted temperature values. Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:cal} shows the transport landscape after remapping the vertical axis to $T_\mathrm{fit}$. The gap closes near $T_\mathrm{c}$, the quasiparticle peaks at the gap edge shrink, and the zero-bias conductance increases near the high-temperature end of the sweep.

        This figure also introduces the transport landscape format used throughout the experimental results chapter. A sweep consists of a series of transport curves recorded while one control parameter is increased. The three columns show \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} representations of the same sweep. The top row shows representative curves, which preserve the detailed line shape. The middle row combines all curves into a surface. The bottom row shows the same data as a two-dimensional false-color map, with the sweep parameter and bias as coordinates and the transport response encoded by color. I refer to this last representation as a map, not to a separate measurement. Surface height and false-color map color use the same value range, so both views can be compared directly. I show both derivative representations because \textit{dI--dV} is natural for voltage biased gap features, while \textit{dV--dI} on a current axis remains useful when comparing to current biased views. The same nine-panel layout is reused below for sweeps in which microwave amplitude is the control parameter.

        The corresponding SIS BCS simulation in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:temperature:sim} uses the same $T_\mathrm{fit}$ axis and the static reference parameters from the temperature column of Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres}. The simulation reproduces the robust trends of the measurement, namely the gap closing, the reduction of the quasiparticle peak height, and the increase in zero-bias conductance. This agreement shows that the same SIS model captures the controlled temperature response of TB1 within the limits of the fitted-temperature calibration.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Microwave Amplitude Calibration}
    \label{subsec:tb:highres:amplitude-calibration}
    %=========================================================

        Next, I used TB1 to calibrate the microwave amplitude axis, to test the dynamic range of the PAT measurement. The two drive frequencies emphasize complementary aspects of the response. At $\qty{18.3}{\giga\hertz}$, the larger single-photon energy produces a wider voltage separation between adjacent replicas, while the weaker microwave coupling limits the maximum reduced amplitude reached in the sweep. The resulting uncluttered maps make the sideband evolution and amplitude calibration particularly clear. I therefore use this measurement first to introduce the analysis. At $\qty{13.6}{\giga\hertz}$, the smaller photon energy and stronger coupling provide access to a much wider range of reduced amplitudes and photon orders. I use this second measurement to test how far the sidebands remain resolvable in the multiphoton regime. For both sweeps, I fitted the effective junction amplitude point by point.

        In this quasiparticle PAT regime the relevant charge is $q=e$, so I use the reduced drive amplitude $\alpha_e=eA/h\nu$ and the voltage equivalent of the single-photon energy, $V_\nu=h\nu/e$.

        TB1 is the cleanest reference system for this purpose. The static data showed sharp quasiparticle onsets, the normal-state conductance placed the device in the tunnel limit, and I did not resolve a supercurrent. I therefore did not have to separate quasiparticle photon assisted tunneling from Shapiro steps or coherent Cooper pair transport. The expected microwave response was the quasiparticle PAT process introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:micro:pat}. For both microwave amplitude sweeps (M3 and M4), I used the corresponding static reference parameters from the matching column of Table~\ref{table:tunnelbarrier:highres}.

        Figure~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:exp} shows the raw $\qty{18.3}{\giga\hertz}$ amplitude sweep as a function of nominal VNA output. This hardware setting does not specify the ac voltage across the junction because the microwave path contains attenuation, impedance mismatch, antenna coupling, and standing waves. The effective transfer to the junction can therefore depend strongly on frequency. It may also depend on the exact contact configuration through its local impedance and electromagnetic environment. All measurements in this section were taken on the same unchanged TB1 contact, so these data cannot determine or exclude such a contact dependence. The resulting calibration is consequently an empirical conversion for this contact and each measured frequency, not a transferable property of the microwave line. The transport landscape figure nevertheless already identifies the mechanism because the main SIS quasiparticle peaks are accompanied by replicas shifted by integer multiples of the single-photon energy.

        \landscapefigure{tunnelbarrier/highres/amp_18.3GHz}{exp}{

        }{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:exp}

        I converted the nominal output setting into an effective junction amplitude by fitting the driven spectra with the Tien-Gordon model. The only fitted microwave parameter at each nominal output setting was the effective ac amplitude $A$ at the junction. This strongly constrains the fit because it cannot improve the agreement by moving the gap edge, changing the normal-state conductance, or broadening the spectrum.

        The single-trace fit in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp18.3GHz:single-iv} is the most direct check that the calibration is quantitative rather than only qualitative. The same reduced amplitude controls all sideband weights at once, so agreement over the main quasiparticle peaks, satellite peaks, and spectral weight tests the full PAT redistribution.

    \subsubsection*{High Dynamic Range}
    \label{subsec:tb:highres:high-dynamic-range}
    %=========================================================

        I then used the $\qty{13.6}{\giga\hertz}$ amplitude sweep (M4) as a dynamic-range test of the same remapped-axis picture. This frequency is favorable for high-order sidebands because the microwave coupling to TB1 was stronger and the photon energy was smaller than at $\qty{18.3}{\giga\hertz}$. For a comparable nominal source range, both effects increase the reduced amplitude and push spectral weight into higher Bessel sidebands. A representative \textit{I--V} curve is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tb:highres:amp13.6GHz:single-iv}.

        \singlefitfigure{tunnelbarrier/highres/amp_13.6GHz/single-fit}{
            Representative photon assisted tunneling fit at $\nu=\qty{13.6}{\giga\hertz}$ and $A_\mathrm{out}=\qty{150}{\milli\volt}$.         This high-order sideband resolution is the strongest result of the section because it requires four things at once: strong microwave coupling at the junction, enough voltage resolution to separate closely spaced photon replicas, a stable contact geometry throughout the sweep, and enough spectral stability that the underlying SIS feature remains identifiable after its weight has been redistributed over many sidebands. Here, I call a replica visually resolved when it appears at the expected displacement, remains distinguishable from neighboring features, and can be followed continuously through the full microwave amplitude map. The experiment therefore resolves a well-organized Tien-Gordon response over a sideband ladder that is much wider than $2\Delta$.

        The remapped amplitude axis is essential for this claim. In nominal VNA units, the high-order structure would only show that the source setting was large. For each trace, I instead infer the effective ac voltage at the junction by fitting the complete \textit{I--V} curve. On the resulting reduced-amplitude axis, the structure can be read as a PAT redistribution over many Bessel weighted channels. The high photon order therefore does not mean that every tunneling event absorbed exactly $25$ photons. It means that photon shifted tunneling channels with order numbers up to at least $25$ remain visually distinguishable in the measured quasiparticle spectrum.

        This interpretation also sets the limits of the result. I do not use the photon order to infer a quasiparticle tunneling time or traversal time. The PAT order labels energy shifted contributions in a driven tunneling probability, not a directly timed sequence of absorption events. The calibration is also local to this device, frequency, and antenna configuration. Heating and nonequilibrium quasiparticle generation may still contribute at high drive, even though the dominant visible structure remains organized by the Tien-Gordon sideband pattern.

        To my knowledge, no previous amplitude map of quasiparticle photon assisted tunneling in an SIS junction resolves and continuously tracks individual replicas to a higher order under this definition. The amplitude map reported by Kot \textit{et al.}~shows approximately $13$ visually distinguishable replica orders \cite{kot_microwave-assisted_2020}. The stable full amplitude map presented here follows the individual branches to at least order $n=25$. This comparison concerns visually resolved quasiparticle replicas, not the reduced drive reached in a single spectrum, high-order Shapiro response, or photon assisted subgap transport in finite-transmission weak links.

        The amplitude calibration data therefore serve two purposes. They establish a quantitative conversion from nominal microwave output to the reduced drive amplitude used on the y-axis, and they demonstrate that the setup resolves photon assisted structure deep into the multiphoton regime. With this reference established, the next step is to study a related tunnel barrier experiment where the plain BCS plus Tien-Gordon description is no longer sufficient.
