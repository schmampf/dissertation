    %=========================================================
    \subsection{The Mesoscopic Pincode}
    \label{sec:ac:pincode}
    %=========================================================

    How does a metallic contact form as its narrowest constriction is reduced to only a few atoms? At this scale, the normal-state conductance no longer provides a direct measure of a continuous contact area. It constrains only the sum of transmissions and therefore does not reveal how the current is distributed among the individual transmission channels. The channel-resolved measurement introduced by Scheer et al. provides a way to access this additional information through the nonlinear superconducting transport of an atomic contact \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. In this section, I reproduced this measurement to follow how the transmission channels evolved while an aluminum contact was opened. The extracted set of transmission eigenvalues \(\{\tau_i\}\) is referred to as the mesoscopic pincode.

    % fitting
    I recorded superconducting \textit{I--V} characteristics while opening a highly transmitting contact in small motor steps. I represented the measured current as a sum of independent MAR contributions and assigned a best-fit pincode to each stable contact state. Different values of $\tau_i$ produce distinct MAR contributions, so the nonlinear subgap spectrum contains information that is absent from $G_\mathrm{N}$ alone. The physical basis of this extraction is introduced in Secs.~\ref{subsec:quasi:meso} and \ref{subsec:meso:mar}, and the numerical fitting procedure is described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis}. The model assumes a short coherent contact with energy-independent normal-state transmissions. It therefore extracts the effective transmission eigenvalues that reproduce the measured dc MAR spectrum. It does not describe inelastic transport inside the contact, energy-dependent scattering, or the atomic orbitals and geometry that produce those eigenvalues.

    In the final fits, I fixed the temperature and Dynes broadening to $T=0$ and $\gamma=0$. Thermal and lifetime broadening were therefore not determined separately. Instead, I convolved the calculated voltage dependence with a Gaussian of width $\sigma_V$, which represents the combined experimental voltage resolution and any unresolved spectral smearing. I initially allowed the zero-temperature gap $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$ to vary independently for every spectrum together with the channel transmissions. Although the pincode changed substantially during opening, both nuisance parameters remained concentrated around
    \begin{align}
        \Delta_0 = 188.5(5)\,\mathrm{ueV}\,, &&
        \sigma_V = 25(1)\,\mathrm{uV}\,.
        \label{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}
    \end{align}
    Here, the values in parentheses quantify the spread of the fitted values across the trace, not the statistical uncertainty of an individual fit. The clustering is physically reasonable because $\Delta_0$ is set by the aluminum electrodes and $\sigma_V$ mainly by the measurement chain, whereas the changing atomic configuration primarily affects $\{\tau_i\}$. It also shows that the evolution assigned to the channels is not accompanied by a systematic drift of the gap or the effective resolution. I therefore used the cluster means as common parameters for the final channel extraction. This reduces correlations between spectral width, feature position, and the fitted transmissions, while retaining the contact-dependent pincode as the quantity of interest.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits} shows six representative contact states. The same best-fit pincode reproduces the measured \textit{I--V} characteristic and the corresponding differential conductance across states with different conductances and different numbers of resolved channels. This comparison tests the MAR line shape over the full bias range, rather than only the sum $G_\mathrm{N}/G_0=\sum_i\tau_i$. The reported transmissions are best-fit values on a grid with spacing $0.001$. This spacing is the numerical sampling resolution and not a confidence interval. Nearly equal channels cannot be distinguished by their identity, and sufficiently weak channels can fall below the sensitivity of the spectrum. The fitted channel count should consequently be understood as the number of contributions resolved within this model and data quality, not as an exact count of atomic orbitals. A full statistical uncertainty analysis and a unique microscopic reconstruction of the contact geometry are outside the scope of this section.

    The recorded motor position did not provide a direct displacement calibration. I therefore determined the scale from the conductance decay after the contact entered the tunnel regime. For a rectangular vacuum barrier, the normal-state conductance depends exponentially on the electrode separation $x$,
    \begin{equation}
        G_\mathrm{N}(x) \propto \exp(-2\kappa x)\,, \qquad
        \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m_\mathrm{e}\Phi}}{\hbar}\,.
        \label{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}
    \end{equation}
    Here, $m_\mathrm{e}$ is the electron mass and $\Phi$ is the effective barrier height. Because the aluminum electrodes were separated by breaking the constriction in situ, I assumed a clean aluminum surface and used $\Phi=4.2\,\mathrm{eV}$. This value is close to the measured work function $4.24(2)\,\mathrm{eV}$ of clean Al(111) \cite{grepstad_anisotropic_1976} and gives $\kappa=1.05\,\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}$.

    I fitted $\ln\left(G_\mathrm{N}/G_0\right)$ as a linear function of the recorded motor coordinate in the tunnel regime, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:tunneling}. The slope of this fit, together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}, converts the motor coordinate into a relative electrode displacement and gives approximately $2\,\mathrm{pm}$ per acquisition step. I defined $x=0$ at the beginning of the tunnel regime. Positions recorded while the electrodes were still in contact consequently have $x<0$. This calibration provides a physically motivated relative displacement scale, but it is not an independent measurement of the absolute electrode separation because it depends on the assumed barrier height and the rectangular-barrier approximation.

    % the trace
    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode} extends the extraction from the representative fits to the complete opening trace, while Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config} summarizes possible atomic configurations. Configurations (a) and (b) have conductances of approximately $3.8\,G_0$ and $3.2\,G_0$ and require seven and six resolved channels, respectively. Several channels have substantial transmission in both configurations. These states are therefore incompatible with the simple three-channel picture of a one-atom-wide aluminum contact and are consistent with the few-atom constriction sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(a). The abrupt transition from (a) to (b) changes both the conductance and the complete pincode. It is consequently not described by a uniform reduction of the existing transmissions. The fitted channel count constrains the minimum complexity of the contact, but it does not determine the number or arrangement of atoms uniquely.

    The transition to configuration (c) reduces the conductance to approximately $1.5\,G_0$ and leaves three resolved channels. At the beginning of this plateau, the dominant channel has $\tau_1\approx0.83$, while the two weaker channels have similar transmissions near $0.3$. This is the clearest candidate for a highly symmetric, one-atom-wide contact in the trace. Both the monomer and the axial dimer geometries in Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(b) and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(c) are compatible with such a minimum cross-section, and the transport data do not distinguish between them. The three-channel structure is qualitatively consistent with one axial channel of mixed $sp_z$ character and an approximately degenerate pair of transverse channels derived mainly from the $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. As configuration (c) evolves, the two weaker transmissions separate. One increases while the other decreases, and the dominant channel also decreases. This lifting of the approximate degeneracy indicates a gradual loss of rotational symmetry. A plausible microscopic picture is the formation of a dimer that becomes progressively distorted or displaced from the contact axis during opening. The modest decrease in $G_\mathrm{N}$ therefore conceals both a substantial redistribution among the three channels and a possible symmetry change of the atomic contact.

    At the transition to configuration (d), the conductance falls below $G_0$, but two principal channels remain. Across the plateau, the dominant transmission increases from approximately $0.33$ to $0.66$, while the second transmission decreases from approximately $0.21$ to $0.14$ and the weak third contribution closes. Configuration (e) begins after another abrupt rearrangement and shows the same qualitative trend. Its dominant transmission rises from approximately $0.35$ to $0.52$, while the second falls from approximately $0.15$ to $0.05$. The reduction from three comparable contributions in early configuration (c) to two principal channels in (d) and (e) is consistent with the off-center geometry sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(d). In this picture, the reduced symmetry suppresses one of the transverse contributions while two channels remain coupled to the electrodes. The upward slopes of the conductance plateaus in (d) and (e) are produced by the increase of the dominant channel outweighing the decrease of the weaker channels. Opening the junction does not require every transmission to decrease because deformation changes the local atomic arrangement and the coupling of the transport modes.

    The trace thus separates two forms of contact evolution. The discontinuities between configurations reorganize several transmissions simultaneously and are consistent with atomic rearrangements. The continuous evolution within a configuration is instead consistent with elastic deformation of a metastable atomic structure \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. Channel identities can be followed continuously within such a configuration, but they cannot be matched uniquely across a discontinuity. The final transition at $x=0$ produces configuration (f), where a single weak channel enters the tunnel geometry shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(e) and its conductance subsequently decays exponentially. The proposed sequence from a few-atom constriction through a symmetric monomer or dimer and an off-center contact to the tunnel regime is consistent with the channel evolution, but it is not a unique reconstruction of the atomic geometry. This combination of tilted plateaus, simultaneous channel evolution, and more than one channel below $G_0$ reproduces the principal phenomenology reported for aluminum atomic contacts \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}.

    % histogram stuff
    The relevant reference for an aluminum atomic contact is the tight-binding model developed by Cuevas, Levy Yeyati, and Mart{\'i}n-Rodero \cite{cuevas_microscopic_1998}. For an ideal one-atom contact oriented along the transport axis, the model gives three conducting channels. One nondegenerate channel with mixed $sp_z$ character is widely transmitting, while two weaker channels derived mainly from the transverse $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals are degenerate by rotational symmetry. After random atomic displacements are introduced, this degeneracy is lifted but the separation into two channel populations remains robust. The calculated distributions place the dominant channel approximately in the interval $0.6\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.9$ and the two weaker channels approximately in $0.1\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.3$. The fourth combination of the aluminum $s$ and $p$ orbitals remains essentially closed. This orbital interpretation was subsequently extended across different metals and connected the number of conducting channels to chemical valence \cite{scheer_signature_1998}.

    Cuevas et al. later calculated how these channels evolve under elastic deformation of a one-atom contact \cite{cuevas_evolution_1998}. This calculation does not give a second statistical distribution. Instead, it shows that the channel transmissions depend systematically on the distance between the central atom and its neighbors. Near the compact equilibrium geometry, the two transverse channels can each have transmissions of order $0.3$. During elongation their overlap decreases particularly strongly and their transmissions move toward zero, while the remaining $sp_z$-derived channel approaches unit transmission. The weak-channel population should therefore not be assigned a geometry-independent center near $0.2$. A mode closer to $0.3$ is compatible with compact or only weakly elongated one-atom configurations, whereas weight below $0.2$ is expected as the same contact is stretched toward rupture.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:histogram} collects all transmissions extracted along the opening trace. With a favorable interpretation, two broad populations can be recognized. The large low-transmission population has a shoulder or mode near $\tau\approx0.3$, while the smaller high-transmission population extends through approximately $0.6$ to $0.9$. This structure is qualitatively consistent with the weak transverse channels and the dominant $sp_z$-derived channel predicted by the tight-binding model. In particular, the position near $0.3$ is plausible for compact or weakly elongated one-atom configurations in the deformation calculation. The broad tail toward lower transmission is also expected because the transverse channels are suppressed as the contact is stretched. The strong accumulation near $\tau=0$ additionally contains channels that close during stretching and the final approach to the tunnel regime. Several intermediate peaks arise because the histogram combines successive, correlated states from the few-atom, symmetric, and off-center configurations rather than an ensemble restricted to equivalent one-atom contacts. I therefore interpret the apparent bimodality and the low-transmission mode near $0.3$ as suggestive evidence for the orbital-derived channel populations and their deformation predicted for aluminum, but not as a quantitative measurement of a theoretical distribution.


    In summary, I established an experimental and numerical workflow for assigning a mesoscopic pincode to each stable measured contact state. The representative fits validate the channel decomposition, and the reconstructed transport landscape shows that the extracted pincodes capture the systematic evolution of the MAR spectrum throughout the opening trace.
