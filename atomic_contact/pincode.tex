    %=========================================================
    \subsection{The Mesoscopic Pincode}
    \label{subsec:ac:pincode}
    %=========================================================

    What happens when a highly transmitting aluminum contact is opened very slowly in picometer scale steps? The constriction does not narrow uniformly. Its conductance evolves along tilted plateaus interrupted by abrupt jumps, suggesting elastic deformation of metastable structures followed by atomic rearrangements. The opening trace reveals when these changes occur, but not how the current is redistributed among the conducting channels. Superconducting transport provides this missing information because each channel produces a characteristic multiple Andreev reflection (MAR) structure. The channel resolved method introduced by Scheer \textit{et al.}~therefore turns the nonlinear subgap spectrum into a probe of the evolving contact \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. The extracted mesoscopic pincode \(\{\tau_i\}\) is the set of transmission eigenvalues obtained from the measured spectrum.

    In this section, I follow the pincode along a complete opening trace rather than analyzing isolated contact states. The fitted spectra test whether the MAR model captures the measured spectral evolution, while the tunnel decay provides a relative displacement calibration. I then use the extracted mesoscopic pincode \(\{\tau_i\}\) to distinguish continuous evolution within plateaus from abrupt reorganizations between them. Comparison with orbital-based models for aluminum suggests plausible changes of the contact symmetry and atomic geometry, but it does not determine a unique microscopic structure.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Pincode Extraction and Fit Quality}
    %=========================================================

        I recorded superconducting \textit{I--V} characteristics while opening a highly transmitting contact in small motor steps. I represented the measured current as a sum of independent MAR contributions and assigned a best-fit pincode to every recorded contact state. Different sets of $\{\tau_i\}$ produce distinct MAR contributions, so the nonlinear subgap spectrum contains information that cannot be retrieved from the normal-state conductance $G_\mathrm{N}$ alone. The physical basis of this extraction is introduced in Secs.~\ref{subsec:quasi:meso} and \ref{subsec:meso:mar}, and the numerical fitting procedure is described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis}. The model assumes a short coherent contact with energy-independent transmissions. It therefore extracts the effective transmission eigenvalues that reproduce the measured dc MAR spectrum. The fit does not uniquely determine the underlying atomic orbitals or contact geometry. I infer plausible microscopic configurations later by comparing the number, magnitude, degeneracy, and evolution of the fitted channels with theoretical expectations for aluminum. Inelastic transport inside the contact and energy-dependent scattering are not included in this interpretation.

        In the final fits, I fixed the temperature and Dynes broadening to $T=0$ and $\gamma=0$. The bath temperature remained below $\qty{50}{\milli\kelvin}$, corresponding to $\qty{4.3}{\micro\electronvolt}$. This energy is small compared with both the superconducting gap and the observed spectral width, which makes the zero-temperature approximation appropriate for the present extraction. Setting $\gamma=0$ is a minimal model assumption rather than evidence for an infinite quasiparticle lifetime. Finite electronic temperature, lifetime broadening, and voltage noise all smooth the MAR structure and cannot be separated reliably in this data set. I therefore did not fit these effects simultaneously. Instead, I convolved the calculated voltage dependence with a Gaussian of width $\sigma_V$, which represents the experimental voltage resolution together with unresolved thermal and lifetime broadening. I allowed the zero-temperature gap $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$ to vary independently for every spectrum in the same fitting step as the channel transmissions. Although the pincode changed substantially during opening, both nuisance parameters remained concentrated around
        \begin{align}
            \Delta_0 = \qty{188.5(5)}{\micro\electronvolt}\,, &&
            \sigma_V = \qty{25(1)}{\micro\volt}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}
        \end{align}
        Here, the central values are the means across the trace and the uncertainties are the corresponding standard deviations, not the statistical uncertainties of individual fits. The medians give the same central values within rounding. The clustering is physically reasonable because $\Delta_0$ is set by the aluminum electrodes and $\sigma_V$ mainly by the measurement chain, whereas the changing atomic configuration primarily affects $\{\tau_i\}$. No systematic drift of $\Delta_0$ or $\sigma_V$ was apparent along the trace.

        I minimized the current-space residual with point weights proportional to the inverse absolute voltage $w_k\propto1/|V_k|$. This weighting reduces the influence of larger absolute currents at high bias and retains sensitivity to the subgap MAR structure. The zero-voltage point carried zero weight, and the central interval containing the switching branch was excluded because it is not described by the dc MAR model.

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits} in the supplementary material, Sec.~\ref{appendix:ac:pincode}, shows six representative contact states. For each state, the best-fit pincode reproduces the measured \textit{I--V} characteristic and the corresponding differential conductance. This comparison covers states with different conductances and numbers of fitted channels and tests the MAR line shape over the full bias range. The derivative is calculated from the same measured \textit{I--V} data and is therefore a complementary representation. The reported transmissions are best-fit values on a grid with spacing $0.001$. This spacing is the numerical sampling resolution, not a confidence interval. Nearly equal channels cannot be distinguished, and sufficiently weak channels can fall below the sensitivity of the spectrum. The fitted channel count should consequently be understood as the number of contributions resolved within this model and data quality, not as an exact count of conduction channels.

        The discrete optimization used random restarts to reduce its sensitivity to the initial pincode, but it did not guarantee the global minimum. Independent restarts converged to the same dominant transmissions. Because the fit also permitted $\tau_i=0$, solutions with a larger maximum channel count included solutions with fewer contributing channels and could contain nearly degenerate weak contributions. A full statistical uncertainty analysis is outside the scope of this section. Claims of channel degeneracy, splitting, and continuity below are therefore qualitative features of the fitted eigenvalue spectrum. They should not be read as confidence-qualified identification or tracking of individual orbital channels.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Displacement Calibration}
    %=========================================================

        To calibrate the distance axis, I determined the scale from the conductance decay after the contact entered the tunnel regime. For a rectangular vacuum barrier, the normal-state conductance depends exponentially on the electrode separation $x$,
        \begin{align}
            G_\mathrm{N}(x) \propto \exp(-2\kappa x)\,, &&
            \kappa = \left. \sqrt{2m_\mathrm{e}U_\mathrm{eff}}\right/\hbar\approx \qty{1.05}{\per\angstromunit}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}
        \end{align}
        Here, $m_\mathrm{e}$ is the electron mass and $U_\mathrm{eff}$ is the effective barrier height. Because the aluminum electrodes were separated by breaking the constriction in situ, I assumed a clean aluminum surface and used $U_\mathrm{eff}=\qty{4.2}{\electronvolt}$ \cite{grepstad_anisotropic_1976}.

            \thesisplot{atomic_contact/breaking}{tunneling}
            % \vspace{-1.5em}
            \caption{
                Tunnel-decay fit used to calibrate displacement.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:pincode:tunneling}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        I fitted $\ln\left(G_\mathrm{N}/G_0\right)$ as a linear function of the recorded motor coordinate in the tunnel regime. The slope of this fit, together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}, converts the motor coordinate into a relative electrode displacement and gives approximately $\qty{2}{\pico\meter}$ per acquisition step. I defined $x=0$ at the beginning of the tunnel regime. Positions recorded while the electrodes were still in contact consequently have $x<0$. This approximate calibration is used only as a relative displacement scale for visualization. Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:tunneling} shows the fitted data with the arbitrary motor coordinate converted to the calibrated displacement $x$.

        With this relative displacement scale established, Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal} and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim}, collected in the supplementary material in Sec.~\ref{appendix:ac:pincode}, compare the complete measured and reconstructed transport landscapes. Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal} displays the data supplied to the fitting workflow, while Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim} is calculated from the extracted pincodes and the mean values in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}, because both parameters describe position-independent properties of the electrodes and measurement chain. The agreement between the landscapes provides a global consistency check complementary to the individual spectra in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits}. Because the reconstruction uses parameters fitted to the same data, it is not an independent validation. It nevertheless shows that the coherent MAR model follows the principal displacement-dependent structures and their abrupt or continuous evolution. The \textit{dI--dV} and \textit{dV--dI} representations emphasize deviations that are less visible in the current itself.


    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Channel Evolution and Contact Geometry}
    %=========================================================

        Having established that the fitted pincodes reproduce the measured transport landscape, I now use their displacement dependence to interpret how the contact evolves. The minimal microscopic reference is the tight-binding model of an aluminum one-atom contact developed by Cuevas, Levy Yeyati, and Mart{\'i}n-Rodero \cite{cuevas_microscopic_1998}. It predicts one highly transmitting channel with mixed axial $sp_z$ character and two weaker channels composed mainly of the transverse $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals. The transverse pair is degenerate in a rotationally symmetric contact and splits when the symmetry is broken \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. Under elongation, the transverse transmissions decrease toward zero while the axial transmission approaches unity \cite{cuevas_evolution_1998}. These signatures provide the reference for interpreting the number, degeneracy, and displacement dependence of the extracted channels, but they do not identify a unique atomic geometry.

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pincode} condenses the complete extraction into the normal-state conductance and the individual channel transmissions. The six regions (a)--(f) mark successive metastable contact configurations separated by abrupt rearrangements. Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config} shows the schematic geometries used to discuss their possible microscopic origin.

        Configurations (a) and (b) contain up to seven contributing channels with substantial transmission. They are therefore consistent with a few-atom constriction, sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(a). The jump between them reorganizes the complete pincode. The channel count constrains the complexity of the constriction, but not its precise atomic arrangement.

        Configuration (c) is fitted with three resolved contributions and is the clearest candidate for a highly symmetric one-atom contact. Its dominant transmission and approximately degenerate pair of weaker transmissions agree qualitatively with the axial $sp_z$ and transverse $p_x,p_y$ contributions expected for aluminum \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. The fitted pair separates as the plateau evolves, which is consistent with a gradual loss of rotational symmetry. Both the monomer and the axial dimer geometries in Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(b) and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(c) are compatible with the initial state. The later separation therefore suggests that a dimer or one-atom contact becomes distorted or displaced from the contact axis.

        Configurations (d) and (e) retain two principal channels even after the conductance falls below $G_0$. Within both plateaus, the dominant channel strengthens while the weaker contribution is suppressed. This behavior is consistent with the off-center geometry in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(d), where reduced symmetry suppresses one transverse contribution.

        The transition from (d) to (e) is unusually selective within the fitted eigenvalue spectrum. The dominant transmission changes abruptly, while the weaker transmission remains almost continuous and the third stays nearly closed. This pattern is consistent with a rearrangement that mainly modifies the axial transport path while retaining a weak transverse contribution of similar magnitude. The states related to the Al $3p_z$ orbital are especially sensitive to slight changes in position, and first-principles calculations show a strong geometry dependence of aluminum channel transmissions \cite{schwingenschlogl_geometry_2007,senkpiel_extracting_2022}. Nevertheless, the fit determines the eigenvalues and not the eigenvectors, so it cannot verify whether the weaker contribution represents the same orbital channel across the jump. The predominantly axial rearrangement is a microscopic hypothesis.

        Overall, the discontinuities between configurations are consistent with atomic rearrangements, while the continuous evolution within each plateau is consistent with elastic deformation of a metastable structure \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. This interpretation is supported by a related study of an aluminum atomic contact, in which superconducting transmission channel analysis combined with molecular dynamics and quantum transport calculations attributed bistable conductance switching to the reversible rearrangement of single atoms \cite{schirm_current-driven_2013}.
        
        In the final configuration (f), a single weak channel enters the tunnel geometry shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(e) and its conductance subsequently decays exponentially according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}. The proposed sequence from a few-atom constriction through a symmetric monomer or dimer and an off-center contact to tunneling captures the channel evolution, but it is not a unique reconstruction of the atomic geometry.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Distribution of the Extracted Transmissions}
    %=========================================================

        The tight-binding results for randomly distorted one-atom aluminum contacts provide a reference for the transmission histogram. They place the dominant axial channel with mixed $sp_z$ character approximately within $0.6\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.9$ and the two weaker transverse channels associated mainly with the $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals approximately within $0.1\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.3$ \cite{cuevas_microscopic_1998}. This channel pattern applies to one-atom contacts and should not be used to interpret the larger few-atom configurations. The separate deformation calculation does not provide a statistical distribution. It instead shows that the transverse transmissions can be of order $0.3$ near a compact geometry and move below $0.2$ as the contact is elongated, while the axial transmission moves toward unity \cite{cuevas_evolution_1998}. The transmission ranges expected for the axial and transverse channels therefore depend on the geometry and its elongation.

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:histogram} is restricted to the active channels in configurations (c)\,-\,(e), which contain two or three resolved contributions and are the plausible one-atom states identified above. It excludes the larger few-atom configurations (a) and (b), for which the one-atom channel pattern is not applicable, as well as the tunnel configuration (f). The active transmissions form a broad low-transmission distribution extending from near zero to approximately $0.6$ and a separate high-transmission distribution between approximately $0.7$ and $0.9$. This separation is qualitatively consistent with weaker transverse channels and a dominant axial channel. Weight around $\tau\approx0.3$ is plausible for compact or weakly elongated configurations, while the extension toward lower transmission is compatible with the suppression of transverse channels during stretching.

        Two relatively sharp peaks near $\tau\approx0.2$ and $0.4$ are superimposed on the broad low-transmission distribution. They arise from long stretches of the opening trace over which the fitted transmissions change very little. Consecutive spectra in these stretches repeatedly contribute nearly the same values to the histogram. The sharp peaks therefore reflect the sampled trajectory and should not be interpreted as intrinsically preferred channel transmissions. More generally, the spectra are correlated and configurations (c)\,-\,(e) contribute unequal numbers of samples, so the relative weights of the two broad distributions are not statistical occurrence probabilities. Restricting the histogram to active channels in these regions nevertheless makes their transmission ranges meaningfully comparable with the one-atom orbital model. This comparison does not distinguish a monomer from an axial dimer or assign an orbital character to an individual fitted eigenvalue.

        In summary, the coherent MAR model reproduces the measured spectral evolution along a complete opening trace with common values of $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$. This agreement is the robust result of the extraction. The extracted mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$ evolves continuously on conductance plateaus and reorganizes at abrupt jumps, which is consistent with elastic deformation between atomic rearrangements. The number, approximate degeneracy, and separation of the fitted transmissions further suggest a progression from a few-atom constriction through a nearly symmetric three-channel monomer or dimer to an off-center contact and finally to the tunnel regime. The orbital assignments and the predominantly axial interpretation of the transition from (d) to (e) remain microscopic hypotheses because the fit does not determine channel eigenvectors or a unique atomic geometry. The restricted histogram provides an additional descriptive comparison with the low transverse and high axial transmission ranges expected for one-atom aluminum contacts, while its sharp peaks reflect repeated sampling along the opening trajectory. Channel resolved MAR spectroscopy therefore reveals changes in the mesoscopic pincode that remain hidden in the normal-state conductance alone and constrain, without uniquely reconstructing, the evolution of the contact.
