    %=========================================================
    \subsection{The Mesoscopic Pincode}
    \label{sec:ac:pincode}
    %=========================================================

    What happens when a highly transmitting aluminum contact is opened very slowly in picometer-scale steps? The constriction does not narrow uniformly. Its conductance evolves along tilted plateaus interrupted by abrupt jumps, suggesting elastic deformation of metastable structures followed by atomic rearrangements. The normal-state trace reveals when these changes occur, but not how the current is redistributed among the transmission channels. Superconducting transport provides this missing information because each channel produces a characteristic multiple-Andreev-reflection structure. The channel-resolved method introduced by Scheer et al. therefore turns the nonlinear subgap spectrum into a probe of the evolving contact \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. The extracted set of transmission eigenvalues \(\{\tau_i\}\) is referred to as the mesoscopic pincode.

    In this section, I followed the pincode along a complete opening trace rather than analyzing isolated contact states. The fitted spectra and reconstructed transport landscape establish the reliability of the extraction, while the tunnel decay provides a relative displacement calibration. The channel evolution reveals a progression from a few-atom constriction through a nearly symmetric three-channel contact and less symmetric off-center configurations to the tunnel regime. Continuous changes within plateaus and abrupt reorganizations between them distinguish elastic deformation from atomic rearrangements. Comparison with orbital-based models for aluminum then connects the observed channel degeneracy, splitting, and transmission distribution to plausible changes of the atomic geometry. This interpretation constrains the microscopic evolution of the contact, although it does not determine a unique atomic structure.

    % fitting
    I recorded superconducting \textit{I--V} characteristics while opening a highly transmitting contact in small motor steps. I represented the measured current as a sum of independent MAR contributions and assigned a best-fit pincode to each stable contact state. Different values of $\tau_i$ produce distinct MAR contributions, so the nonlinear subgap spectrum contains information that is absent from $G_\mathrm{N}$ alone. The physical basis of this extraction is introduced in Secs.~\ref{subsec:quasi:meso} and \ref{subsec:meso:mar}, and the numerical fitting procedure is described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis}. The model assumes a short coherent contact with energy-independent normal-state transmissions. It therefore extracts the effective transmission eigenvalues that reproduce the measured dc MAR spectrum. The fit does not determine the underlying atomic orbitals or contact geometry uniquely. I infer plausible microscopic configurations later by comparing the number, magnitude, degeneracy, and evolution of the fitted channels with theoretical expectations for aluminum. Inelastic transport inside the contact and energy-dependent scattering are not included in this interpretation.

    In the final fits, I fixed the temperature and Dynes broadening to $T=0$ and $\gamma=0$. The measurements were performed with the bath temperature constantly below $50\,\mathrm{mK}$, corresponding to $4.3\,\mathrm{ueV}$. This energy is small compared with both the superconducting gap and the observed spectral width, which makes the zero-temperature approximation appropriate for the present extraction. Setting $\gamma=0$ is a minimal-model assumption rather than evidence for an infinite quasiparticle lifetime. A finite electronic temperature, lifetime broadening, and voltage noise all smooth the MAR structure and cannot be separated reliably from this data set. I therefore did not fit these effects simultaneously. Instead, I convolved the calculated voltage dependence with a Gaussian of width $\sigma_V$, which represents the experimental voltage resolution together with unresolved thermal and lifetime broadening. I initially allowed the zero-temperature gap $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$ to vary independently for every spectrum together with the channel transmissions. Although the pincode changed substantially during opening, both nuisance parameters remained concentrated around
    \begin{align}
        \Delta_0 = 188.5(5)\,\mathrm{ueV}\,, &&
        \sigma_V = 25(1)\,\mathrm{uV}\,.
        \label{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}
    \end{align}
    Here, the uncertainty quantify the spread of the fitted values across the trace, not the statistical uncertainty of an individual fit. The clustering is physically reasonable because $\Delta_0$ is set by the aluminum electrodes and $\sigma_V$ mainly by the measurement chain, whereas the changing atomic configuration primarily affects $\{\tau_i\}$. It also shows that the evolution assigned to the channels is not accompanied by a systematic drift of the gap or the effective resolution.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits} shows six representative contact states. The same best-fit pincode reproduces the measured \textit{I--V} characteristic and the corresponding differential conductance across states with different conductances and different numbers of resolved channels. This comparison tests the MAR line shape over the full bias range rather than only the total normal-state conductance. The reported transmissions are best-fit values on a grid with spacing $0.001$. This spacing is the numerical sampling resolution and not a confidence interval. Nearly equal channels cannot be distinguished by their identity, and sufficiently weak channels can fall below the sensitivity of the spectrum. The fitted channel count should consequently be understood as the number of contributions resolved within this model and data quality, not as an exact count of atomic orbitals. A full statistical uncertainty analysis and a unique microscopic reconstruction of the contact geometry are outside the scope of this section.

    % position calibration
    The recorded motor position did not provide a direct displacement calibration. I therefore determined the scale from the conductance decay after the contact entered the tunnel regime. For a rectangular vacuum barrier, the normal-state conductance depends exponentially on the electrode separation $x$,
    \begin{equation}
        G_\mathrm{N}(x) \propto \exp(-2\kappa x)\,, \qquad
        \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m_\mathrm{e}\Phi}}{\hbar}\approx 1.05\,\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}\,.
        \label{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}
    \end{equation}
    Here, $m_\mathrm{e}$ is the electron mass and $\Phi$ is the effective barrier height. Because the aluminum electrodes were separated by breaking the constriction in situ, I assumed a clean aluminum surface and used $\Phi=4.2\,\mathrm{eV}$ \cite{grepstad_anisotropic_1976}.

    I fitted $\ln\left(G_\mathrm{N}/G_0\right)$ as a linear function of the recorded motor coordinate in the tunnel regime, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:tunneling}. The slope of this fit, together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}, converts the motor coordinate into a relative electrode displacement and gives approximately $2\,\mathrm{pm}$ per acquisition step. I defined $x=0$ at the beginning of the tunnel regime. Positions recorded while the electrodes were still in contact consequently have $x<0$. This calibration provides a physically motivated relative displacement scale, but it is not an independent measurement of the absolute electrode separation because it depends on the assumed barrier height and the rectangular-barrier approximation.

    With this relative displacement scale established, Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal} and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim} compare the complete measured and reconstructed transport landscapes. Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal} displays the data supplied to the fitting workflow, while Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim} is calculated from the extracted pincodes and the common parameters in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}. Their agreement provides a global validation complementary to the individual spectra in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits}. In particular, the reconstruction follows the displacement-dependent MAR structures, their abrupt reorganization between stable configurations, and their continuous evolution within a plateau. The \textit{dI--dV} and \textit{dV--dI} representations emphasize deviations that are less visible in the current itself, although they are derived from the same measured \textit{I--V} data and are therefore not independent fit observables. 

    % the trace
    Having established that the fitted pincodes reproduce the measured transport landscape, I now use their displacement dependence to interpret how the contact evolves. Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode} condenses the complete extraction into the normal-state conductance and the individual channel transmissions. The six regions (a)--(f) mark successive metastable contact configurations separated by abrupt rearrangements. Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:config} provides the schematic geometries used to discuss their possible microscopic origin.

    Configurations (a) and (b) contain six or more resolved channels with substantial transmission. They are therefore consistent with a few-atom constriction, sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(a), rather than a one-atom-wide aluminum contact. The jump between them reorganizes the complete pincode instead of uniformly reducing all transmissions. The channel count constrains the complexity of the constriction, but not its precise atomic arrangement.

    Configuration (c) leaves three channels and is the clearest candidate for a highly symmetric one-atom contact. Its dominant channel and approximately degenerate pair of weaker channels agree qualitatively with the axial $sp_z$ and transverse $p_x,p_y$ contributions expected for aluminum \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. The weaker channels split as the plateau evolves, indicating a gradual loss of rotational symmetry. Both the monomer and the axial dimer geometries in Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(b) and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(c) are compatible with the initial state. The later splitting suggests that a dimer or one-atom contact becomes distorted or displaced from the contact axis.

    Configurations (d) and (e) retain two principal channels even after the conductance falls below $G_0$. Within both plateaus, the dominant channel strengthens while the weaker contribution is suppressed. Their upward slope is therefore caused by a redistribution between channels rather than by all transmissions changing in the same direction. This behavior is consistent with the off-center geometry in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(d), where reduced symmetry suppresses one transverse contribution.

    The transition from (d) to (e) is unusually selective. The dominant channel changes abruptly, while the weaker transmission remains almost continuous and the third channel stays nearly closed. This suggests that the rearrangement mainly modifies the axial transport path while leaving a weak transverse contribution coupled with similar strength. The interpretation is supported by the sensitivity of Al $3p_z$-related states to local coordination and by first-principles calculations showing the strong geometry dependence of aluminum channel transmissions \cite{schwingenschlogl_geometry_2007,senkpiel_extracting_2022}. Nevertheless, the fit determines eigenvalues rather than eigenvectors, so it cannot prove that the weaker contribution is the same orbital channel across the jump. I therefore describe the transition as predominantly axial rather than as an unambiguous tracking of one channel.

    In the final configuration (f), a single weak channel enters the tunnel geometry shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(e) and its conductance subsequently decays exponentially, as discussed before. 

    Overall, the discontinuities between configurations are consistent with atomic rearrangements, while the continuous evolution within each plateau reflects elastic deformation of a metastable structure \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. In the final configuration (f), a single weak channel enters the tunnel geometry shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(e) and its conductance subsequently decays exponentially, as discussed before. The proposed sequence from a few-atom constriction through a symmetric monomer or dimer and an off-center contact to tunneling captures the channel evolution, but it is not a unique reconstruction of the atomic geometry.

    % histogram stuff
    The relevant reference for an aluminum atomic contact is the tight-binding model developed by Cuevas, Levy Yeyati, and Mart{\'i}n-Rodero \cite{cuevas_microscopic_1998}. For an ideal one-atom contact oriented along the transport axis, the model gives three conducting channels. One nondegenerate channel with mixed $sp_z$ character is widely transmitting, while two weaker channels derived mainly from the transverse $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals are degenerate by rotational symmetry. After random atomic displacements are introduced, this degeneracy is lifted but the separation into two channel populations remains robust. The calculated distributions place the dominant channel approximately in the interval $0.6\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.9$ and the two weaker channels approximately in $0.1\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.3$. The fourth combination of the aluminum $s$ and $p$ orbitals remains essentially closed. This orbital interpretation was subsequently extended across different metals and connected the number of conducting channels to chemical valence \cite{scheer_signature_1998}.

    Cuevas et al.~later calculated how these channels evolve under elastic deformation of a one-atom contact \cite{cuevas_evolution_1998}. This calculation does not give a second statistical distribution. Instead, it shows that the channel transmissions depend systematically on the distance between the central atom and its neighbors. Near the compact equilibrium geometry, the two transverse channels can each have transmissions of order $0.3$. During elongation their overlap decreases particularly strongly and their transmissions move toward zero, while the remaining $sp_z$-derived channel approaches unit transmission. The weak-channel population should therefore not be assigned a geometry-independent center near $0.2$. A mode closer to $0.3$ is compatible with compact or only weakly elongated one-atom configurations, whereas weight below $0.2$ is expected as the same contact is stretched toward rupture.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:histogram} collects all transmissions extracted along the opening trace. With a favorable interpretation, two broad populations can be recognized. The large low-transmission population has a shoulder or mode near $\tau\approx0.3$, while the smaller high-transmission population extends through approximately $0.6$ to $0.9$. This structure is qualitatively consistent with the weak transverse channels and the dominant $sp_z$-derived channel predicted by the tight-binding model. In particular, the position near $0.3$ is plausible for compact or weakly elongated one-atom configurations in the deformation calculation. The broad tail toward lower transmission is also expected because the transverse channels are suppressed as the contact is stretched. The strong accumulation near $\tau=0$ additionally contains channels that close during stretching and the final approach to the tunnel regime. Several intermediate peaks arise because the histogram combines successive, correlated states from the few-atom, symmetric, and off-center configurations rather than an ensemble restricted to equivalent one-atom contacts. I therefore interpret the apparent bimodality and the low-transmission mode near $0.3$ as suggestive evidence for the orbital-derived channel populations and their deformation predicted for aluminum, but not as a quantitative measurement of a theoretical distribution.

    In summary, I reliably extracted a mesoscopic pincode for every stable state along a complete opening trace of an aluminum atomic contact. The representative spectra and the reconstructed transport landscape reproduce the measured MAR structure with common values of $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$, showing that the spectral evolution is captured primarily by the position-dependent channel transmissions. The resulting trace distinguishes continuous channel evolution within metastable plateaus from abrupt reorganizations associated with atomic rearrangements. It is consistent with a progression from a few-atom constriction through a nearly symmetric three-channel monomer or dimer to an off-center contact and finally the tunnel regime. In particular, the approximate transverse-channel degeneracy in configuration (c), its subsequent lifting, and the predominantly axial reorganization between configurations (d) and (e) connect the measured pincodes to changes in the local contact symmetry. The transmission histogram further shows the two broad populations expected for a dominant $sp_z$-derived channel and weaker transverse channels in aluminum. These assignments do not provide a unique atomic reconstruction, but together they demonstrate that channel-resolved MAR spectroscopy reveals structural and orbital changes that remain hidden in the normal-state conductance alone.
