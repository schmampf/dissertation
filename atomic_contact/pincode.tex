    %=========================================================
    \subsection{The Mesoscopic Pincode}
    \label{subsec:ac:pincode}
    %=========================================================

    What happens when a highly transmitting aluminum contact is opened very slowly in picometer-scale steps? The constriction does not narrow uniformly. Its conductance evolves along tilted plateaus interrupted by abrupt jumps, suggesting elastic deformation of metastable structures followed by atomic rearrangements. The normal-state trace reveals when these changes occur, but not how the current is redistributed among the transmission channels. Superconducting transport provides this missing information because each channel produces a characteristic multiple-Andreev-reflection structure. The channel-resolved method introduced by Scheer et al. therefore turns the nonlinear subgap spectrum into a probe of the evolving contact \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. The extracted mesoscopic pincode \(\{\tau_i\}\) is the set of transmission eigenvalues obtained from this spectrum.

    In this section, I followed the pincode along a complete opening trace rather than analyzing isolated contact states. The fitted spectra test whether the coherent MAR model captures the measured spectral evolution, while the tunnel decay provides a relative displacement calibration. I then use the extracted mesoscopic pincode \(\{\tau_i\}\) to distinguish continuous evolution within plateaus from abrupt reorganizations between them. Comparison with orbital-based models for aluminum suggests plausible changes of the contact symmetry and atomic geometry, but it does not determine a unique microscopic structure.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Pincode Extraction and Fit Quality}
    %=========================================================

    I recorded superconducting \textit{I--V} characteristics while opening a highly transmitting contact in small motor steps. I represented the measured current as a sum of independent MAR contributions and assigned a best-fit pincode to every recorded contact state. Different values of $\tau_i$ produce distinct MAR contributions, so the nonlinear subgap spectrum contains information that is absent from $G_\mathrm{N}$ alone. The physical basis of this extraction is introduced in Secs.~\ref{subsec:quasi:meso} and \ref{subsec:meso:mar}, and the numerical fitting procedure is described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis}. The model assumes a short coherent contact with energy-independent normal-state transmissions. It therefore extracts the effective transmission eigenvalues that reproduce the measured dc MAR spectrum. The fit does not determine the underlying atomic orbitals or contact geometry uniquely. I infer plausible microscopic configurations later by comparing the number, magnitude, degeneracy, and evolution of the fitted channels with theoretical expectations for aluminum. Inelastic transport inside the contact and energy-dependent scattering are not included in this interpretation.

    In the final fits, I fixed the temperature and Dynes broadening to $T=0$ and $\gamma=0$. The bath temperature remained below $50\,\mathrm{mK}$, corresponding to $4.3\,\upmu\mathrm{eV}$. This energy is small compared with both the superconducting gap and the observed spectral width, which makes the zero-temperature approximation appropriate for the present extraction. Setting $\gamma=0$ is a minimal-model assumption rather than evidence for an infinite quasiparticle lifetime. A finite electronic temperature, lifetime broadening, and voltage noise all smooth the MAR structure and cannot be separated reliably from this data set. I therefore did not fit these effects simultaneously. Instead, I convolved the calculated voltage dependence with a Gaussian of width $\sigma_V$, which represents the experimental voltage resolution together with unresolved thermal and lifetime broadening. I allowed the zero-temperature gap $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$ to vary independently for every spectrum in the same fitting step as the channel transmissions. Although the pincode changed substantially during opening, both nuisance parameters remained concentrated around
    \begin{align}
        \Delta_0 = 188.5(5)\,\upmu\mathrm{eV}\,, &&
        \sigma_V = 25(1)\,\upmu\mathrm{V}\,.
        \label{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}
    \end{align}
    Here, the central values are the means across the trace and the uncertainties are the corresponding standard deviations, not the statistical uncertainties of individual fits. The medians give the same central values within rounding. The clustering is physically reasonable because $\Delta_0$ is set by the aluminum electrodes and $\sigma_V$ mainly by the measurement chain, whereas the changing atomic configuration primarily affects $\{\tau_i\}$. No systematic drift of $\Delta_0$ or $\sigma_V$ was apparent along the trace.

    I minimized the current-space residual with point weights $w_k\propto1/|V_k|$. This weighting reduces the influence of the larger absolute currents at high bias and retains sensitivity to the subgap MAR structure. The zero-voltage point carried zero weight, and the central interval containing the switching branch was excluded because it is not described by the dc MAR model.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits} shows six representative contact states. For each state, the best-fit pincode reproduces the measured \textit{I--V} characteristic and its corresponding differential conductance. This comparison covers states with different conductances and different numbers of fitted channels and tests the MAR line shape over the full bias range rather than only the total normal-state conductance. The derivative is calculated from the same measured \textit{I--V} data and is therefore a complementary representation rather than an independent fit observable. The reported transmissions are best-fit values on a grid with spacing $0.001$. This spacing is the numerical sampling resolution and not a confidence interval. Nearly equal channels cannot be distinguished by their identity, and sufficiently weak channels can fall below the sensitivity of the spectrum. The fitted channel count should consequently be understood as the number of contributions resolved within this model and data quality, not as an exact count of atomic orbitals.

    The discrete optimization used random restarts to reduce its sensitivity to the initial pincode, but it did not guarantee the global minimum. Independent restarts converged to the same dominant transmissions. Because the fit also permitted $\tau_i=0$, solutions with a larger maximum channel count included solutions with fewer contributing channels and could contain nearly degenerate weak contributions. A full statistical uncertainty analysis is outside the scope of this section. Claims of channel degeneracy, splitting, and continuity below are therefore qualitative features of the fitted eigenvalue spectrum. They should not be read as confidence-qualified identification or tracking of individual orbital channels.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Displacement Calibration}
    %=========================================================

    The recorded motor position did not provide a direct displacement calibration. I therefore determined the scale from the conductance decay after the contact entered the tunnel regime. For a rectangular vacuum barrier, the normal-state conductance depends exponentially on the electrode separation $x$,
    \begin{equation}
        G_\mathrm{N}(x) \propto \exp(-2\kappa x)\,, \qquad
        \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m_\mathrm{e}\Phi}}{\hbar}\approx 1.05\,\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}\,.
        \label{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}
    \end{equation}
    Here, $m_\mathrm{e}$ is the electron mass and $\Phi$ is the effective barrier height. Because the aluminum electrodes were separated by breaking the constriction in situ, I assumed a clean aluminum surface and used $\Phi=4.2\,\mathrm{eV}$ \cite{grepstad_anisotropic_1976}.

        \caption{
            Tunnel-decay fit used to calibrate displacement.
        }
        \label{fig:ac:pincode:tunneling}
    \end{wrapfigure}
    I fitted $\ln\left(G_\mathrm{N}/G_0\right)$ as a linear function of the recorded motor coordinate in the tunnel regime, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:tunneling}. The slope of this fit, together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}, converts the motor coordinate into a relative electrode displacement and gives approximately $2\,\mathrm{pm}$ per acquisition step. I defined $x=0$ at the beginning of the tunnel regime. Positions recorded while the electrodes were still in contact consequently have $x<0$. This approximate calibration is used only as a relative displacement scale for visualization. It is not an independent measurement of the absolute electrode separation because it depends on the assumed barrier height and the rectangular-barrier approximation.

    With this relative displacement scale established, Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal} and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim} compare the complete measured and reconstructed transport landscapes. Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal} displays the data supplied to the fitting workflow, while Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim} is calculated from the extracted pincodes and the mean values in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:parameter}, because both parameters describe position-independent properties of the electrodes and measurement chain. The agreement between the landscapes provides a global consistency check complementary to the individual spectra in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits}. Because the reconstruction uses parameters fitted to the same data, it is not an independent validation. It nevertheless shows that the coherent MAR model follows the principal displacement-dependent structures and their abrupt or continuous evolution. The \textit{dI--dV} and \textit{dV--dI} representations emphasize deviations that are less visible in the current itself, although they are derived from the same measured \textit{I--V} data and are therefore not independent fit observables.


    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Channel Evolution and Contact Geometry}
    %=========================================================

    Having established that the fitted pincodes reproduce the measured transport landscape, I now use their displacement dependence to interpret how the contact evolves. The minimal microscopic reference is the tight-binding model of an aluminum one-atom contact developed by Cuevas, Levy Yeyati, and Mart{\'i}n-Rodero \cite{cuevas_microscopic_1998}. It predicts one highly transmitting channel with mixed axial $sp_z$ character and two weaker channels derived mainly from the transverse $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals. The transverse pair is degenerate in a rotationally symmetric contact and splits when the symmetry is broken \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. Under elongation, the transverse transmissions decrease toward zero while the axial transmission approaches unity \cite{cuevas_evolution_1998}. These signatures provide the reference for interpreting the number, degeneracy, and displacement dependence of the extracted channels, but they do not identify a unique atomic geometry.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode} condenses the complete extraction into the normal-state conductance and the individual channel transmissions. The six regions (a)--(f) mark successive metastable contact configurations separated by abrupt rearrangements. Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:config} shows the schematic geometries used to discuss their possible microscopic origin.

    Configurations (a) and (b) contain up to seven contributing channels with substantial transmission. They are therefore consistent with a few-atom constriction, sketched in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(a), rather than a one-atom-wide aluminum contact. The jump between them reorganizes the complete pincode instead of uniformly reducing all transmissions. The channel count constrains the complexity of the constriction, but not its precise atomic arrangement.

    Configuration (c) is fitted with three resolved contributions and is the clearest candidate for a highly symmetric one-atom contact. Its dominant transmission and approximately degenerate pair of weaker transmissions agree qualitatively with the axial $sp_z$ and transverse $p_x,p_y$ contributions expected for aluminum \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. The fitted pair separates as the plateau evolves, which is consistent with a gradual loss of rotational symmetry. Both the monomer and the axial dimer geometries in Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(b) and \ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(c) are compatible with the initial state. The later separation therefore suggests, but does not establish, that a dimer or one-atom contact becomes distorted or displaced from the contact axis.

    Configurations (d) and (e) retain two principal channels even after the conductance falls below $G_0$. Within both plateaus, the dominant channel strengthens while the weaker contribution is suppressed. The upward slope of the total conductance is therefore caused by the net result of this redistribution rather than by all transmissions changing in the same direction. This behavior is consistent with the off-center geometry in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(d), where reduced symmetry suppresses one transverse contribution.

    The transition from (d) to (e) is unusually selective within the fitted eigenvalue spectrum. The dominant transmission changes abruptly, while the weaker transmission remains almost continuous and the third stays nearly closed. This pattern is consistent with a rearrangement that mainly modifies the axial transport path while retaining a weak transverse contribution of similar magnitude. Al $3p_z$-related states are sensitive to local coordination, and first-principles calculations show a strong geometry dependence of aluminum channel transmissions \cite{schwingenschlogl_geometry_2007,senkpiel_extracting_2022}. Nevertheless, the fit determines eigenvalues rather than eigenvectors, so it cannot establish that the weaker contribution represents the same orbital channel across the jump. I therefore treat a predominantly axial rearrangement as a microscopic hypothesis rather than an unambiguous tracking of one channel.

    Overall, the discontinuities between configurations are consistent with atomic rearrangements, while the continuous evolution within each plateau is consistent with elastic deformation of a metastable structure \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. In the final configuration (f), a single weak channel enters the tunnel geometry shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:config}(e) and its conductance subsequently decays exponentially according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pincode:tunnel-decay}. The proposed sequence from a few-atom constriction through a symmetric monomer or dimer and an off-center contact to tunneling captures the channel evolution, but it is not a unique reconstruction of the atomic geometry.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Distribution of the Extracted Transmissions}
    %=========================================================

    The tight-binding results for randomly distorted one-atom aluminum contacts provide a reference for the transmission histogram. They place the dominant channel approximately within $0.6\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.9$ and the two weaker channels approximately within $0.1\lesssim\tau\lesssim0.3$ \cite{cuevas_microscopic_1998}. The separate deformation calculation does not provide a statistical distribution. It instead shows that the transverse transmissions can be of order $0.3$ near a compact geometry and move below $0.2$ as the contact is elongated, while the axial channel moves toward unity \cite{cuevas_evolution_1998}. The expected position of the weak-channel population therefore depends on the geometries sampled along the trace.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:histogram} collects all seven allowed channel slots from every fitted spectrum. Every spectrum contributes equally, and inactive slots with $\tau_i=0$ are retained. The large peak at zero therefore primarily records the variable effective channel count and should not be interpreted as a physical transmission population. Away from zero, two broad populations are visually suggested. The low-transmission population has a shoulder near $\tau\approx0.3$, while the smaller high-transmission population extends from approximately $0.6$ to $0.9$. This structure is qualitatively consistent with the weak transverse channels and the dominant $sp_z$-derived channel predicted by the tight-binding model. In particular, weight near $0.3$ is plausible for compact or weakly elongated one-atom configurations in the deformation calculation. The tail toward lower transmission is also compatible with the suppression of transverse channels during stretching. However, the histogram combines correlated consecutive spectra and unequal numbers of spectra from distinct configurations. The relative weights therefore depend on the physical trajectory and its sampling. I treat the histogram as a descriptive comparison with the orbital model, not as a statistical channel distribution or quantitative evidence for bimodality.

    In summary, the coherent MAR model reproduces the measured spectral evolution along a complete opening trace with common values of $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$. This agreement is the robust result of the extraction. The extracted mesoscopic pincode $\{\tau_i\}$ evolves continuously on conductance plateaus and reorganizes at abrupt jumps, which is consistent with elastic deformation between atomic rearrangements. The number, approximate degeneracy, and separation of the fitted transmissions further suggest a progression from a few-atom constriction through a nearly symmetric three-channel monomer or dimer to an off-center contact and finally the tunnel regime. The orbital assignments and the predominantly axial interpretation of the transition from (d) to (e) remain microscopic hypotheses because the fit does not determine channel eigenvectors or a unique atomic geometry. The histogram provides an additional descriptive comparison with the channel populations expected for aluminum, but not an independent statistical test. Channel-resolved MAR spectroscopy therefore reveals changes of the mesoscopic pincode that remain hidden in the normal-state conductance alone and constrain, without uniquely reconstructing, the evolution of the contact.
