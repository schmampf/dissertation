    %=========================================================
    \subsection{The Mesoscopic Pincode}
    \label{sec:ac:pincode}
    %=========================================================

    How does a metallic contact form as its narrowest constriction is reduced to only a few atoms? At this scale, the normal-state conductance no longer provides a direct measure of a continuous contact area. It constrains only the sum $G_\mathrm{N}/G_0=\sum_i\tau_i$ and therefore does not reveal how the current is distributed among the individual transmission channels. The channel-resolved measurement introduced by Scheer et al. provides a way to access this additional information through the nonlinear superconducting transport of an atomic contact \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. In this section, I reproduced this measurement to follow how the transmission channels evolved while an aluminum contact was opened. The extracted set of transmission eigenvalues \(\{\tau_i\}\) is referred to as the mesoscopic pincode.

    After breaking the mechanically controlled break junction, I recorded superconducting \textit{I--V} characteristics while opening the contact in small displacement steps. I represented the measured current as a sum of independent MAR contributions and assigned a best-fit pincode to each stable contact state. Different values of $\tau_i$ produce distinct MAR contributions, so the nonlinear subgap spectrum contains information that is absent from $G_\mathrm{N}$ alone. The physical basis of this extraction is introduced in Secs.~\ref{subsec:quasi:meso} and \ref{subsec:meso:mar}, and the numerical fitting procedure is described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:methods:digital:analysis}. In the final fits, I fixed the temperature and Dynes broadening to $T=0$ and $\gamma=0$. I allowed the zero-temperature gap $\Delta_0$ and the effective voltage broadening $\sigma_V$ to vary independently for each spectrum together with the channel transmissions. The fitted values of $\Delta_0$ and $\sigma_V$ formed narrow clusters around
    \begin{align}
        \Delta_0 = 188.5(5)\,\mathrm{ueV}\,, &&
        \sigma_V = 26(1)\,\mathrm{uV}\,.
        \label{fig:ac:pincode:parameter}
    \end{align}

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode:fits} validates the extraction for six representative contact states. The displacement $\Delta x$ is given in arbitrary motor units. It is monotonic and assumed to be proportional to the relative electrode displacement, but it is not calibrated as an absolute distance. The same best-fit pincode reproduces both the measured \textit{I--V} characteristic and its differential conductance. The agreement extends across contact states with different conductance and different numbers of resolved channels. It therefore tests the channel-dependent subgap structure rather than only the total normal-state conductance.

    Figure \ref{fig:ac:pincode} extends the extraction from these examples to the complete opening trace. Configurations (a) and (b) require more than three channels to reproduce the MAR spectrum. They are therefore incompatible with the simple three-channel picture of a one-atom aluminum contact and indicate a wider, few-atom constriction. The channel count alone does not determine the exact number of atoms. Configurations (c)--(e) contain no more than three dominant channels and are consistent with a one-atom contact. Configuration (c) provides the clearest example of this picture: one dominant channel and two weaker channels of comparable transmission are consistent with an axial channel of mixed $sp_z$ character and an approximately degenerate pair of transverse channels derived mainly from the $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals. At the transition to configuration (d), the conductance falls below $G_0$, but two principal channels remain. Configuration (e) is likewise dominated by two channels. The final transition into configuration (f) marks the tunnel regime, where the fitted transmissions fall below the resolution of the extraction.

    The channel decomposition reveals changes that are hidden in $G_\mathrm{N}$ alone. Within configurations (d) and (e), the conductance rises gradually toward the end of each plateau. This increase is carried mainly by the most transmissive channel, while the second channel becomes less transmissive. The pincode therefore changes through a redistribution between channels rather than through a uniform increase of all transmissions. At the boundaries between configurations, several channels instead change discontinuously. These two forms of evolution reproduce the principal phenomenology reported in the original experiment, including the simultaneous evolution of several channels along tilted plateaus and the presence of more than one channel below $G_0$ \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}.

    Following Scheer et al., I associate the discontinuities with atomic rearrangements and the continuous evolution within a configuration with elastic deformation of the contact \cite{scheer_conduction_1997}. This interpretation is consistent with the transport data, but the measurement does not determine the atomic geometry directly. Later work connected the channel structure of one-atom contacts to chemical valence. For aluminum, a dominant channel with mixed $sp_z$ character and two transverse channels derived mainly from the $p_x$ and $p_y$ orbitals provide a microscopic explanation for the occurrence of three channels \cite{scheer_signature_1998}. I use this result as context for the observed channel count, but I do not assign an orbital character to an individual fitted channel.

    The complete measured transport landscape is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:cal}, and its reconstruction from the extracted pincodes is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:pincode:sim}.

    In summary, I established an experimental and numerical workflow for assigning a mesoscopic pincode to each stable measured contact state. The representative fits validate the channel decomposition, and the reconstructed transport landscape shows that the extracted pincodes capture the systematic evolution of the MAR spectrum throughout the opening trace. The reported transmissions are best-fit values on a grid with spacing $0.001$. This spacing defines the numerical resolution and is not a statistical confidence interval. A full uncertainty analysis and a microscopic reconstruction of the atomic geometry are outside the scope of this section.
