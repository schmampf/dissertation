\subsection{Photon Assisted Transport}
\label{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}
%=========================================================

    I first consider contact 1, whose static transport and multichannel MAR fit are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:mar-fit}. In addition to the finite-bias MAR structure, the measurement contains a narrow zero-bias peak associated with a supercurrent contribution. This contribution is not included in the dc MAR model used to determine the transmission eigenvalues. I therefore excluded the data near zero bias from the fit and obtaine
    \begin{align}
        \{\tau_i\} = \{0.517, 0.399, 0.332, 0.305, 0.204\}\,, &&
        G_\mathrm{N} = 1.757\,G_0\,.
        \label{eq:ac:contact1:pincode}
    \end{align}

Data near zero bias are excluded because the additional narrow peak is not contained in the dc MAR model.
    }{fig:ac:contact1:mar-fit}

    The largest transmission of this contact is only $\tau_1=0.517$. Contact 1 therefore provides a comparatively low-transmission starting point for separating the photon-assisted quasiparticle, MAR, and supercurrent contributions below. I used the same static MAR fitting procedure for all irradiated contacts. Their extracted pincodes are collected in Table~\ref{table:ac:pincodes} in Appendix~\ref{appendix:coupling-and-pincode}, demonstrating that the undriven transport of the complete contact set can be described within the same multichannel MAR framework. At the end of this section, I test the photon-assisted model on contact 2. Its nearly ballistic leading channel, $\tau_1=0.996$, provides a deliberately contrasting validation case rather than a repetition of the contact 1 regime.

    \subsubsection*{Incoherent Cooper-Pair Transport at Zero Bias}

        I now return to the narrow zero-bias feature that was excluded from the static MAR fit. Its location identifies it with a Cooper-pair contribution, but the measurement does not show a stable zero-voltage supercurrent branch. This suggests that fluctuations of the phase and the electromagnetic environment suppress coherent Josephson transport. I therefore test whether the feature is consistent with incoherent Cooper-pair tunneling (ICPT), for which individual charge-$2e$ transfer events exchange energy with the environment.

        In the perturbative ICPT regime, the current samples the environmental probability density $P_{2e}(E)$ at $E=2eV$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current}. I use the zero-temperature approximation, for which the environment does not emit energy into the junction. Equivalently, a tunneling Cooper pair can release energy into the environment, while the reverse process is absent.

        I first invert the measured current to visualize the environmental response before choosing a circuit model. Under the zero-temperature approximation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current} gives an effective $P_{2e}(E)$ directly from the measured $I(V)$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:supercurrent}(b). Equations~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-definition} and \eqref{eq:stochastic:phase-correlation} then yield the corresponding effective dissipative impedance in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:supercurrent}(c). This inversion is a diagnostic construction. It shows that the inferred frequency dependence can be represented by a minimal parallel-$RC$ environment and therefore motivates the impedance ansatz
        \begin{equation}
            Z(\omega;R_\mathrm{eff},C_\mathrm{eff}) = \frac{R_\mathrm{eff}}{1+ \ima\omega R_\mathrm{eff}C_\mathrm{eff}}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:impedance}
        \end{equation}
        After selecting this circuit form, I reverse the calculation for the quantitative fit. For each trial, I calculate $P_{2e}(E)$ from the assumed impedance and substitute it into Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current}. This gives the forward model, which I fit directly to the measured zero-bias \textit{I--V} curve. Thus, the inversion motivates the parallel-$RC$ model, while the fitted parameters are obtained from the forward calculation.

(c) Effective dissipative impedance reconstructed from $P_{2e}(E)$, together with the parallel-$RC$ form used in the subsequent forward fit.         This forward fit gives
        \begin{align}
            I_\mathrm{c} = 7.76(14)\,\mathrm{nA}\,, &&
            R_\mathrm{eff} = 3.32(5)\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\,, &&
            C_\mathrm{eff} = 2.02(10)\,\mathrm{fF}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact1:icpt-parameter}
        \end{align}

        The static characteristic is therefore consistent with ICPT in an effective dissipative environment, but it does not by itself exclude a broadened coherent Josephson response. The photon-assisted replicas provide an independent test below because incoherent transfer produces squared-Bessel weights, whereas coherent Shapiro response has a different amplitude dependence. The parameters extracted here define the undriven ICPT contribution used in that comparison. The remaining calibration step is to relate the nominal microwave output to the ac voltage at the junction.

    \subsubsection*{Coupling Parameter}

        In contrast to the tunnel-junction calibration, the irradiated atomic contact curves cannot be fitted with a single complete PAT model because quasiparticle transport, MAR, and the zero-bias Cooper pair contribution coexist. I therefore use a self-referenced PAT approximation to estimate the coupling and assume the familiar linear relation $A=\eta A_\mathrm{out}$.

        The measured unirradiated conductance is then used as the undriven characteristic in the Tien--Gordon transformation from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-iv}. This construction is self-contained because it does not require a separate microscopic fit of the static or irradiated curves. It preserves the measured line shape and asks which value of $\eta$ best reproduces its photon-assisted replicas over the complete amplitude sweep. I perform the comparison in \textit{dI--dV}, which suppresses the approximately voltage-independent excess current and emphasizes the replicated spectral features. The map is dominated by quasiparticle PAT, so I use the transferred charge $q=e$. A trial coupling $\eta$ therefore gives
        \begin{align}
            G_\mathrm{test}\!\left(\eta, A_\mathrm{out}, V\right) 
            &= \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
                        J_n^2\!\left(\frac{q\eta A_\mathrm{out}}{h\nu}\right)
                        G_\mathrm{exp}\!\left(V - \frac{n h\nu}{q}, A=0\right)\,,
            \label{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:coupling:dG}\\
            \chi^2\!(\eta) &= \left\langle\left(G_\mathrm{test}\!(\eta, A_\mathrm{out}, V)-G_\mathrm{exp}\!(A_\mathrm{out}, V)\right)^2\right\rangle_{(A_\mathrm{out}, V)}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:coupling:chi}
        \end{align}
        The objective averages the squared deviation over the complete amplitude and voltage map. Narrow MAR and zero-bias features are not expected to follow the charge-$e$ transformation exactly, but they occupy a smaller part of the map than the quasiparticle replicas and therefore do not set the fitted coupling.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{1.4in}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1em}
            \import{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline}{eta_fit.pgf}
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \caption{
                Coupling calibration for contact 1 at 15\,GHz.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:contact1:eta}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        I minimize Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:coupling:chi} with a gradient-based optimizer. This continuous optimization is more efficient than a dense grid search because each objective evaluation transforms the complete measurement map and the minimum is narrow. The independently sampled objective landscape in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:eta} is not the fit procedure itself. It visualizes the wider parameter range and confirms that the optimizer converges to the clear global minimum. For contact 1, this gives
        \begin{align}
            \nu = 15.0\,\mathrm{GHz}\, &&
            \eta = 0.006179(16)\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact1:eta}
        \end{align}

        This calibration replaces the hardware-dependent source setting by the effective amplitude at the contact. The coupling parameters obtained for all irradiated contacts are summarized in Table~\ref{table:ac:coupling-parameter} in Appendix~\ref{appendix:ac:coupling}.

    \subsubsection*{Photon-Assisted Multiple Andreev Reflection}


        In order to understand this, we need the unified tien gordon equation (Eq.~\ref{eq:meso:pamar-current}).

        The first obvious test is, if the charge resolved currents from Full counting statistics could provide us with those. As one can easily see, this doesn't work out very well unfortunately. as shown in figure \ref{fig:ac:contact1:sim-img}(c).

        if we look at the charge resolved pamar contributions in figure \ref{fig:ac:contact1:pamarm}(a). It becomes obvious, that not just the dominant positive conductance gets replicated, but also the nearby negative conductance.

        From here on we need to think about what $I_m$ might actually be. We should think about it such, that it is the transport dominating that threshold, given by the MAR thresholds \eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}.

        i define weights such:
        \begin{align}
            w_m(V)=\exp\!\left(-\left(\frac{2\Delta_0/|eV|-m}{2}\right)^2\right)\,,&&
            \sum_{m=0}^\infty w_m(V)\overset{!}{=}1\,.
        \end{align}

        Notice, that the weights are gaussian distributions on the inverse voltage scale. $w_\infty$ denotes the region of higher orders and might remain unused. $w_0$ is artificially created here and is merged into $w_1$, since quasiparticle transport is dominant at $eV>2\Delta$.

        With these weights i can define:
        \begin{align}
            I_m\!(V) = \int w_m(V)\, \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}\!(V)\, \mathrm{d}V\,, &&
            I_m\!(0) \overset{!}{=} 0 \, .
        \end{align}
        as shown in \ref{fig:ac:pamar-weights}.

        \begin{figure}[t]
            \centering
            \subfigure[
                Voltage representation
            ]{\import{atomic_contact/pamar-weights}{V.pgf}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[
                Inverse-voltage representation
            ]{\import{atomic_contact/pamar-weights}{1_V.pgf}}
            \caption{
                Phenomenological weights used to decompose the static \textit{dI--dV} by MAR threshold. (a) On the voltage axis, each $w_m(V)$ selects the vicinity of $e|V|=2\Delta_0/m$, while $w_1$ contains the quasiparticle region above $2\Delta_0$ and $w_\infty$ collects unresolved higher orders near zero bias. (b) On the inverse-voltage axis, the finite-order weights form equally spaced Gaussians centered at integer $m$. The weights are normalized to unity at every voltage before constructing the effective currents $I_m(V)$.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:pamar-weights}
        \end{figure}

        why use differential conductance you might ask and not the current?
        \begin{align}
            \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}V}(w_m \cdot I) = \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}\cdot w_m + I \cdot \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}V}w_m
        \end{align}
        Here the derivative of the weight adds up another term.

        With the unified tien gordon equation we can simulate the map as shown in \ref{fig:ac:contact1:sim-img}(e).

        The pamar contributions by threshold are shown in figure \ref{fig:ac:contact1:pamarm}(b).

    \subsubsection*{Photon Assisted Incoherent Cooper Pair Tunneling}

        Now we can take a closer look at the replicas caused by the zero bias peak. A direct test if the zero bias peak is caused by phase coherent shapiro peaks or incoherent cooper pair tunneling, is to check the amplitude of the replicas.

        one would expect a simple bessel like behavior for shapiro steps \ref{eq:macro:shapiro-iv} and a bessel square behaviour, as in \ref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current}.
