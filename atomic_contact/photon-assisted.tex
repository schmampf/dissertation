%=========================================================
\subsection{Photon Assisted Transport}
\label{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}
%=========================================================

    I first consider atomic configuration C1, whose static transport and multichannel MAR fit are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:mar-fit}. In addition to the finite-bias MAR structure, the measurement contains a narrow zero-bias peak associated with a supercurrent contribution. This contribution is not included in the dc MAR model used to determine the transmission eigenvalues. I therefore excluded the data near zero bias from the fit and obtained
    \begin{align}
        \{\tau_i\} = \{0.517, 0.399, 0.332, 0.305, 0.204\}\,, &&
        G_\mathrm{N} = 1.757\,G_0\,.
        \label{eq:ac:contact1:pincode}
    \end{align}

    \singlefitfigure{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/mar_fit}{
        Static transport of C1 and multichannel MAR fit. The fit reproduces the finite-bias \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} characteristics for the transmission eigenvalues in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:contact1:pincode}. Data near zero bias are excluded because the additional narrow peak is not contained in the dc MAR model.
    }{fig:ac:contact1:mar-fit}

    The largest transmission of this contact is only $\tau_1=0.517$. C1 therefore provides a comparatively low-transmission starting point for separating the photon assisted quasiparticle, MAR, and supercurrent contributions below. I used the same static MAR fitting procedure for all irradiated contacts. Their extracted pincodes are collected in Table~\ref{table:ac:pincodes} in Appendix~\ref{appendix:ac:photon-assisted-transport}, demonstrating that the undriven transport of the full contact set can be described within the same multichannel MAR framework. At the end of this section, I test the photon assisted model on C2. Its nearly ballistic leading channel, $\tau_1=0.996$, provides a deliberately contrasting validation case rather than a repetition of the C1 regime.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Incoherent Cooper-Pair Transport (ICPT)}
    %=========================================================

        I now return to the narrow zero-bias feature that was excluded from the static MAR fit. Its location identifies it with a Cooper pair contribution, but the measurement does not show a stable zero-voltage supercurrent branch. This suggests that phase fluctuations induced by the electromagnetic environment suppress coherent Josephson transport. I therefore test whether the feature is consistent with ICPT, for which individual charge-$2e$ transfer events exchange energy with the environment.

        In the perturbative ICPT regime, the current samples the environmental probability density $P_{2e}(E)$ at $E=2eV$ according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current}. I use the zero-temperature approximation, in which the environment can absorb energy released by a tunneling Cooper pair but cannot supply energy to it.

        \begin{figure}
            \centering
            \subfigure[Zero-bias \textit{I--V} and ICPT fit.]{\thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/sc_fit}{iv}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[Extracted $P_{2e}(E)$.]{\thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/sc_fit}{pe}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[Effective dissipative $Z(\omega)$.]{\thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/sc_fit}{zv}}
            \caption{Effective environment analysis and forward model fit of the zero-bias feature of C1. (a) Measured \textit{I--V} characteristic and fitted ICPT current. Data below $V=0$ and in the quasiparticle branch are excluded. (b) Effective zero-temperature $P_{2e}(E)$ obtained from the measured current. (c) Effective dissipative impedance reconstructed from $P_{2e}(E)$, together with the parallel-$RC$ form used in the subsequent forward fit. The resulting fit parameters are given in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:contact1:icpt-parameter}.}
            \label{fig:ac:contact1:supercurrent}
        \end{figure}

        I first invert the measured current to visualize the environmental response before choosing a circuit model. Under the zero-temperature approximation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current} gives an effective $P_{2e}(E)$ directly from the measured $I(V)$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:supercurrent}(b). Equations~\eqref{eq:stochastic:pe-definition} and \eqref{eq:stochastic:phase-correlation} then yield the corresponding effective dissipative impedance in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:supercurrent}(c). This inversion is a diagnostic construction. The inferred frequency dependence motivates the simplest impedance ansatz that includes both low-frequency dissipation and a high-frequency cutoff. I represent the linear environment seen by the junction by an effective resistance $R_\mathrm{eff}$ in parallel with an effective capacitance $C_\mathrm{eff}$. The resistance sets the low-frequency impedance, while the capacitance progressively shunts the impedance as the frequency increases. The resulting impedance is
        \begin{equation}
            Z(\omega,R_\mathrm{eff},C_\mathrm{eff}) = \frac{R_\mathrm{eff}}{1+ \ima\omega R_\mathrm{eff}C_\mathrm{eff}}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:impedance}
        \end{equation}
        After selecting this circuit form, I reverse the calculation for the quantitative fit. For each trial, I calculate $P_{2e}(E)$ from the assumed impedance and substitute it into Eq.~\eqref{eq:stochastic:icpt:current}. This gives the forward model, which I fit directly to the measured zero-bias \textit{I--V} curve. Thus, the inversion motivates the parallel-$RC$ model, while the fitted parameters are obtained from the forward calculation.

        This forward fit gives
        \begin{align}
            I_\mathrm{c} = \qty{7.76(14)}{\nano\ampere}\,, &&
            R_\mathrm{eff} = \qty{3.32(5)}{\kilo\ohm}\,, &&
            C_\mathrm{eff} = \qty{2.02(10)}{\femto\farad}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact1:icpt-parameter}
        \end{align}

        The static characteristic is therefore consistent with ICPT in an effective dissipative environment, but it does not by itself exclude a broadened coherent Josephson response. The photon assisted replicas provide an independent test below because incoherent transfer produces squared-Bessel weights, whereas coherent Shapiro response has a different amplitude dependence. The parameters extracted here define the undriven ICPT contribution used in that comparison. The remaining calibration step is to relate the nominal microwave output to the ac voltage across the junction.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Coupling Parameter}
    %=========================================================

        In contrast to the tunnel-junction calibration, the irradiated atomic contact curves cannot be fitted with a single complete PAT model, as in Sec.~\ref{subsec:tb:highres}, because quasiparticle transport, MAR, and the zero-bias Cooper pair contribution coexist. I therefore use a self-referenced PAT approximation to estimate the amplitude coupling.

        For all microwave amplitude sweeps on the atomic contacts, I recorded a series of \textit{I--V} curves while stepping the source power uniformly in dBm. Conversion to the nominal voltage amplitude $A_\mathrm{out}\propto\sqrt{P_\mathrm{out}}$ therefore gives logarithmically spaced rather than equally spaced amplitude values. I acquired the unirradiated curve at $A_\mathrm{out}=0$ separately. In the corresponding amplitude maps, bias and calibrated amplitude form the two coordinates, while current or differential response is encoded by color. The maps retain the measured amplitude coordinates after calibration.

        The measured unirradiated conductance is then used as the undriven characteristic in the Tien--Gordon transformation from Eq.~\eqref{eq:micro:pat-iv}. This construction is self-contained because it does not require a separate microscopic fit of the static or irradiated curves. It preserves the measured line shape and asks which value of $\eta$ best reproduces its photon assisted replicas over the complete amplitude sweep. I perform the comparison in \textit{dI--dV}, which suppresses the approximately voltage independent excess current and emphasizes the replicated spectral features. The map is dominated by quasiparticle PAT, so I use the transferred charge $q=e$. A trial coupling $\eta$ therefore gives
        \begin{align}
            \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}\left(\eta, A_\mathrm{out}, V\right)
            &= \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
                        J_n^2\!\left(\frac{q\eta A_\mathrm{out}}{h\nu}\right)\cdot
                        \tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}_\mathrm{exp}^{(A_\mathrm{out}=0)}\!\left(V - \frac{n h\nu}{q}\right)\,,
            \label{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:coupling:dG}\\
            \chi^2(\eta) &= \left\langle\!\!\left(\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(\eta, A_\mathrm{out}, V)-\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}_\mathrm{exp}(A_\mathrm{out}, V)\right)^2\right\rangle_{\!\!(A_\mathrm{out}, V)}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:coupling:chi}
        \end{align}
        I evaluate the squared deviation at each sampled amplitude and voltage point and average it over the map to obtain the objective $\chi^2(\eta)$. Narrow MAR and zero-bias features are not expected to follow the charge-$e$ transformation exactly, but they occupy a smaller part of the map than the quasiparticle replicas and therefore do not set the fitted coupling.

        \begin{wrapfigure}[13]{r}{1.4in}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            % \vspace{-1em}
            \thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline}{eta_fit}
            % \vspace{-1.5em}
            \caption{
                Coupling calibration for C1 at \qty{15}{\giga\hertz}.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:contact1:eta}
        \end{wrapfigure}
        I minimize Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:photon-assisted-transport:coupling:chi} with a gradient based optimizer. This continuous optimization is more efficient than a dense grid search because each objective evaluation transforms the complete measurement map and the minimum is narrow. The independently sampled objective landscape in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:eta} is not the fit procedure itself. It displays a wider parameter range and confirms that the optimizer converges to the clear global minimum. For C1, this gives
        \begin{align}
            \eta = 0.006179(16) && (\nu = \qty{15.0}{\giga\hertz})\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact1:eta}
        \end{align}

        This calibration replaces the hardware dependent source setting with the effective amplitude at the contact. The coupling parameters obtained for all irradiated contacts are summarized in Table~\ref{table:ac:coupling-parameter} in Appendix~\ref{appendix:ac:photon-assisted-transport}.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Photon Assisted Multiple Andreev Reflection (PAMAR)}
    %=========================================================

        The calibrated \textit{dI--dV} map contains several families of photon assisted features. Replicas emerge from the quasiparticle threshold at $e|V|=2\Delta_0$ and from the subgap MAR thresholds near $e|V|=2\Delta_0/m$. The full counting statistics (FCS) decomposition introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:mar} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:fcs-decomposition} separates the dc MAR current into components $I_m(V)$ associated with the transfer of an effective charge $q=me$. I first test whether these components can be used directly in the unified charge dependent Tien--Gordon expression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:pamar-current}.

        This is a natural microscopic starting point because each FCS component already carries an effective charge assignment. Dressing each $I_m(V)$ with the corresponding Bessel argument and voltage spacing therefore requires no additional assignment. The center panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:exp-pamar}, however, shows that this direct construction does not reproduce the measured sideband pattern.

        \begin{figure}[t]
            \centering
            \thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/didv-img}{exp-pamar}
            \caption{
                Test of the photon assisted MAR construction for C1 at $\nu=\qty{15.0}{\giga\hertz}$. The left panel shows the measured \textit{dI--dV} map on the logarithmically sampled amplitude grid described in Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}. The center panel applies charge dependent Tien--Gordon dressing directly to the dc FCS components. This approach does not reproduce the measured sideband pattern because positive and neighboring negative conductance contributions are replicated with the same effective charge. The right panel instead dresses components localized around the MAR thresholds and recovers the principal measured sideband trajectories. All calculations use the calibrated drive amplitude.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:contact1:exp-pamar}
        \end{figure}

        The difficulty is not the charge dependence of the Tien--Gordon transformation, but the choice of its input currents. The FCS contributions are charge resolved, but they are not localized exclusively around the corresponding MAR thresholds. Their differential conductance can contain both the dominant positive MAR feature and neighboring negative structure over the same voltage range, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:meso:mar}. Direct photon dressing consequently replicates both contributions with the same effective charge, producing additional features that are absent or substantially weaker in the measurement. The component maps in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:pamarm}(a) make this mixing visible.

        I therefore retain the charge dependent Tien--Gordon transformation but replace the dc FCS currents with phenomenological currents localized around the MAR thresholds in Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:mar-sgs}. Carrad \textit{et al.}~used a related charge-dependent sideband spacing to identify high-order MAR processes in individual traces \cite{carrad_photon-assisted_2022}. Here I instead build explicit threshold-localized input currents, so that the same decomposition predicts the full amplitude-resolved response. I construct these currents from the simulated static MAR current calculated with the pincode in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:contact1:pincode}. The natural coordinate for the decomposition is $2\Delta_0/|eV|$, on which the thresholds occur at integer $m$. I define normalized Gaussian weights
        \begin{align}
            w_m(V) = \frac{\tilde{w}_m(V)}{w_\mathcal{N}(V)}\,, &&
            \tilde{w}_m(V)=\exp\!\left(-\left(\frac{2\Delta_0/|eV|-m}{2}\right)^2\right)\,,
            \label{eq:ac:pamar:weights}
        \end{align}
        I merge the contribution $w_0$ into $w_1$ because quasiparticle transport dominates above $e|V|=2\Delta_0$. For practical reasons, I use a maximum order $M$ that combines order $M$ and all higher orders. Typically i would use $M=10$. The normalization factor $w_\mathcal{N}(V)$ is chosen so that the individual weights $w_m(V)$ sum to unity at every voltage. Figure~\ref{fig:ac:pamar-weights} shows that the same smooth partition appears unequally spaced on the voltage axis but equally spaced on the inverse-voltage coordinate.

        \begin{figure}[t]
            \centering
            \subfigure[
                Voltage representation
            ]{\thesisplot{atomic_contact/pamar-weights}{V}}
            \hfill
            \subfigure[
                Inverse-voltage representation
            ]{\thesisplot{atomic_contact/pamar-weights}{1_V}}
            \caption{
                Phenomenological weights used to decompose the simulated static MAR \textit{dI--dV} by threshold. (a) On the voltage axis, each $w_m(V)$ selects the vicinity of $e|V|=2\Delta_0/m$, while $w_1$ contains the quasiparticle region above $2\Delta_0$ and the terminal weight $w_M$ collects all remaining higher orders near zero bias. (b) On the inverse-voltage axis, the explicitly resolved weights form equally spaced Gaussians centered at i    nteger $m$. The weights are normalized to unity at every voltage before constructing the effective currents $I_m(V)$. The only free parameter used in these plots is $M=6$.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:pamar-weights}
        \end{figure}

        I apply the weights to the differential conductance of the simulated static MAR current and reconstruct an effective current for each threshold,
        \begin{align}
            I_m(V) = \int w_m(V)\,\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}(V)\,\mathrm{d}V\,, &&
            I_m(0)\overset{!}{=}0\,.
            \label{eq:ac:pamar:effective-current}
        \end{align}
        Partitioning \textit{dI--dV} rather than multiplying the current directly avoids an artificial boundary term. In the latter construction,
        \begin{equation}
            \tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}V}\!\left(w_m\,I\right)
            =w_m\tfrac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}V}+I\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}V}w_m\,,
            \label{eq:ac:pamar:current-weight-derivative}
        \end{equation}
        so the derivative of the weight would generate artificial conductance structure that is not present in the simulated static MAR spectrum.

        I then use the effective currents from Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:pamar:effective-current} in the unified Tien--Gordon expression. Figure~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:pamarm}(b) shows the resulting contributions by threshold. Their sum gives the right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:exp-pamar} and reproduces the principal quasiparticle and MAR sideband trajectories without the mixing produced by direct FCS dressing.

            \subfigure[
                Threshold-weighted components
                ]{\thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/didv-img}{pamarm}}
            \caption{
                Charge resolved contributions to the photon assisted MAR model of C1. Each row shows the summed response and the components assigned to effective charge orders $m=1$ to $4$. (a) Direct dressing of the dc FCS components replicates positive and neighboring negative \textit{dI--dV} contributions with the same effective charge. (b) Decomposing the simulated static MAR \textit{dI--dV} around the thresholds before applying the charge dependent dressing suppresses this mixing and isolates the sidebands associated with each threshold. All panels use the calibrated drive amplitude at $\qty{15.0}{\giga\hertz}$.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:contact1:pamarm}
        \end{figure}

        This threshold weighted PAMAR construction is a phenomenological stationary replica model. It assigns the simulated static MAR structure near each threshold to its nominal effective charge order, but it does not replace a microscopic Floquet calculation of irradiated MAR. In particular, it neglects coherent interference between driven MAR paths, irradiation induced changes of the contact occupations, and nonequilibrium redistribution at high drive. Within these limits, it provides a transparent description of the principal finite-bias replicas. A detailed voltage cut decomposition into the individual charge orders is provided in Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:voltage-cuts}. The narrow replicas originating from the zero-bias feature require the separate Cooper pair contribution considered next.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Photon Assisted Incoherent Cooper Pair Tunneling (PAICPT)}
    %=========================================================

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:exp-pamar} shows experimental features that are not associated with PAMAR. Instead, they originate from the zero-bias feature identified above as ICPT. Their spacing alone does not distinguish ICPT from coherent Josephson transport. Both mechanisms involve charge $2e$ and therefore generate features at voltages separated by $h\nu/(2e)$. The distinction lies in how their spectral weight evolves with microwave amplitude.

        For PAICPT, the microwave drive dresses the static ICPT characteristic determined above with probabilities proportional to squared Bessel functions, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{subsec:stochastic:icpt}. An ideal coherent Shapiro response follows the phase-locking relation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:macro:shapiro-iv}, for which the Bessel functions enter at the amplitude level. I calculate both responses using the calibrated ac voltage at the contact. The PAICPT calculation additionally uses the static ICPT parameters in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:contact1:icpt-parameter}. The comparison therefore tests the amplitude dependence without refitting the individual replicas.

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:didv-cuts} follows the central feature and the first three pairs of replicas along the microwave amplitude axis. The measured positive- and negative-bias branches show the same sequence of maxima and minima as the PAICPT calculation over most of the calibrated range. The ideal Shapiro response instead predicts a different redistribution between the central feature and successive replica orders. The finite PAMAR contribution provides a smooth background to these cuts but does not account for their oscillatory amplitude dependence.

        \begin{figure}[t]
            \centering
            \thesisplot{atomic_contact/1.8G0/15.0GHz_stripline/didv-cuts}{amplitude-cuts}
            \caption{
                Amplitude dependence of the features originating from zero bias in C1 at $\nu=\qty{15.0}{\giga\hertz}$. The four panels follow the central feature and the first three replica pairs, $n=0,\pm1,\pm2,\pm3$. The measured positive- and negative-bias branches are compared with the squared-Bessel PAICPT calculation and an ideal coherent Shapiro response on a PAMAR background. The observed redistribution among the replica orders is described more consistently by PAICPT.
            }
            \label{fig:ac:contact1:didv-cuts}
        \end{figure}

        Deviations remain at the largest amplitudes, where the measured line shapes broaden and the stationary PAICPT model becomes less accurate. Heating, nonequilibrium occupation, and drive dependent phase dynamics are not included in the calculation. The line cuts therefore do not establish ICPT from the microwave response alone. Together with the static $P(E)$ analysis, however, their squared-Bessel-like amplitude evolution supports the interpretation of the zero-bias feature as incoherent Cooper pair transport rather than an ideal coherent Shapiro response.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Comparison of Experiment and Simulation}
    %=========================================================

        I finally combine the threshold weighted PAMAR and PAICPT contributions. The calculation uses the static pincode, the effective ICPT environment, and the microwave coupling determined above. No additional adjustment is made for the comparison in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:comparison}.

        The agreement is exceptionally close considering the number and overlap of the observed structures. The simulation reproduces not only their positions but also the redistribution of spectral weight across the microwave amplitude range in both derivative representations. The remaining differences are concentrated at the largest drive amplitudes and in the detailed line shapes of the sharpest subgap features, consistent with the limits identified above.

        Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:model-comparison} provides the stricter model selection test. It compares all four combinations of the two MAR constructions and the two Cooper pair responses, together with their residual maps. The combination of threshold weighted PAMAR and PAICPT gives the best agreement. It reduces the structured residuals both along the MAR trajectories and around the replicas originating from zero bias. The complete measured and simulated transport landscapes are shown in Appendix Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:contact1:cal} and \ref{fig:ac:contact1:sim}. The full comparison therefore confirms that both ingredients are required to capture the dominant microwave response of C1 within a common description constrained by independently determined parameters.

    %=========================================================
    \subsection*{Validation with C2}
    %=========================================================

        I next test whether the threshold weighted PAMAR construction remains useful for a contact with a nearly ballistic leading channel. The static MAR fit gives
        \begin{align}
            \{\tau_i\} = \{0.997, 0.440, 0.184, 0.184, 0.182\}\,, &&
            G_\mathrm{N} = 1.987\,G_0\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact2:pincode}
        \end{align}
        This pincode is deliberately different from that of C1. Its leading channel produces a strong MAR contribution that dominates the measured response around zero voltage. Therefore, a separate static supercurrent contribution cannot be isolated and fitted reliably. For this validation, I use only the threshold weighted PAMAR model constructed from the simulated static MAR current and do not add a PAICPT contribution.

        \singlefitfigure{atomic_contact/2.4G0/15.0GHz_stripline/mar_fit}{
            Static transport of C2 and multichannel MAR fit. The nearly ballistic leading channel produces a stronger excess current and sharper subgap structure than in C1. The simultaneous comparison of \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} establishes the undriven reference used for the photon assisted calculation.
        }{fig:ac:contact2:mar-fit}

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:contact2:comparison} shows that PAMAR alone already reproduces the overall microwave response remarkably well. The simulation captures the dominant quasiparticle and MAR trajectories, their charge dependent spacing, and the broad redistribution of spectral weight over the calibrated amplitude range. This agreement is obtained despite the nearly ballistic leading channel and without using the ICPT analysis developed for C1.

        The PAMAR simulation provides a reference that separates the dominant MAR response from weaker experimental structure. The tilted lines parallel to the principal simulated features in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact2:comparison} arise from the numerical calculation and do not have physical significance. The measured \textit{dI--dV} map additionally contains faint vertical lines, with corresponding lines perpendicular to the principal features in \textit{dV--dI}. Their absence from the PAMAR reference identifies them as a separate contribution, which I interpret as PAICPT replicas originating from ICPT near zero bias.

        The full measured and simulated landscapes in Appendix Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:contact2:cal} and \ref{fig:ac:contact2:sim} support this interpretation. PAMAR reproduces the dominant zero-bias response, while the different redistribution of part of the low-voltage spectral weight reveals the weaker contribution as the drive amplitude increases. In the static \textit{I--V} characteristic, ICPT overlaps directly with the strong MAR response of the nearly ballistic channel, so its independent input cannot be extracted for a quantitative PAICPT simulation. The comparison nevertheless resolves the physical picture. The zero-bias response of C2 contains a dominant MAR contribution together with a weaker ICPT contribution.

        C2 therefore validates the principal PAMAR picture and reveals a weaker ICPT contribution within the MAR dominated zero-bias response. Together, C1 and C2 test opposite pincode regimes, from a moderate leading transmission to an almost ballistic channel, and establish the complementary roles of PAMAR and PAICPT. The same static MAR, microwave calibration, and photon assisted modeling procedures describe the overall response of the full atomic contact set summarized in Appendix Tables~\ref{table:ac:pincodes} and \ref{table:ac:coupling-parameter}. Within the tabulated range, PAMAR captures the dominant finite-bias structure, while PAICPT accounts for the contribution originating from zero bias when it can be separated from the MAR background.
