\subsection{Subharmonic Features and High Conductivity}
\label{subsec:ac:subharmonic}
%=========================================================

    This contact3 is a reclosed break junction with up to about 29 G0. In this high conductive regime we observe additional features than the established PAMAR and PAICPT in the previous section.

    \subsubsection*{Series Resistance Correction}

        \begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{1.6in}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \import{atomic_contact/43.0G0/15.0GHz_stripline}{rs_fit.pgf}
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \caption{
                    Linear fit to the low-voltage superconducting branch.
                }
            \label{fig:ac:contact3:rs-fit}
        \end{wrapfigure}

        The low-voltage superconducting branch exhibits a small linear slope. I assume that this slope is dominated by a residual series resistance between the contact and the effective four-point measurement terminals. Under this assumption, the linear fit shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:rs-fit} gives an effective series resistance and a corrected voltage $V'$ of
        \begin{align}
            R_\mathrm{s}=12.61(7)\,\Omega\,, && V'=V-I R_\mathrm{s}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact3:voltage-correction}
        \end{align}

        The extracted resistance likely originates from the copper-wire and silver-paste contacts to the sample. It is not expected to include a substantial contribution from the on-chip aluminum leads. Their large cross section gives them a high critical current, so they most likely remain superconducting throughout this measurement. This procedure provides an approximate series correction rather than an independent measurement of the lead resistance. In particular, any intrinsic linear voltage response of the contact in this regime would also be included in the fitted value.

    \subsubsection*{Static \textit{I--V} Description}

        The unirradiated \textit{I--V} characteristic combines a zero-voltage branch up to approximately $404\,\mathrm{nA}$, pronounced subgap structure, and several abrupt voltage changes. After correcting the series resistance and voltage offset, I compared the characteristic with short-junction multiple-Andreev-reflection (MAR) calculations. Fits using individual transmission channels, randomly generated pincodes, grouped channels, and a continuous transmission distribution were considered. The large normal-state conductance of approximately $29\,G_0$ made a unique channel-by-channel pincode inaccessible.

        Upon further investigation, the MAR thresholds dont exactly line up with the peaks in the \textit{dI--dV}, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:mar-fit}. While most of the MAR orders are slightly missaligned, the forth order is completly missing. This behaviour is not covered by any combination of MAR curves.

        \singlefitfigure{atomic_contact/43.0G0/19.3GHz_antenna/mar_fit}{
            Series-resistance-corrected static transport of contact 3. The panels show the \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} characteristics. Gray lines mark the expected MAR voltages \(V_m=2\Delta_0/(me)\). The conductance features are displaced from several of these thresholds, and no distinct feature coincides with the fourth-order threshold. The sharp, step-like characteristic therefore cannot be described convincingly by a conventional multichannel MAR model.
        }{fig:ac:contact3:mar-fit}

        I then tested whether the zero-voltage and resistive branches could be described within a common Josephson--MAR model. Adding an independent Andreev bound-state current did not improve the description, while overdamped and time-dependent RCSJ variants produced low-voltage states and plateaus that were not observed experimentally. Treating the measured switching boundary separately and using effective distributions with two or three channel groups yielded somewhat better agreement with the stable resistive branches. The fitted noninteger channel weights, however, cannot be interpreted as a literal pincode.

        Despite these improvements, none of the tested models provided a convincing description of the complete characteristic. In particular, they did not reproduce its sharp, step-like shape and instead connected the subgap structures too smoothly. Phenomenological corrections improved some transitions locally, but did not provide a physical explanation. I therefore do not attempt a more detailed interpretation of this \textit{I--V} characteristic.

    \subsubsection*{Self-consistent Phenomenological Description}

        This is why we move on to self-consistent description of the phenomena in the irradiation maps.

        Figure \ref{fig:ac:contact3:comparison} shows the experimental observed map, next to an simulation attempt. We once more observe replicas of the qp branch and we can observe replicas originating from the sub-gap structure (SGS). Those can be adequatly reproduced by the Phenomenological weighted PAMAR description from the section before (Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}). Here I use the measured static \textit{I--V}, instead of a simulated \textit{I--V}.

        When it comes to the replicas ot the supercurrent features, I observe additional features. For once, there are feature with the usual spacing associated with Cooper pair processes with
        \begin{align}
            V_{n,p} = \frac{n}{p}\,\frac{2e}{h\nu}\,.
        \end{align}
        Those can de described with the PAICPT concept. I once again took the static measured IV as input and replicated it accordingly.

        However there are additional features in between those. Would we assume Shapiro physics and we now in fact we are most likely in the strong coupling regime, we could associate them with fractional Shaprio Steps (ref Section meso:abs).
        However, if we would asume incoherent tunneling of Cooper pairs, as we did before, then we dont now anymore.

        I developed this purely phenomenological model in analogy to fss from abs.
        \begin{align}
            I\!(V,A) &=
                \sum_{p\geq 1}\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty
                J_n^2\!\left(\frac{p(2e)A}{h\nu}\right)\,
                I_p\!\left(V+\frac{nh\nu}{p(2e)}\right)\,.
        \end{align}

        with coefficients
        \begin{align}
            I_p(V) = w_p\,I_0(V)\,, && 
            \Sigma_p w_p \overset{!}{=} 1\,.
        \end{align}
        $I_0$ was recovered by isolating the zero voltage conductance peak and integrating it as done before (Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}).

        The model appear to work best if i use the weights
        \begin{align}            
            \left\{ w_p \right\} = \left\{ \tfrac{3}{5},\ \tfrac{2}{5} \right\}\,.
        \end{align}
