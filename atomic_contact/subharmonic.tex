\subsection{Subharmonic Transport at High Conductance}
\label{subsec:ac:subharmonic}
%=========================================================

    I measure C3 after reclosing the mechanically controllable break junction. The resulting contact reaches a conductance of approximately $29(1)\,G_0$, indicating transport through many contributing channels rather than a small set of individually resolvable channels. This high-conductance regime differs substantially from the few-channel contacts considered in the preceding section. Its static and irradiated transport exhibits pronounced subgap structure and additional replicas beyond the standard PAMAR and PAICPT descriptions introduced above.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Series Resistance Correction}
    %=========================================================

        \begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{1.6in}
            \captionsetup{format=plain}%
            \centering
            \vspace{-1.5em}
            \thesisplot{atomic_contact/43.0G0/15.0GHz_stripline}{rs_fit}
            \vspace{-.5em}
            \caption{
                    Linear fit to the low-voltage superconducting branch.
                }
            \label{fig:ac:contact3:rs-fit}
        \end{wrapfigure}

        The low-voltage superconducting branch exhibits a small linear slope. I assume that this slope is dominated by a residual series resistance between the contact and the effective four-point measurement terminals. Under this assumption, the linear fit shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:rs-fit} gives the effective series resistance \(R_\mathrm{s}\), which I use to define the corrected voltage $V'$ as
        \begin{align}
            R_\mathrm{s}=\qty{12.61(7)}{\ohm}\,, && V'=V-I R_\mathrm{s}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact3:voltage-correction}
        \end{align}

        The extracted resistance likely originates from the copper wire and silver paste contacts to the sample. I do not expect it to include a substantial contribution from the on-chip aluminum leads. Their large cross section gives them a high critical current, so they most likely remain superconducting throughout this measurement. This procedure provides an approximate series correction rather than an independent measurement of the lead resistance. In particular, any intrinsic linear voltage, e.g.~from phase diffusion, or environmental fluctuations, would also be included in the fitted value.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Static \textit{I--V} Description}
    %=========================================================

        The unirradiated \textit{I--V} characteristic combines a zero-voltage branch up to approximately $\qty{404}{\nano\ampere}$, pronounced subgap structure, and several abrupt voltage changes. After correcting the series resistance and voltage offset, I compare the \textit{I--V} characteristic with MAR calculations. I consider fits using individual transmission channels, randomly generated pincodes, grouped channels, and a continuous transmission distribution. The large conductance of approximately $29\,G_0$ makes the channel-by-channel pincode inaccessible.

        The expected MAR thresholds do not coincide exactly with the measured \textit{dI--dV} maxima, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:mar-fit}. Most orders are slightly displaced, and no distinct feature appears at the fourth-order threshold. The gray lines indicate the opening voltages of the MAR processes, not the positions at which their differential conductance must be maximal. Calculations for a single channel show that the amplitude, line shape, and position of each maximum depend strongly on the transmission. Only in the low-transmission limit do sharp maxima remain close to the thresholds. With increasing transmission, the maxima broaden and move to lower voltage, between neighboring nominal thresholds, while minima can remain near the subharmonic voltages~\cite{scheer_conduction-channels_2000}. Broad, split, and nonideally scaled MAR maxima have also been observed in high-transparency diffusive junctions~\cite{hoss_multiple_2000}.

        This transmission dependence provides a natural explanation for the displaced maxima in C3. It can also make an individual order difficult to resolve because contributions from many channels with different transmissions overlap. The missing fourth-order maximum therefore does not imply that fourth-order MAR is absent. It means only that this order does not produce a separate extremum in the measured \textit{dI--dV}. I nevertheless cannot reproduce the detailed combination of peak positions, amplitudes, and the unresolved fourth order with any of the tested transmission distributions. The comparison thus does not support a unique pincode, but the offset between maxima and threshold lines is not by itself evidence against conventional MAR.

        \singlefitfigure{atomic_contact/43.0G0/19.3GHz_antenna/mar_fit}{
            Series resistance corrected static transport of C3. The panels show the \textit{I--V}, \textit{dI--dV}, and \textit{dV--dI} characteristics. Gray lines mark the MAR opening voltages \(V_m=2\Delta_0/(me)\). Differential conductance maxima need not coincide with these thresholds because their positions and line shapes depend on transmission. No separate maximum is resolved for fourth-order MAR. The tested multichannel fits do not reproduce the complete sharp, step-like characteristic.
        }{fig:ac:contact3:mar-fit}

        I then test whether the zero-voltage and resistive branches can be described within a common Josephson-MAR model. I add a separate current from Andreev bound states, but it does not improve the description, while overdamped and time dependent RCSJ variants produce low-voltage states and plateaus that are not observed experimentally. I treat the measured switching boundary separately and use effective distributions with two or three channel groups, which yields somewhat better agreement with the stable resistive branches. The fitted noninteger channel weights, however, cannot be interpreted as a literal pincode.

        Despite these improvements, none of the tested models provides a convincing description of the complete \textit{I--V} characteristic. In particular, they do not reproduce its sharp, step-like shape and instead connect the subgap structures too smoothly. Phenomenological corrections improve some transitions locally but do not provide a physical explanation. I therefore do not attempt a more detailed interpretation of this characteristic.

    %=========================================================
    \subsubsection*{Phenomenological Replica Description}
    %=========================================================

        Because no microscopic model reproduces the static \textit{I--V} characteristic convincingly, I use the measured characteristic directly as input for a phenomenological description of the irradiated transport. The complete measured map is shown in Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:cal}. I describe replicas originating from the quasiparticle branch and the static subgap structure using the threshold weighted PAMAR procedure introduced in Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}. This approach preserves the measured step-like line shape instead of replacing it with a simulated MAR \textit{I--V} characteristic.

        The replicas originating at zero voltage require an additional contribution. I parameterize their positions as
        \begin{align}
            V_{n,p} = \frac{n}{p}\,\frac{h\nu}{2e}\,,
            \label{eq:ac:contact3:fractional-replicas}
        \end{align}
        where $p=1$ gives the conventional Cooper pair spacing $h\nu/(2e)$ and $p=2$ generates the trajectories halfway between these positions. This regular spacing resembles the PAMAR pattern, but the anchor points distinguish the two responses. Each PAMAR fan emerges from its corresponding finite-bias MAR threshold, whereas both families in Eq.~\eqref{eq:ac:contact3:fractional-replicas} emerge from the zero-voltage contribution. In a coherent Josephson picture, the $p=2$ family could be associated with fractional Shapiro steps produced by a nonsinusoidal current--phase relation. The corresponding single-harmonic estimate for the step width is given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:meso:shapiro-fractional-width}. I instead extend the incoherent replica construction used above. This extension is empirical. In particular, the $p=2$ term behaves mathematically like an effective $4e$ contribution, but it does not by itself establish coherent transfer of two Cooper pairs or any other microscopic mechanism.

        I isolate and integrate the static zero-voltage conductance feature as in Sec.~\ref{subsec:ac:photon-assisted-transport}, obtaining the current $I_0(V)$. I then construct the irradiated contribution as
        \begin{align}
            I\!(V,A) &=
                \sum_{p=1}^{2}\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty
                J_n^2\!\left(\frac{p(2e)A}{h\nu}\right)\,
                I_p\!\left(V+\frac{nh\nu}{p(2e)}\right)\,,
            \label{eq:ac:contact3:fractional-paicpt}
        \end{align}
        with
        \begin{align}
            I_p(V) &= w_p\,I_0(V)\,, &
            \sum_{p=1}^{2} w_p &= 1\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact3:fractional-weights}
        \end{align}
        I select the relative weights by comparing the calculated maps and amplitude cuts with the measured data. I obtain the closest qualitative agreement for
        \begin{align}
            \left\{w_1,w_2\right\}=\left\{\tfrac{3}{5},\tfrac{2}{5}\right\}\,.
            \label{eq:ac:contact3:fractional-weight-values}
        \end{align}
        I do not extract these weights through an independent microscopic fit. They should be interpreted only as empirical spectral weights of the two replica components.

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:comparison} compares the resulting calculation with the measured derivative maps. The combined construction reproduces the principal finite-bias and low-voltage trajectories in both representations. It also retains the sharp structure of the measured static \textit{I--V}, although differences remain in the detailed line shapes and spectral weights.

        Figure~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:amplitude-cuts} compares amplitude cuts at the integer positions $V_{n,p}$. Both components contribute at these voltages because $V_{n,1}=V_{2n,2}$. The cuts therefore contain the $n$th replica of the $p=1$ contribution and the $2n$th replica of the $p=2$ contribution. Their superimposed squared-Bessel amplitude dependence follows the measured redistribution more closely than the ideal Shapiro response. The intermediate trajectories in the full maps provide complementary evidence that a separate $p=2$ component is required.

        I include the complete measured and reconstructed transport maps in Appendix Figs.~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:cal} and~\ref{fig:ac:contact3:sim} in Sec.~\ref{appendix:ac:subharmonic}. They show the same comparison as heat maps, surfaces, and waterfall cuts over the full calibrated amplitude range.

        Taken together, the intermediate trajectories, the overlapping $p=1$ and $p=2$ contributions at the integer positions, and their squared-Bessel-like amplitude dependence consistently point toward a subharmonic PAICPT response. The present phenomenological construction does not provide a precise microscopic model, and it does not establish whether the $p=2$ component originates from incoherent correlated Cooper pair transfer or from another mechanism. Nevertheless, conventional PAICPT has been reported only with the usual $h\nu/(2e)$ replica spacing~\cite{roychowdhury_microwave_2015}. To the best of my knowledge, a subharmonic PAICPT response containing the additional $p=2$ structure observed here has not been reported previously. The experimental observation is therefore a new result in its own right, independent of the unresolved microscopic interpretation.
